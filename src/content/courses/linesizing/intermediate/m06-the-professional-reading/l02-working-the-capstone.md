# Working the capstone

A graded gas exercise is marked on the figure produced, and the figures most often lost are the ones that answer a neighbouring question correctly. Four habits carry it: read the conditions, answer the direction asked, check the units, and never guess.

{{panel:fc-gasline-explorer}}

## Read the conditions

A gas rate is meaningless without the conditions it was computed at. Before working anything, write down the bore, the length in miles, both pressures in psia, the gas gravity, the average flowing temperature, the average compressibility, the elevation change in feet and the transmission efficiency.

The efficiency deserves its own line. Every form carries it, it is held for the literature in this course, and every graded gas figure states the efficiency it belongs to. A rate computed at an efficiency the question did not name is a different answer to a different question.

The same applies to the form. Four forms answer the same inputs with four rates, so a figure quoted without the form that produced it is not checkable by anybody.

## Answer the direction asked

There are two questions in this tier and they are inverses. Given both pressures, what rate. Given a rate, what outlet pressure. Producing a rate when the question asked for a pressure is the commonest way to lose a graded field while doing correct arithmetic throughout.

The inverse also has a sign to get right. A drop is negative on a descent, and the honest answer at an outlet above the inlet is a negative drop rather than its absolute value.

## Check the units

| quantity | how it is written |
| --- | --- |
| gas rate | scfd, at a base of 520.000000 degR and 14.650000 psia |
| gas line length | miles |
| elevation change | feet, on the same call |
| pressures | psia, absolute throughout |
| temperature | degR, absolute |
| the driving group | psia squared |
| gas rates and Reynolds numbers | four decimals |
| pressures, ratios and inches | six decimals |
| friction factors and the elevation group factors | ten decimals |

A length in feet handed to a gas form is not a long line, and a gauge pressure squared is wrong by an amount no later step recovers.

## Never guess

When the engine refuses, the refusal is the answer. An outlet meeting its inlet, a rate the line cannot carry to atmosphere, a climb the static column alone pays for: each of those returns an error string rather than a number, and each names a state the method has no answer for. Writing a plausible figure into that gap turns a correct reading into a wrong one, and the gap was the finding.

Quote figures at the precision the engine prints rather than rounding on the way. A gas rate is printed to four decimals and a friction factor to TEN, and a friction factor rounded to six is already outside a field graded to a billionth.

## One habit behind the other three

All four reduce to the same discipline. A figure is worth only as much as the statement of what produced it. The conditions say which case it belongs to, the direction says which question it answers, the units say what it means, and a refusal reported honestly says that no figure exists. A capstone is marked on figures, and each one is a claim about a specific line under specific conditions.
