# Two things a hill does

A hill scales the outlet pressure inside the driving group and it changes the length the friction acts over. Those are two different things, they are driven by the same s, and neither one is the mirror of itself uphill and down.

| panhandleB | 88369202.2673 | 82321752.3402 | 94087628.1888 | 0.931566 | 1.064711 |
| general | 73861363.0502 | 68827677.8048 | 78615728.3463 | 0.931850 | 1.064369 |

All four lose rate climbing and gain it descending, and they do not lose and gain by the same amounts. Panhandle A is the most sensitive of the four to this hill in both directions, losing the most at 0.927767 and gaining the most at 1.068566. Weymouth loses the least.

## Uphill is not the mirror of downhill

Read across the Weymouth row and average its two fractions. The answer is 0.998132, which is close to one and is not one. The term is an exponential and an exponential is not symmetric about zero, so equal distances up and down are not equal effects on the rate. The average sitting below one is the direction of the asymmetry: on that row the climb takes away slightly more than the matching descent gives back.

This is the most practically important sentence in the module. A route that climbs 1500.000000 ft and then descends 1500.000000 ft has not done nothing to the gas, and treating an undulating profile as though the ups and downs cancel is an assumption the arithmetic refuses.

## The same hill, four different costs

The elevation term is shared by all four forms, and the four still respond differently to it, because each form combines the shared adjustment with its own friction treatment. So the choice of form affects not only the rate on a flat line but also what a hill does to that rate. A project that has chosen its form has therefore also chosen, without deciding anything further, how sensitive its answer is to the survey, and a route with real relief on it deserves the same scrutiny of the form as of the profile.

## Both effects, one slope

A reader who has followed the two previous lessons can now say what happens physically when a line climbs. The gas has to carry a column of itself uphill, which shows up as a multiplier on the outlet inside the group and takes rate away. The pressure distribution along the line changes as a result, which shows up as a longer effective length and takes a little more rate away. One slope, two mechanisms, and the engine reports both.

## The mistake

The mistake is netting a profile to a single elevation change and assuming the answer is the same. The engine will accept the net figure and answer it honestly, and the answer will be for a line that climbs once rather than a line that undulates.

The second mistake is assuming a descent buys back what the matching climb cost. On the Weymouth row it buys back slightly less, which is what an average of 0.998132 rather than 1.000000 is saying.
