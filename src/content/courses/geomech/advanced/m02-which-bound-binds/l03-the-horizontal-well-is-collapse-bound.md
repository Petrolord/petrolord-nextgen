# The horizontal well is collapse bound

The shallow case, and what it implies.

{{panel:gm-window-explorer}}

## At the tightest point

| quantity | value |
|---|---|
| measured depth | 1020 m |
| pore pressure | 1030 kg/m3 |
| collapse | 1363.0134212003486 kg/m3 |
| lower bound | 1363.0134212003486 kg/m3 |
| fracture initiation | 2727.199741688036 kg/m3 |

The collapse pressure exceeds the pore pressure by 333.0134212003486 kg/m3. Stability sets the floor, and by a wide margin.

## Why the collapse pressure is so high here

Because the rock is weak. At about 1020 m of true vertical depth the UCS interpolates to about 18 MPa, against over 100 MPa at the slant well's tightest point.

A weak rock cannot carry the hoop stress concentration, so it needs the mud to hold it up.

## Why the pore pressure is so low

Because this depth is above the ramp. It is normally pressured at 1030 kg/m3, and it stays there down to 1500 m.

## The consequence for the mud programme

The mud weight in this section is set by STABILITY, not by well control. A mud at the pore pressure plus a trip margin, say 1080 kg/m3, would be perfectly safe against a kick and would produce a hole full of cavings.

That is the failure mode nobody sees coming on a well drilled by pore pressure alone.

## The other levers

Because the bound is collapse rather than pore pressure, the mud weight is not the only option.

**Trajectory.** The window widens as the well builds angle, so kicking off shallower or building faster would move the tightest point up into stronger territory sooner. That is a well plan change with other consequences.

**Open-hole time.** This model has no time in it, and real shale weakens the longer it is exposed. Drilling the section faster genuinely helps and the model cannot show it.

**Mud chemistry.** A reactive shale exposed to the wrong water activity loses strength. Also outside this model, also real.

## The number to give the driller

1363.0134212003486 kg/m3 is a zero-breakout-width number. It is the mud weight at which a breakout would just begin.

Allowing a modest breakout would lower it. Whether to do so is a judgement that needs offset caliper data, and stating the assumption is more useful than adjusting the number quietly.

## The trend either side

The collapse gradient falls all the way through this interval, from 1429.547271173751 kg/m3 at 900 m to 1277.2367873694418 at 1200 m, because the rock is strengthening faster than the stresses grow.

So the tightest point is not where the collapse gradient is highest. It is where the falling collapse trend and the trajectory effect combine to give the narrowest window, and that is 1020 m.
