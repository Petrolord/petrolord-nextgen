# No time, and no chemistry

The two reasons a hole that was stable yesterday is not stable today.

## No time

Every calculation in this course is a state. Given a stress field, a rock and a hole, here are two pressures.

Nothing in it depends on how long the hole has been open.

## What happens over time in a real hole

**Pore pressure diffuses into the near-wall rock.** Mud pressure higher than the pore pressure drives fluid in, which raises the near-wall pore pressure, which lowers the effective stresses, which weakens the rock. A hole that was stable at the moment of drilling becomes less so over hours to days.

**The rock creeps.** Shale and salt deform continuously under a constant load. Salt closes a hole; shale relaxes stress and redistributes it.

**Damage accumulates.** Microcracking under sustained load progresses, and the effective strength falls.

All three make a hole worse the longer it is open, and none is in this model.

## The field consequence

The strongest single predictor of trouble in a shale section is OPEN HOLE TIME, and this model cannot show it.

That is why drilling a difficult section fast and casing it promptly works, and why a model-derived mud weight that was adequate on a fast section can be inadequate on a slow one.

## No chemistry

The model has one strength number and no notion of what the mud is made of.

## What chemistry does

**Water activity.** A water-based mud whose water activity differs from the shale's drives water in or out by osmosis, which changes the near-wall pore pressure and the rock's strength directly.

**Clay swelling.** Reactive clays take up water and expand, which damages the wall mechanically.

**Ion exchange.** The salt content of the mud changes the clay's behaviour, and matching it to the formation is a large part of shale drilling practice.

## Why an oil-based mud helps

It presents a low water activity and a membrane that limits transport. That is a chemical solution to a problem that looks mechanical, and it is why oil-based mud is chosen for reactive shale despite its cost.

A model with no chemistry cannot represent any of that, so it cannot explain why the same mud weight succeeds in one mud system and fails in another.

## What this means for using the output

The collapse gradient is a mechanical answer to a problem that is often partly chemical and always partly time-dependent.

Where it disagrees with field experience in shale, the model is not necessarily wrong about the mechanics. It may simply be answering a smaller question than the hole asked.
