# Linear elastic, and isotropic

Two assumptions in the first line of the model.

## Linear elastic

Stress proportional to strain, with no permanent deformation and no dependence on the loading path.

That is what lets a single Young's modulus and a single Poisson ratio describe the rock, and it is what makes the Kirsch solution valid at all: superposition only works in a linear system.

## Where it holds

At small strains, well below failure, in a competent rock. Most of the rock most of the time.

## Where it does not

**Near the hole wall at high stress.** The rock there is close to failure, and rock close to failure is not linear: it dilates, it softens, and it redistributes stress away from the peak.

That redistribution is why a real breakout stabilises at a finite width instead of running away. A linear elastic model has no mechanism for it, which is part of why the zero-breakout-width criterion is so conservative.

**In salt and in very plastic shale.** Those creep, which is a time-dependent inelastic response, and the model has no time in it at all.

**In a depleted reservoir.** Repeated loading and unloading leaves permanent strain, and the stress path there is not the burial path the model assumes.

## Isotropic

The same elastic properties in every direction. One Young's modulus, one Poisson ratio, no dependence on orientation.

## Where that fails

**Shale.** Shale is the most anisotropic common sedimentary rock, with a clear difference between properties along the bedding and across it. Ratios of 1.3 to 2 between the two directions are ordinary.

**Any laminated sequence.** Thin beds of different lithologies behave as an anisotropic medium at the scale of a wellbore.

## What anisotropy would change

The stress concentration around the hole depends on the elastic contrast, so an anisotropic rock loads its wall differently, and the difference is largest when the hole is at an angle to the bedding.

Which is exactly the situation of a deviated well through shale, which is the situation that gives the most trouble in the field.

## The size of the error

Not small. Studies comparing isotropic and transversely isotropic models on the same deviated well through shale find collapse gradients differing by an amount comparable with the whole window width.

## Why the model is isotropic anyway

Because a transversely isotropic model needs five elastic constants instead of two, and four of them cannot be obtained from a standard log suite.

The model uses what a well has. That is a legitimate reason and it is not the same as the assumption being harmless.
