# No natural fractures or bedding

The model sees a continuum, and rock has planes in it.

## What the model assumes

An intact, unfractured, homogeneous rock. Failure happens when the stress at the wall exceeds what the material can carry, at whatever angle the stress dictates.

## What real rock has

**Bedding planes.** Every sedimentary rock has them, and they are weaker than the material between them.

**Natural fractures.** Most rock at depth is fractured at some scale, and the fractures have their own orientations and their own low strengths.

**Faults.** Larger, and with a shear strength governed by friction rather than by cohesion.

## What that does to collapse

A hole intersecting a set of weak planes can fail by slip on those planes rather than by shear through intact rock.

The pressure required to prevent that is a different calculation with a different answer, and it depends on the angle between the hole and the planes.

The practical consequence is the classic one: a well drilled at a shallow angle to bedding in fissile shale gives trouble at mud weights that an intact-rock calculation says are ample.

## What that does to fracture

Worse, and in the opposite direction.

The fracture initiation criterion in this model is about splitting intact rock at the wall. A hole intersecting a natural fracture does not have to split anything: it only has to open a plane that already exists.

The pressure to do that is roughly the normal stress across the fracture, which can be well below the intact initiation pressure and even below the minimum horizontal stress if the fracture is favourably oriented.

## The consequence for lost circulation

Losses in a naturally fractured formation start at pressures the model says are safe.

That is why the practical upper bound in a fractured formation is a measured leak-off or a lost circulation history rather than a computed initiation pressure, and why this course said so in the Professional tier.

## What you can do without a new model

**Look at the image logs.** Fracture orientation and density are directly observable and they say whether the assumption is reasonable.

**Look at the offset losses.** A field with a history of losses at low equivalent circulating densities is telling you the intact-rock upper bound is not the operative one.

**Cap the upper bound.** Using the minimum horizontal stress instead of the initiation pressure is a cheap and defensible conservatism in fractured ground.

## What a full treatment needs

A discrete fracture network, with orientations, apertures and strengths, and a criterion for slip and for opening on each set.

That is a specialist study, it needs data most wells do not have, and it is the right answer when the field's loss history says the continuum model is not describing what happens.
