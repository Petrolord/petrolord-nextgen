# They cross twice

A finding the engine's own output produces, and a rule of thumb it destroys.

{{panel:gm-stress-explorer}}

## The claim people make

That the shale correlation is the conservative one, so using it everywhere is safe.

## What the engine says

Sweep both correlations across this profile's sonic range and the sign of the difference flips TWICE.

The two crossings are at about **198.68499251376295** microseconds per metre and at about **409.8356528477385** microseconds per metre.

## The three regions

| sonic range | which reads higher |
|---|---|
| below 198.685 us/m | Horsrud |
| between 198.685 and 409.836 us/m | McNally |
| above 409.836 us/m | Horsrud |

## Where this profile sits

The profile runs from 188 microseconds per metre at 2600 m to 494 at 50 m, so it spans ALL THREE regions.

At the bottom of the hole Horsrud reads higher. Through the middle McNally reads higher. At the top Horsrud reads higher again.

## Why it happens

Because one is a power law and the other an exponential. A power law and an exponential can meet at most twice, and here they do.

That is a property of the functional forms rather than of the rocks. Neither correlation was designed with the other in mind, and there is no physical reason their curves should nest.

## What it destroys

Any statement of the form "use correlation A, it is the conservative one". Conservative in which direction, at which slowness?

At 2600 m the shale correlation gives the LARGER strength, which in a stability calculation is the LESS conservative choice, because a stronger rock tolerates a lighter mud.

## What to do instead

Use the correlation that matches the lithology, because that is what it was calibrated for. When the lithology is uncertain, run both and quote the range, and say which end of the range each decision was made on.

## Where the difference is largest

At the extremes. At 494 microseconds per metre, the shallowest reading in this profile, Horsrud gives 7354691.975767447 Pa and McNally gives 5309584.7770084385 Pa, a ratio of about 1.39.

That is a 39 percent disagreement about the strength of the shallowest rock in the well, from two equally published correlations.
