# Why we map

Correlation left you with a framework of picks. In each well you decided which surface was which, and you defended that decision with log character rather than depth coincidence. That is a real result, but it is still a set of isolated facts. You know the depth of TOP_SAND at six points on a field that covers several square kilometres, and you know nothing about the rock between those points.

Mapping is the step that closes that gap. It takes scattered picks and produces a continuous surface: a value for the depth of TOP_SAND everywhere inside a defined area, not just where a bit was turned. The product is a depth map, and it is the first deliverable in the geoscience path that describes the field as a whole rather than as a collection of wells.

## What a depth map answers

A depth map is not decoration. It is the object that four expensive questions are asked of, and none of them can be answered from a table of well picks.

Where is the structure high? Hydrocarbons are buoyant. They migrate updip and collect at the crest of a structure, so the shallowest part of the mapped surface is the first place worth caring about. A table of six depths tells you which well is shallowest. A map tells you where the shallowest point of the surface is, which is very often not at a well at all.

How big is the closure? A closure is the part of the structure that can hold hydrocarbons, bounded by the deepest contour that still closes on itself. You cannot see a closure in a list of numbers, because a closure is a shape. It exists only once the surface is continuous, and its size in map area is what later gets multiplied into a volume.

Where do we drill next? A development or appraisal well is proposed at a location, and that location has to come with a predicted depth for the target. That prediction is read off the map. It is a commitment made before the rock is seen, and the map is the document that justifies it.

What volume sits above a contact? If a fluid contact is known or assumed at some depth, the rock above it and inside the closure is the gross rock volume. That number is the ancestor of every barrel that ever gets booked, and it is computed from the map surface and the contact together.

## Where mapping sits in the workflow

The geoscience ladder has an order, and each rung answers a different question about the same rock.

Petrophysics tells you what a well contains. It works down one vertical line and reports shale volume, porosity and saturation for that line.

Correlation tells you how the wells relate. It works laterally between wells and decides which surfaces are the same surfaces.

Mapping tells you what happens between and beyond the wells. It is the first step whose output covers ground that no well has touched. That is exactly what makes it powerful and exactly what makes it dangerous.

Volumetrics then integrates the map. It takes the surface, a contact, an area and a set of rock properties, and turns them into a volume. Every uncertainty in the map arrives in the volume magnified, because volume is an integral over the whole map area, including the parts of it that were invented.

## The discipline of the course

Here is the sentence that runs through every module that follows. A map is mostly interpolation.

Count it on the fixture you are about to meet. Six wells provide six known depths. The grid you build in the capstone has hundreds of nodes. Every node that is not a well is a value that no measurement produced. The algorithm made it up, in a controlled and defensible way, but it made it up. A printed map hides that distinction completely: the contour drawn through hard data and the contour drawn through pure inference are the same weight of line on the same sheet of paper.

So the honesty of a map is not a matter of how smooth or how pretty it is. It is a matter of whether the map says where it is supported by data and where it is not. That is why this course spends a whole module on extrapolation limits, and why the capstone grades how many of the grid's nodes were allowed to carry a value at all. A map that stops at the edge of its support is more useful than one that confidently covers the whole licence block on the strength of six picks.

Keep the question in your head from here on. For any point on the map, how far is it from the nearest well, and how much of what I am reading is measurement rather than assumption?
