# The thick spot is not the crest

A depth map has a crest. A thickness map has a thick spot. On this field they are in different places, and the fact that they can be is one of the more useful things a Professional-tier mapper knows.

{{panel:mp-isochore-explorer}}

## Where each one is

Set the surface to TOP_SAND and find the shallowest point: 1539.7181 m at node (1400, 2000), in the north-centre of the field near Ekene-3 and Ekene-6.

Now switch to the isochore and find the thickest point: 35.8977 m at node (2200, 1200), in the southeast near Ekene-2.

They are about 1100 m apart, in different quadrants of a field 2400 m across.

## Why that is not surprising

Structure and thickness are made by different processes at different times.

**Thickness** is depositional, and mostly set at the time the sand was laid down: where the accommodation was, where the channel or the lobe was thickest, where later erosion cut into it.

**Structure** is what happened afterwards: burial, compaction and whatever tectonics folded or faulted the interval into its present shape.

There is no reason for the two to coincide, and when they do it is usually because something links them, such as growth over a structure that was already there during deposition, or differential compaction over a thick sand body.

So a map pair that shows a crest in one place and a thick spot in another is the ordinary case, and one that shows them coincident is the case worth explaining.

## The check the pair makes possible

Compare the wells across both quantities:

| Well | TOP_SAND | Rank by depth | Thickness | Rank by thickness |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Ekene-3 | 1541 | shallowest | 29 | 5th |
| Ekene-6 | 1546 | 2nd | 34 | 2nd |
| Ekene-1 | 1548 | 3rd | 32 | 3rd |
| Ekene-5 | 1552 | 4th | 31 | 4th |
| Ekene-2 | 1565 | 5th | 36 | thickest |
| Ekene-4 | 1590 | deepest | 25 | thinnest |

The two rankings agree in the middle and disagree at both ends. The shallowest well is nearly the thinnest, and the second deepest well is the thickest. Only the deepest well, Ekene-4, is at an extreme of both.

That pattern rules out the simplest depositional story, which would be a sand thickening into a low, and it also rules out the simplest structural story, which would be uniform thickness folded into a dome. Something less tidy happened, and two maps say so where one could not.

## What this changes about the prospect

Prospect P-1 at (1600, 1600) sits closer to the crest than to the thick spot. Its mapped depth is 1542.62 m, only 2.9 m below the crest, so structurally it is well placed. Its mapped thickness is 34.05 m, which is above the well mean of 31.17 m but below the thickest well.

Those two facts belong in the same sentence when the prospect is described, because a structural high with a thin reservoir and a thick reservoir off structure are different prospects with the same map.

## The habit

> Never present a depth map without its thickness map, and never rank locations on one of them alone.

A ranking on depth alone finds the structurally best location, which may have little rock in it. A ranking on thickness alone finds the most rock, which may be below the contact. The pair together is the minimum for a decision, and the volumetrics course is where the pair plus a contact becomes a number.

## Worked example

A colleague ranks three locations for a well by depth alone and picks the shallowest. You have the isochore. Location A maps at 1541 m depth and 28 m thick, B at 1545 m and 34 m thick, C at 1548 m and 35 m thick. What does the pair say?

A is 4 m higher than B and carries 6 m less rock, which is 21 percent less. Unless the contact is very shallow, so that the extra 4 m of structure buys column that B does not have, B is the better location on this evidence and C is close behind. The depth-only ranking inverts the order.

The point is not that thickness beats depth. It is that neither number decides on its own, and the calculation that combines them properly is volumetrics.
