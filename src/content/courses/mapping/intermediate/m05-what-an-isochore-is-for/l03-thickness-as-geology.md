# Thickness as geology

A thickness map is the closest a mapper gets to reading depositional history. This lesson looks at what the Ekene isochore appears to say, and is careful about how much of that six control points can support.

## What the map appears to show

The isochore thins toward the northeast and thickens toward the south-centre. The thinnest point is 25 m at Ekene-4 in the far northeast; the thickest is nearly 36 m near Ekene-2 in the southeast; the well in the middle, Ekene-6, sits at 34 m.

Read as geology that is a sand body thinning toward the northeast, with its depositional axis somewhere through the middle and south of the field.

## Four stories that fit

Every one of these produces the same map, and the map cannot separate them.

**Depositional thinning.** The sand was laid down thinner to the northeast, because that direction was further from the sediment source, shallower, or outside the main channel or lobe. The most common reading and usually the right one.

**Onlap.** The interval was deposited against a pre-existing high in the northeast and pinches out against it. That would predict a genuine zero somewhere northeast of Ekene-4, which is a testable difference from the first story.

**Erosion at the top.** The interval was originally uniform and its upper part was removed in the northeast by a later erosional surface. This predicts that the **top** is the truncated surface, so the missing section is at the top of the interval rather than distributed through it.

**Erosion or non-deposition at the base.** The mirror image, with the missing section at the base.

Those four have different consequences for reservoir quality, for lateral continuity and for where the sand disappears entirely, and none of them can be distinguished from a thickness map alone.

## What would separate them

**The logs.** If the interval thins by losing its uppermost sand while the lower part is unchanged, the responsible surface is at the top. If every part thins proportionally, deposition is more likely. That comparison is a correlation exercise, and the well correlation course is where the section is flattened to make it.

**Seismic.** Onlap, truncation and simple thinning look different on a section, and this is the case where the seismic answers a question the wells cannot.

**More wells.** Five of the six here are on the outside of the pattern, so the thinning direction rests on Ekene-4 alone. A single additional well northeast of it would say more than any amount of reinterpretation.

## How much six points support

Very little, and this is the honest part of the lesson.

The thinning direction is defined by **one well**. Remove Ekene-4 and the map loses its thin corner and the northeast trend with it. A trend that depends on a single control point is a hypothesis, not a finding.

The thick spot near Ekene-2 is likewise a single well, and the previous module showed that the map cannot even report a value at Ekene-2's own coordinates because it sits on the mask boundary.

Between the wells the map is a minimum-bending surface, which produces smooth, plausible, geologically-innocent shapes whatever the data. The contours between wells are the spline's opinion, not the field's.

## The sentence to write

> The isochore thins from about 34 to 36 m in the centre and south to 25 m at Ekene-4 in the northeast. The direction is controlled by a single well and is consistent with depositional thinning, onlap or truncation, which the present data cannot distinguish.

That sentence is short, it commits to the observation, and it declines to commit to the interpretation. A reader can act on it. The alternative, *the isochore shows a channel axis trending north-northwest*, sounds more expert and asserts something six wells cannot support.

## Worked example

A colleague adds a fifth story: the sand is uniform and Ekene-4 simply missed part of it because the well deviated through the interval. How would you test that?

Check the deviation survey. A well that crosses a dipping bed obliquely records more measured-depth thickness rather than less, so a deviated well is more likely to overstate than understate an interval, which makes this story a poor fit for the thinnest well in the set. If Ekene-4 is vertical, as the course's fixture assumes, the story fails immediately.

The general point is that the first test of an anomalous well is usually the well data rather than the geology.

## Exercise

Name three depositional or structural histories consistent with the Ekene isochore and state what evidence would separate them. Then say how many wells define the thinning direction and what follows from that.

As a self-check: depositional thinning away from a source, onlap against a pre-existing high, and erosional truncation at the top or base of the interval are all consistent with the map; logs correlated on a flattened section would show whether the loss is at the top, the base or distributed, and seismic would distinguish onlap and truncation geometries directly. The thinning direction is defined by Ekene-4 alone, so it is a hypothesis supported by one control point rather than a mapped trend, and it should be reported as such.
