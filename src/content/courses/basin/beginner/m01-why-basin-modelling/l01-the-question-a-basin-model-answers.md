# The question a basin model answers

Every other course in this ladder describes rock that is there. Petrophysics reads what a tool saw in a hole that exists. Seismic interpretation maps a reflector that is in the ground today. An earth model builds a container out of surfaces picked from data you can point at. Pore pressure reads the state of a column as it stands this morning. All of that is honest work, and all of it describes a snapshot. The snapshot is today.

A basin model asks something different. It asks whether this basin ever made hydrocarbons, when it made them, and whether they got anywhere. Not one of those three questions can be answered by looking at the rock as it is now. They are questions about a sequence of events, so the only way to answer them is to run the clock.

## Three questions, and all of them are about time

The first question is whether a source rock in this basin ever got hot enough for long enough to convert its organic matter into oil or gas. A source rock that never reached the temperatures required is still a source rock in the descriptive sense. It has the organic carbon, it has the kerogen type, and it has generated nothing. The rock looks the part and the basin is empty.

The second question is when. This is the one that catches people, because it sounds like a detail and it decides the answer. Generation that happened before there was a trap to receive it is generation into nothing. The hydrocarbons were made, they moved, and they left the system through the seabed or the outcrop long before the structure that you have mapped existed. A model that reports generation without reporting its date has answered half a question.

The third question is whether the charge got anywhere. Generation is a change inside the source rock. It only becomes a discovery if enough was made to push fluid out of the source, if there was a path, and if something at the end of the path held it.

## Why a snapshot cannot answer them

Take a shale you have logged with its top at 1000 m. Today it is 100 m thick. It was not always 100 m thick, and it was not always at 1000 m. Restore that same grain to the surface and it occupies 159.79553483785466 m. The layer has lost 59.79553483785466 m of thickness to compaction, which is 37.42 percent of its original thickness.

That single fact breaks the snapshot. If you want to know how deep the layer below this shale sat 40 million years ago, you cannot read it off today's depths, because the shale above it was over half again as thick then and everything under it was pushed down accordingly. Depth in the past is not today's depth minus what has been added since. It has to be reconstructed, layer by layer, with the compaction taken back out.

Temperature has the same problem and it matters more. A source rock's history of temperature is what decides whether it generated anything, and temperature follows burial depth. Get the burial history wrong and the thermal history is wrong, which means the answer to question one is wrong, and the date attached to question two is wrong with it.

## What running the clock requires

A basin model is built in a fixed order, and each stage is the input to the next.

First, burial. You need to know where every layer sat at every point in the past, which means taking compaction back out of the section and putting each layer back at the thickness and the depth it had when it was younger.

Second, heat. Given a depth history, you need a temperature history, which means knowing what heat is flowing up through the section and how well the rocks conduct it.

Third, maturity. Given a temperature history, kinetics tells you how much of the kerogen has reacted, and how far the source rock has advanced towards and through the oil window.

Fourth, charge. Given a conversion, you get a mass of hydrocarbons generated, a fraction of it expelled from the source, and a migration path towards a trap.

This tier builds the first two. That is not a shortcut, and the reasons are in lesson 4 of this module.

## What the higher tiers add

The Professional tier runs the maturity kinetics, where the striking result is that time acts as a reagent, so a slower heating history can end up more mature at the same temperature than a faster one. The Expert tier runs the full forward model over 150 million years on a four layer reference basin, reads generation and expulsion, and recovers the signature of a 600 m erosion event by rerunning the history without it. Those are named here so you know where the ladder goes. They are not taught at this tier and they are not examined at this tier.

## What a basin model is not

A basin model is not a volumetric estimate. It does not tell you how many barrels are in a structure, because that is what the reservoir and volumetrics courses do with a container.

It is not a substitute for a well. Every basin model contains assumptions about heat flow, about lithology, about how much section has been removed by erosion, and each of those assumptions is a place where the answer can go wrong quietly.

What it does give you is the thing no map gives you. It gives you a history, dated, with the trap and the charge placed on the same timeline so that you can see whether they ever lined up.

## Exercise

Write down the three questions a basin model answers, in your own words. Then, for each one, say what would go wrong if you tried to answer it from today's depths alone, without reconstructing the past.

Self check: the questions are whether a source rock ever generated, when it generated, and whether the charge reached a trap. Answering the first from today's depths assumes the source rock has always been as deep as it is now, so a rock that was uplifted looks cooler than it ever was and a rock that was buried deeper before erosion looks cooler still. The second cannot be answered at all from a snapshot, because a snapshot carries no dates. The third fails because a trap that exists today may not have existed when the charge arrived, and a snapshot cannot tell those two situations apart.
