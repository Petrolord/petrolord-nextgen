# Why time and temperature both matter

## A temperature is not an answer

The most common way to misuse a heat column is to read one number off it and stop. The source rock is at 59.619047619047684 degC at 1950 m, somebody looks up a window in a textbook, and a whole prospect is called immature on the strength of one figure.

That reasoning skips the thing that makes a basin model worth building. Turning kerogen into oil is a chemical reaction, and a reaction has a rate. The rate depends on temperature, so the reading at 1950 m matters. The amount of reaction that has happened depends on the rate multiplied by the time spent at it, so the reading at 1950 m is only half of what you need.

## Two rocks at the same temperature are not the same rock

Consider two source rocks that both sit at 41.66666666666673 degC at 950 m today.

The first arrived at 950 m early and has been there ever since, held near that temperature for most of the basin's life. The second was shallower for almost all of that time and only reached 950 m recently. A thermometer lowered into either well today reads the same value. The two rocks are not in the same state, because one has been reacting at that rate for a long time and the other has barely started.

Now turn it around. A rock that was pushed briefly to 59.619047619047684 degC at 1950 m and then lifted back to a shallower depth may have converted less kerogen than a rock that never left 41.66666666666673 degC but stayed there far longer. Higher and briefer is not automatically further along than cooler and longer.

That is the sentence worth carrying out of this lesson. Temperature sets the rate. Time sets how much of that rate is collected. Maturity is what you get after collecting.

## Why the effect is not small

If the reaction rate rose gently with temperature, time would be a minor correction and the deepest reading would be a fair proxy. Reaction rates in rock do not behave that way. They rise steeply with temperature, which is why a modest extra burial can change a source rock's state a great deal, and it is also why a long residence at a temperature just below a threshold is not negligible. A slow rate applied for a very long time still accumulates.

The two effects pull in opposite directions and neither one dominates in general. That is exactly why the answer has to be computed rather than judged. A basin that buried its source rock quickly and a basin that buried the same rock slowly to the same present depth can end up in different states, and the difference is not something you can eyeball from a present day temperature log.

## Heating rate is the honest way to say it

The higher tiers express this as a heating rate, in degC per Ma. A rock heated at 1 degC per Ma takes three times as long to reach any given temperature as the same rock heated at 3 degC per Ma. Both rocks pass through the same temperatures. One of them lingers.

Here is the part that surprises people the first time. When both rocks are compared at the same temperature, the slow one is the more mature of the two, because getting there took it longer and every degree along the way was collected for longer. The comparison is made at equal temperature and the slow rock still wins, which only makes sense once you accept that time is a reagent in the reaction rather than a bookkeeping detail.

The Professional tier turns that statement into numbers, using a published kinetic scheme and a kerogen clock. This tier does not, and you should not try to force a maturity out of a steady column. What this tier gives you is the input that scheme consumes: a track of depths and a temperature at each one.

## What this means for the workflow

Three practical consequences follow, and all three are within this tier's reach.

The first is that a burial history is worth reconstructing properly. If decompaction is skipped and the old layers are placed too shallow, the model has them too cool for too long, and every downstream maturity is understated. The geometry error becomes a chemistry error.

The second is that the shape of the burial curve matters as much as its end point. Two curves that finish at the same depth but differ in the middle describe different histories. A basin that buried its source early and then sat still is not the same as one that buried it late and fast.

The third is about how you report a result. A temperature quoted on its own invites the reader to draw a maturity conclusion from it. Quote the temperature with its depth, say that the column is a steady state snapshot rather than a history, and let the tier that owns the kinetics do the kinetics.
