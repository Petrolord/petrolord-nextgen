# What a steady model would miss

The Associate tier computed temperatures from a steady column, and its final lesson listed "steady state" among its four omissions. This tier built the transient machine. The fair question: what did the extra machinery actually buy on this fixture? This lesson answers by comparing, feature by feature, what a steady-per-step model would have reported.

## Where steady would have done fine

Be honest about the boring majority first. Through the basin's quiet stretches, 140 to 81 Ma with slow shallow geometry, 60 to 21 Ma with geometry frozen and heat flow drifting gently, the column tracks its steady answer closely: changes arrive slowly enough that relaxation keeps pace. The graded final temperature is the clearest case, 149.76037539670858 in the transient run against essentially the same from a steady solve of today's column, because ten quiet megayears closed the gap. A basin whose history is gentle everywhere is a basin where the Associate tier's arithmetic, applied per step, would grade nearly as well.

## Where steady fails: the arrivals

Every instant deposition breaks the steady assumption spectacularly for a few megayears. At the Upper Shale's arrival, the transient source reads 153.07156059972218, then climbs through 161.38 toward the mid-160s over subsequent steps; a steady model jumps straight to the settled answer in the arrival step. The transient lag is real physics, cold sediment and finite diffusion, and its kinetic consequence is systematic: the steady shortcut over-cooks the years the real column spent catching up. On this fixture the arrival lags are the main transient-versus-steady difference in the maturity integrals.

## Where steady fails worst: the removal

Erosion inverts the sign and raises the stakes. At the 10 Ma removal, the transient column relaxes downward from 167.24573484238402 through 153.84048634909556 toward 149.76: the deep rock stays warm for a while after its blanket is stripped, because heat takes time to drain. A steady model snaps cold instantly, deleting that warm tail from the hot decade's end.

The tail matters for exactly the quantities this module's statement cares about. The ratchet does not care, the maximum was already set, but any process still integrating in those years, the last increments of TR, and in a basin with a still-live kitchen the last expulsion, sees temperatures the steady model denies ever existed. The Associate tier's sentence was precise: the steady assumption is most misleading exactly when the basin is doing something interesting, and erosion is the most interesting thing this basin does.

## The diagnostic

The comparison yields a working diagnostic for when transient machinery is worth its cost, and the answer is a timescale ratio. Conductive relaxation across this basin's depth runs of order a megayear; geometry events here are instantaneous. Whenever geological change is faster than relaxation, arrivals, erosion, rapid subsidence pulses, the transient column diverges from steady for a relaxation time afterward, and any kinetics running during that window inherit the difference. Change slower than relaxation, and steady is a fine approximation. Real basins put most of their maturation in the fast-change eras, which is why serious models are transient even though most of their steps would forgive steadiness.

## Worked example

A modeller proposes running this fixture with steady heat to save complexity, arguing the graded values would survive. Assess field by field. Final temperature: survives, quiet ending. Final Ro and TR, and both masses: shift by the accumulated arrival-lag and removal-tail differences, small on this fixture but not obviously inside the tight tolerances, and unverifiable without running the transient model anyway. The signature: differences of two steady runs, arrival-lag biases partially cancelling, closest call of the six. Verdict: some fields probably survive, none provably, and the proof requires the machinery being avoided. That is the general shape of shortcut arguments against transient physics.
