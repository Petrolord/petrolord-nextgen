# The hole is not the bit

An 8-1/2 inch bit does not leave an 8-1/2 inch hole.

{{panel:cm-volume-explorer}}

## The fact

Open hole is bigger than the bit that drilled it, almost everywhere, and by an amount nobody measured.

**Washout.** Circulating mud erodes the wall, particularly in unconsolidated sand and particularly where the hole was open for a long time.

**Breakout.** Where the rock fails in shear at the wall, which is the collapse mechanism the Geomechanics course computes, the hole becomes oval and larger in one direction.

**Soluble section.** Salt dissolves into a water-based mud and leaves a cavern.

## Why it matters here and almost nowhere else

Every other calculation in this suite is happy with the bit size. Hydraulics is a bit conservative with it, torque and drag hardly notices, and the collapse rating of the casing does not care at all.

Cement volume cares completely, because cement volume IS the annular capacity. A hole ten percent bigger in area takes ten percent more cement, and the cement that is not there is a length of uncemented annulus at the top.

## The direction of the error

Always the same direction. Guess the excess LOW and the job comes up short: the top of cement is below the target, and the interval that was supposed to be isolated is not.

Guess it HIGH and you have spent money on cement that went further up the annulus than planned, which is a cost and occasionally a nuisance if it reaches somewhere it has to be drilled out of.

So the guess is deliberately biased. The industry uses excess figures that are known to be generous, because the two errors are not symmetric.

## What the number typically is

Ten to twenty percent in a competent, quickly drilled hole. Thirty to fifty in a long-exposed or unconsolidated one. More than that where salt or a known washout zone is involved.

This course's wells use 15 percent, which is a fairly tight assumption for 1600 m of open hole.

## The only way to know

A caliper log, run before the casing. It measures the hole diameter, usually on several arms, and integrates to a real volume.

Most wells do not have one over the section being cemented, which is why the excess is a guess.
