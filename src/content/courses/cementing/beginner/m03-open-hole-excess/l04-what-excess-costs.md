# What excess costs

In sacks, in money, and in the two things that are not money.

{{panel:cm-volume-explorer}}

## In sacks

On this course's slant well, at a yield of 0.0382 cubic metres a sack:

| excess | sacks |
|---|---|
| 0 | 583.6636965864452 |
| 15 | 657.6801293970221 |
| 30 | 731.6965622075991 |
| 50 | 830.3851392883682 |

Every 15 percent of excess is about 74 sacks. Going from a tight assumption to a generous one costs a quarter of the cement bill.

## In job time

    job time = total pumped / pump rate

More slurry means more total pumped, so the job takes longer. On this well the difference between zero and 50 percent excess is 9.424759111213458 cubic metres of extra slurry, which at 0.02 cubic metres a second is about eight minutes.

That is small against the 72 minute job, because the displacement volume does not change and it is the bulk of the pumping.

## In circulating density

This is the cost that is not money. A bigger excess means a taller and heavier cement column in the annulus for the same top of cement target, and that raises the equivalent circulating density at every exposed formation.

The Professional tier computes that directly. A generous excess is not free: it moves the job toward the fracture limit.

## In the top of cement, if you get it wrong LOW

The cement stops short. On the slant well, missing the excess by 15 percentage points is 2.8274277333640374 cubic metres of annular slurry, which at the cased capacity of 0.013356688045922537 is 212 m of uncemented annulus at the top.

Two hundred metres. That is the whole reason the industry biases the guess upward.

## In the top of cement, if you get it wrong HIGH

The cement goes further up than planned. Usually harmless, occasionally not: cement above a planned top can reach a previous casing shoe, or a liner hanger, or a section that has to be drilled out, and each of those turns a saving into a problem.

## The honest summary

The asymmetry is real but it is not infinite. A generous excess costs money, time and margin against the fracture gradient, and a tight one costs isolation. The right answer is a caliper log, and where there is not one, an excess chosen from offset wells in the same formation rather than from a template.
