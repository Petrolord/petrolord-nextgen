# What the diagnostics agreed on

Two completely independent methods, from two different tiers, using different data and different mathematics, reached the same conclusion about the Ekene flood. This lesson is about why that agreement is worth more than either result alone, and about how far it actually goes.

## The two arguments

**The Chan diagnostic**, from the Professional tier. It reads the SHAPE of the water oil ratio over time on log-log axes and classifies the mechanism from the late-time slope of the derivative. On Ekene-6 the slope is 2.348281726147951 against a channelling threshold of 0.4, and on Ekene-3 it is 1.6028659409443355.

Inputs: producer oil and water rates. Method: numerical differentiation and a log-log regression. No volumes, no allocation, no pattern geometry.

**The volumetric back-out**, from this module. It takes the observed breakthrough date, the water allocated to that producer by then, and the two dimensionless numbers from the displacement construction and the areal sweep correlation, and inverts for the pore volume the water could have contacted: 1.4697005138728763 percent of the element.

Inputs: an injection record, an allocation matrix, a booked pore volume, and two correlations. No curve shapes, no derivatives, no thresholds.

The two share essentially nothing except the field.

## Why independence matters

A single diagnostic that says "channelling" is a hypothesis with a known failure mode. Chan's classification is a pattern match against cases where the mechanism was known, and the Professional tier was explicit that the Ekene water histories are designed ramps whose shape may reflect the design rather than a mechanism.

A single volumetric argument is also a hypothesis, resting on an allocation matrix that is judgement and on two correlations whose provenance is 1950s regression.

Two hypotheses failing in the same direction, from disjoint inputs, is a much stronger position. For both to be wrong, the shape argument would have to be misled by the designed ramp AND the volumetric argument would have to be wrong by a factor of fifty in the same direction. Those are unrelated failures and requiring both is a much smaller probability than requiring either.

That structure, two independent methods pointing the same way, is what evidence looks like in reservoir engineering. It is worth building your work so that you have it.

## How far the agreement goes

Honestly: not as far as it might look.

**Both are about the same well.** Ekene-6 dominates both results. The field-level Chan aggregate is essentially a restatement of Ekene-6, and the back-out was computed for Ekene-6. Two methods agreeing about one well is weaker than two methods agreeing across four.

**The back-out is quantitative and Chan is not.** The back-out says 1.47 percent. Chan says "channelling-like" with a hedge in the wording. Only one of them can be argued with numerically.

**Neither identifies the path.** Both say the water is going somewhere fast. Neither says whether it is a thin high-permeability streak, a fracture, a fault, or communication behind casing, and the treatment differs for each.

## The third piece of evidence that is missing

A tracer. Inject a chemical or radioactive tag into Ekene-2, watch which producers return it and when, and the transit time gives the path length and the return fraction gives the connectivity directly.

That is the measurement that would settle it, it costs real money and months of waiting, and it is the natural next step given two independent indications. A course that stopped at "two methods agree, therefore channelling" would be teaching over-confidence. The correct conclusion is "two independent indications justify the cost of a direct measurement".

## What would falsify it

Worth stating, because a hypothesis that nothing could falsify is not a hypothesis.

If a tracer injected at Ekene-2 returned at Ekene-6 slowly and broadly, with a long tail and a large fraction of the injected mass recovered over months, that would be matrix displacement and both arguments here would be wrong.

If a pressure interference test showed poor connectivity between Ekene-2 and Ekene-6, the allocation would be wrong and the back-out's input volume with it.

If the water at Ekene-6 turned out to be formation water rather than injected water, from a chemical analysis, then it never came from the flood at all and both diagnostics are reading a coincidence.

All three are checkable. The last one is cheap and should be done first.

## The habit

When you have one diagnostic, look for a second that shares as little as possible with it. When two agree, say what they share and what they do not. When they disagree, that is more informative still, and the reconciliation is usually where the real answer is.

## The misconception to avoid

"Two methods agree, so the conclusion is established." Two methods agreeing raises confidence and does not establish anything, particularly when both are indirect and one is qualitative. What the agreement establishes is that a direct measurement is now worth its cost, which is a decision, not a conclusion.
