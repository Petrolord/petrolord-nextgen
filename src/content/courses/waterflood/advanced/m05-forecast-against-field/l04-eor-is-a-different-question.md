# EOR is a different question

After a waterflood forecast the natural next question is whether something other than water would do better. This lesson takes that question seriously enough to frame it properly, and then hands it on. Answering it is a separate study with its own tools, and treating a waterflood forecast as if it could answer it would be the exact failure this course keeps warning about.

## What the forecast leaves behind

The Ekene design case recovers 0.5545614215589451 of the flooded oil in place, which is 0.2854276257199057 of the element once the vertical sweep is counted properly.

So roughly seventy percent of the oil in the element is still there at the economic limit. It is in three distinct places, and which place decides what could be done about it:

**Behind the displacement front**, as residual oil in swept rock. The Ekene sand's residual oil saturation is 0.25, and the ceiling displacement efficiency is $(1 - 0.35 - 0.25)/(1 - 0.35) = 0.6153846153846154$. Nothing that displaces oil with an immiscible fluid recovers this. It needs a change in the capillary number: surfactant to lower interfacial tension, or a miscible solvent.

**In unswept parts of the pattern area**, because the areal sweep never reached the corners before the economic limit. This is a conformance and pattern problem: infill drilling, pattern realignment, or a mobility improvement.

**In unswept layers**, because the slow layers never broke through. This is vertical conformance: polymer to improve the mobility ratio, or mechanical conformance control to shut off the fast layers.

Three targets, three completely different technologies, and the first step in any screening is deciding which of the three holds the oil.

## What the Ekene evidence says

The channelling result changes this calculation entirely.

If the water is reaching Ekene-6 through 1.47 percent of the pore volume, then the dominant loss is not residual oil in swept rock. It is oil in rock the water never went near. That points at conformance, not at chemistry.

Conformance treatments are also, generally, much cheaper than chemical EOR. A gel treatment on one injector costs a fraction of a surfactant flood. So the evidence points at the cheaper intervention first, which is a fortunate ordering and not always the case.

## Why polymer is the natural screening question and why the answer is muted

Polymer raises the water viscosity, which lowers the mobility ratio, which improves both the areal and the vertical sweep. The SCAL course's screening treatment covers the displacement side.

For Ekene the honest screening answer is that polymer is not the obvious first move.

The mobility ratio is 1.2, already close to unity. Module 2 showed the vertical coverage at first breakthrough moving from 0.5146907350993352 at $M = 1.2$ to 0.5283542009271434 at $M = 1$, a gain of 0.013663465827808197. Module 3 showed the areal sweep at breakthrough moving from 0.6573574366303985 to 0.6838157924688618 over the same change.

Both gains are real and both are small, because the mobility ratio was never the problem. A polymer flood addresses viscous fingering and unfavourable mobility, and Ekene has a light 1.8 cp oil against 0.5 cp water. The problem at Ekene is a path, not a mobility.

## Why this course stops here

Because EOR is a different question from the one this course answers, and its first step has a tool of its own.

The Petrolord Suite's **EOR Screening** app, in the Reservoir module, does that first step. It checks a reservoir and its oil against the published screening criteria of Taber, Martin and Seright (1997, Parts 1 and 2), with every limit read from the paper's own tables and tested at, inside and beyond each one. For CO2 it adds a minimum miscibility pressure check from Zhu and co-authors (2025), which reproduces the error figures that paper reports. That is validated screening, built the way this curriculum asks every engine to be built: goldens and published references first, and anything that relies on the engine afterwards.

Screening is still a long way short of design. A screening verdict says which processes deserve a closer look for this oil in this rock. It cannot say where the remaining oil sits, how much of it a process would recover, or whether the project pays. Those are the questions in the next section, and a table of published limits answers none of them.

So nothing in this lesson is graded, no EOR number appears in the capstone, and the treatment above stays qualitative on purpose. This course owns the waterflood; when you want to screen EOR processes for a field, open the EOR Screening app and read its verdicts as a shortlist. When you see an EOR chart anywhere with three significant figures and a recovery factor uplift, ask two things: what validated it, and whether it is a screen or a design.

## What a proper screening would need

For completeness, the shape of the work this course is not doing:

A residual oil saturation to a chemical flood, measured on core, not correlated. A phase behaviour study for the surfactant or solvent at reservoir conditions. An adsorption measurement, because chemical lost to the rock is chemical not doing work. A compositional or chemical-flood simulator, because these processes are not screenable with the displacement construction this course imports. And an economics case, because chemical EOR is capital-intensive and its value is dominated by the timing.

Every one of those is a real project. None of them is a correlation.

## The misconception to avoid

"EOR is what you do when the waterflood is finished." Most EOR decisions are made while the waterflood is still running, because the flood's own performance is the main evidence about where the remaining oil is and why. And a great deal of what gets called EOR at Ekene's stage is not enhanced recovery at all; it is conformance improvement, which is fixing the waterflood rather than replacing it.
