# Elongation pushes in

What happens geometrically when the string gets longer, and which travel it consumes.

{{panel:cd-spaceout-explorer}}

## The direction

The string is hung at surface and its bottom is free in the bore. Heat it and it gets longer, and since the top cannot move, the extra length goes downward.

The seal assembly moves deeper into the receptacle.

## Which travel that consumes

The bore below the seals. Insertion depth is measured from the top, so seals three metres into a six point one metre bore have three metres above them and three point one below, and moving deeper consumes the three point one.

This is where the sign errors live, so it is worth fixing in a sentence you can repeat: elongation consumes the receptacle length less the insertion depth.

## Say it in terms of the receptacle

The receptacle is fixed to the packer. The seals move down relative to it. The room they have to move down is the length of bore below them, which is the receptacle length less the insertion depth.

For the published job that is six point one less three, which is three point one metres.

## What happens at the end of it

The seal assembly shoulders on the bottom of the receptacle, or on whatever stop is machined there. From that point the string cannot lengthen further without loading up, and it goes into compression against the packer.

That is not immediately catastrophic. It is a transition from free to anchored, and the load that then develops is the Casing and Tubing Design course's problem.

## The published elongation case

One point two metres of elongation against three point one metres available. One point nine remaining, comfortably above the half metre margin.

The elongation direction on this job has room to spare, and that is the direction with the smaller length change. That is the imbalance the module is building towards.
