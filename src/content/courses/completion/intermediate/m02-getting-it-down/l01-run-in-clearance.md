# Run-in clearance

The subtraction, the sign convention, and what the number physically is.

{{panel:cd-clearance-explorer}}

## The subtraction

For each component, the clearance is the governing drift to that component's bottom depth, less the component's outside diameter.

Positive means it goes. Negative means it does not. The magnitude is how much room there is, measured as a difference of diameters.

## Diametral, not radial

The number is a difference of two diameters, so it is a diametral clearance. The gap on either side, if the component were perfectly centred, is half of it.

That halving matters when you are picturing the thing. A component with five millimetres of clearance has two and a half millimetres of gap on each side if it is centred, and none at all on one side if it is lying against the wall, which it will be.

The engine reports the diametral number because that is what the specification is stated in and what a vendor quotes. Halving it in your head is the reader's job.

## What the number is not

It is not a running clearance in a deviated hole, because there is no dogleg in this calculation. A rigid component of appreciable length going round a bend needs more clearance than the same component in a straight hole, by an amount that depends on its length and the curvature.

It is not a clearance against anything that is in the well but not in the casing program: scale, a stuck tool, a deformed section.

And it is not a hydraulic clearance. Two millimetres of annulus around a component may be sufficient to run it and completely insufficient to circulate past it.

## The published string

Thirteen rows, all positive. The loosest is over a hundred millimetres and the tightest is under five.

That range of more than twenty to one across a single string is the thing to notice. Clearance is not roughly the same everywhere with one or two exceptions; it varies by more than an order of magnitude, because the wide bodies and the liner conspire at the bottom of the string.
