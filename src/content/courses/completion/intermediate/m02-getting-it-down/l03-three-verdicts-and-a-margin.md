# Three verdicts and a margin

Pass, warn and fail, and why the middle one exists.

{{panel:cd-clearance-explorer}}

## The three

A clearance below zero fails: the component is larger than the guaranteed bore and it will not go.

A clearance above zero but below the warn margin warns: it goes on paper, with less room than the margin says is prudent.

Anything above the margin passes.

## Why a margin at all

Because the drift is a guarantee about new pipe in a rack, and the well is not a rack. Everything the drift lesson listed as outside the guarantee, from scale to ovality to a deformed joint, makes the real bore smaller than the catalog one.

A clearance of half a millimetre against a guarantee is not half a millimetre in the ground. It is very likely negative.

## The published margin

Three millimetres, in this course. The value is a choice and not a specification, and different operators use different ones. What matters is that there is one and that it is stated.

Under that margin the published string passes everywhere. The tightest row clears by about four point seven millimetres, which is one point seven above the margin. That is a thin result and the string is correctly reported as passing.

## The middle verdict is the useful one

A pass and a fail are both easy to act on. The warn is where the engineering conversation happens: the string will probably go, and somebody should decide whether probably is good enough for this well.

Turning the margin up and seeing what starts warning is the fastest way to understand how much slack a design has. The panel lets you do exactly that.

## A margin is not a safety factor

A safety factor multiplies. This margin subtracts, and it subtracts a fixed length rather than a proportion.

That is the right shape here, because the things it protects against are absolute. A millimetre of scale is a millimetre of scale whether the casing is five inches or twenty. A proportional margin would be too tight on small casing and too generous on large.
