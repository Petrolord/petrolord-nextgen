# The class boundary

What happens exactly at eight and five eighths and exactly at thirteen and three eighths, and why the test for it is worth writing.

{{panel:cd-string-explorer}}

## Two boundaries, four behaviours

There are two places where the deduction changes, and at each of them there are two things to get right: what the pipe exactly at the boundary does, and what the pipe just above it does.

Casing at exactly eight and five eighths inches takes one eighth. Casing at anything larger takes five thirty seconds. Casing at exactly thirteen and three eighths takes five thirty seconds. Casing at anything larger takes three sixteenths.

## Testing it properly

The way to pin that down is to hold the inside diameter fixed and vary only the outside diameter across the boundary. Feed the engine a bore of eight inches with an outside diameter of eight and five eighths, then again with an outside diameter one thousandth larger, and compare the two drifts.

The difference between them should be exactly the difference between two deduction constants, and nothing else. If it is anything else, the classification is not doing what the specification says.

That is a strange pipe to ask about. Eight inches of bore inside eight and five eighths of outside diameter is a wall thickness no mill makes. It does not matter. The classification is a lookup on a number, and the test is about the lookup.

## Why a real catalog will not test this

Every real casing row sits comfortably inside a class. The nine and five eighths rows are three quarters of an inch above the first boundary and the sixteen inch rows are well above the second, so a test written only from the catalog never exercises the comparison itself.

A classification error at the boundary would then survive every test, and would show up the first time somebody put a genuine eight and five eighths inch string in a well and got a drift that was too small by a fortieth of an inch.

## Inclusive means inclusive

The single most likely error is writing the comparison the wrong way at the top of a range: treating eight and five eighths as being above eight and five eighths. It reads as a triviality and it is worth three quarters of a millimetre on one of the most common intermediate casing sizes there is.

Three quarters of a millimetre is more than a sixth of the tightest clearance in the published completion in this course.
