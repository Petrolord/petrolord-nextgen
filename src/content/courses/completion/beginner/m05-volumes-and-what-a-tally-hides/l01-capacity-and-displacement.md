# Capacity and displacement

Two volumes computed from the same tally, and the different questions they answer.

{{panel:cd-string-explorer}}

## Capacity

The capacity of a string is how much fluid it holds inside. It is the sum over components of the inside area times the length.

For the published string that comes to about eleven point eight cubic metres. That is the volume you would have to pump to fill the tubing from empty, and it is the volume that has to come out when you displace it.

## Displacement

The displacement is how much fluid the string pushes out of the well when it goes in. It is the sum over components of the outside area times the length.

For the same string that is about twenty six point eight cubic metres, more than twice the capacity.

## Why the second is larger

Because the outside diameter is larger than the inside diameter for every component. The displacement is the volume of the outside envelope of the string, and the capacity is the volume of the inside.

If the string is run open ended and fills as it goes, the volume it actually displaces from the well is the difference between the two, which is the steel. If it is run with the bottom plugged, it displaces the full outside envelope, which is the whole displacement figure.

That distinction is an operational one and it decides how much fluid comes over the shakers while the completion is being run.

## Two calculations that do not use the casing

Both of these are properties of the string alone. Neither reads the casing program, and neither would change if the same completion were run into a different well.

The other two volumes in the next module are not like that at all. They are annular volumes, and they depend entirely on the casing.

## Where these numbers get used

Capacity sets the displacement volume when the completion is displaced to packer fluid, which is a routine operation with a hard endpoint: you have to know when the interface reaches the packer.

Displacement sets the pit gain expected while running in, which is how the crew detects a problem. An unexpected gain or loss against the calculated displacement is a swab or a loss, and the number has to be right for the comparison to mean anything.
