# What the components do

The fourteen kinds of thing that get made up into a completion string, and the job each one does.

{{panel:cd-string-explorer}}

## Pipe, and everything else

Most of a completion by length is tubing. The published string in this course is thirteen components and about two and a half kilometres long, and four of those thirteen components are plain tubing accounting for almost all of the length.

The other nine are what makes it a completion rather than a pipe. They are short, they are expensive, and every fit problem in the well comes from them.

## The list

The catalog this course uses carries fourteen types.

Tubing is the conduit. A flow coupling is a thick walled short joint placed above and below anything that creates turbulence, so that the erosion happens in a sacrificial piece. A blast joint is the same idea against jetting from perforations.

A safety valve is a surface controlled subsurface valve set a few hundred metres down, held open by hydraulic pressure from surface and closing on its own if that pressure is lost. A landing nipple is a machined internal profile that a plug or a gauge can be locked into. An X nipple has a selective profile and an XN nipple has a no go shoulder below it, so that a tool run in cannot pass.

A sliding sleeve opens communication between tubing and annulus. A side pocket mandrel carries a gas lift valve in an offset pocket beside the bore. An expansion joint absorbs length change. A production packer seals tubing to casing. A perforated joint lets fluid in when the shoe is plugged. A wireline entry guide is a bell mouth at the bottom that guides a tool back into the string.

## Why the list matters here

Each of those is described in this course by exactly two numbers: an outside diameter and an inside diameter. The safety valve is not modelled as a valve. The sliding sleeve is not modelled as a sleeve. They are modelled as short lengths of steel with two diameters each.

That sounds like a severe reduction, and it is. It is also the correct reduction for the questions this course asks, because a fit problem does not care what the component does.
