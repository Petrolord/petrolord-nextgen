The annulus loss is the smallest of the three, and its SHARE falls as the rate rises, because the other two grow faster.

## Why it is so much smaller than the pipe loss

Because the annulus is much bigger. The space between a 0.127 m drill pipe and a 0.2159 m hole has about four times the area of the pipe's bore, so the velocity is a quarter and the velocity squared is a sixteenth.

And because the annular flow is largely laminar, where the friction factor falls as one over the Reynolds number and partly cancels the velocity dependence.

## Why it matters far more than its size suggests

Because it is the only one the formation feels.

The mud in the annulus is a column of fluid standing on the formation. Its weight produces the static pressure, and the friction of pushing it up adds to that. The pump and the bit are upstream of the formation entirely.

## The two regimes in one annulus

The cased section is 0.2204974 m in diameter and the open hole is 0.2159 m. The collars are 0.17145 m and the drill pipe is 0.127 m.

So the annulus around the collars in open hole is a 0.0445 m gap and the annulus around the drill pipe in casing is a 0.0935 m gap. A factor of two in the characteristic dimension, and the velocities differ by a similar factor.

The tight annulus around the collars is where the annular loss concentrates, and the wide one around the drill pipe in casing is where the hole cleaning is worst.

## The one to watch

Neither of them is at total depth. The tightest annulus is around the collars, which are just above the bit, and the slowest is in the shallowest cased section.

That is the same lesson the torque and drag course reaches about side force: the interesting places in a well are rarely the deepest ones.
