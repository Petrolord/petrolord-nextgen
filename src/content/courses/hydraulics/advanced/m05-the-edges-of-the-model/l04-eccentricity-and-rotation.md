# Eccentricity and rotation

Two geometric assumptions, both wrong in a real hole.

## The assumption of concentricity

The annulus is treated as two concentric circles: the pipe exactly in the middle of the hole.

In a vertical hole with stabilisers that is roughly true. In an inclined hole the pipe lies on the low side and the annulus is a crescent: a wide gap above and almost nothing below.

## What eccentricity does to the pressure

It LOWERS it. Flow takes the path of least resistance, which is the wide side, and a given flow rate through a wider effective channel costs less pressure.

The reduction can be twenty to forty percent of the annulus loss in a fully eccentric annulus, which is a very large error on the number that sets the equivalent circulating density.

And it is in the unsafe direction only if you assumed concentric and it is actually eccentric: the real pressure is LOWER, so the concentric calculation is conservative.

## What eccentricity does to the cleaning

It makes it much worse. The cuttings are on the low side and the low side has the least flow.

So eccentricity is conservative for the pressure and dangerously optimistic for the cleaning, and the two effects go in opposite directions.

## The assumption of no rotation

The pipe is stationary. In practice it is turning at 100 to 200 rpm while drilling.

Rotation adds a tangential velocity component, which raises the total shear rate, which thins the mud, which lowers the friction factor. It can also destabilise a laminar annulus into turbulence at a lower axial velocity, which raises it.

So rotation moves the annulus loss in either direction, and which one depends on the regime.

## What rotation does to the cleaning

It helps a great deal. A rotating pipe lying in a bed stirs it mechanically, which is the single most effective bed removal mechanism there is.

## The pattern across both

Both assumptions are conservative for the pressure and optimistic for the cleaning, except rotation, which is optimistic for neither in a simple way.

So a concentric non-rotating model overstates the equivalent circulating density a little and overstates the hole cleaning a lot.

## Why the engine does not model them

Because both need a substantially more complex flow solution: an eccentric annulus has no closed-form laminar solution and a rotating one is a three-dimensional problem.

Correlations exist for both and they are correlations, with the same status as the turbulent friction factor: fitted, published, and with a stated range.
