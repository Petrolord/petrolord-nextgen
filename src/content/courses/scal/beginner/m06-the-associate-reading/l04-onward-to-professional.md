# Onward to Professional

You can now take a rock's endpoints and curves, build the fractional flow function, construct the Welge tangent, and turn the result into the numbers a flood plan actually needs: how high the front, how soon the water, how much oil, how many days. That is a complete piece of reservoir engineering. If the Associate capstone is behind you, the skill is real.

It is also, deliberately, half of SCAL. Everything in this tier described fluids in motion. The other half of the subject describes them at rest, and that is where the Professional tier begins.

Here is what awaits, told as story rather than syllabus.

**The pressure between the fluids.** Water and oil in a pore do not meet at zero pressure difference. The curvature of every interface carries a pressure, it depends on saturation, and the laboratory can measure it. That one curve, capillary pressure against saturation, turns out to answer a question this tier could not even ask: why the water saturation in a reservoir varies with depth before anyone drills a well or injects a barrel.

**One curve hiding under many.** Measure capillary pressure on three plugs from the same sand and you get three curves that look nothing alike, because permeability, porosity, and the laboratory fluid pair all scale the measurement. In 1941 Leverett showed that a single dimensionless transformation collapses them onto one curve that belongs to the rock itself. You will run that collapse on three Ekene plugs measured in three different laboratory systems and watch it work. You will also learn what it looks like when it fails, and what a failure tells you about the data.

**A published example, end to end.** The tier walks a classic textbook worked example from lab table to reservoir curve, at full precision alongside the book's printed roundings. The difference between those two chains is small, but the tier makes a point of it, because knowing which chain produced a number is the difference between reproducing a result and merely resembling it.

**Saturation with height.** The payoff. Scale the rock's curve back to reservoir fluids, convert pressure to height, and the curve becomes a profile: how water saturation falls as you climb above the free water level. On Ekene this closes a loop you have been part of since the geoscience ladder, because the field's booked volumes assumed a single flat water saturation, and the profile will tell you exactly where in the column that assumption is honest.

None of it requires anything you do not already have. The algebra is lighter than the Welge construction. What it requires is the habit this tier has already built: derive the number, check it through an identity, and know which assumptions it stands on.

The tier closes the way this one did, with a synthesis, a capstone worked through the panel, and six graded numbers. The oil you learned to move in this course is still there, waiting. Professional teaches you where it sits.
