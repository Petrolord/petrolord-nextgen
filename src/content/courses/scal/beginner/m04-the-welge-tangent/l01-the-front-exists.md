# The front exists

Everything in the last module was smooth. The fractional flow curve rises from zero at connate water to one at residual oil without a single kink, corner or jump. So here is a fair question: if the mathematics is smooth, why does a waterflood arrive at a producer as an event? Wells do not water out gradually over years of gentle increase. They cut water within weeks of a distinct arrival, and the arrival is sharp enough that operators talk about breakthrough as a date.

The answer is one of the most satisfying results in reservoir engineering: the smooth curve itself manufactures a discontinuity. This lesson tells that story with no numbers at all, because the idea deserves to stand on its own before we measure it.

## Each saturation travels at its own speed

Buckley and Leverett showed in 1942 that in a one dimensional displacement, a given water saturation value moves through the rock at a speed proportional to the slope of the fractional flow curve at that saturation. Not the value of $f_w$, its derivative. Where the curve is steep, that saturation travels fast. Where the curve is flat, it crawls.

Look back at the S shape from the last module and read it as a speed chart. Near connate water the curve is nearly flat, so the lowest water saturations barely move. Through the middle the curve steepens, so intermediate saturations sprint. Near residual oil the curve flattens again, so the highest saturations dawdle at the back.

Now run the flood forward in your head. Intermediate saturations are fast, high saturations are slow, and both start from the injector at the same time. The fast intermediate values pull ahead. If you plotted the resulting saturation profile along the reservoir, you would find something absurd: the mathematics wants three different water saturations to occupy the same location at the same instant, the way a fast runner who started behind a slow one wants to be in two positions in the ordering at once.

## Nature resolves the overtake with a shock

A porous rock cannot hold three saturations at one point. The physical resolution is that the overtaking profile collapses into a jump: a sharp face, called the flood front or the shock, where saturation leaps from connate water ahead of it to some definite front saturation behind it. Ahead of the front, the rock has not yet seen injection water and sits at connate water saturation with oil flowing. Behind the front, water saturation ramps smoothly from the front value up toward the injector.

That single jump is the reason breakthrough is an event. The producer sees essentially no injection water until the day the front arrives, and then sees a lot of it at once. The smooth curve did not fail us. It predicted its own breakdown, and the breakdown is the physics.

The front is not a wall of water pushing oil like a piston. Water and oil flow together on both sides of it. It is a moving boundary between rock that has been swept to a mixed, flowing condition and rock that is still waiting. The piston picture is worth naming because the correction to it is the whole content of this module.

## The misconception to avoid

The tempting error is to think the front saturation must be the highest saturation injected, or that everything behind the front is at residual oil. Neither is true. The front carries one specific intermediate saturation, selected by the shape of the curve, and behind it lies a whole spectrum of saturations still pushing oil forward long after breakthrough. Floods recover a large share of their oil after the front has arrived. If the piston picture were right, breakthrough would be the end of the story. In real displacement it is closer to the middle.

Which saturation does the front carry? That is not a matter of opinion or measurement convention. The curve picks it, exactly, and the geometric construction that finds it is the subject of the next lesson.
