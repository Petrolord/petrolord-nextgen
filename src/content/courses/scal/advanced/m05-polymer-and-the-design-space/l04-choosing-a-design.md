# Choosing a design

You now hold every lever this course installs. This closing lesson of the module lines them up against each other, because design is not the study of levers one at a time; it is the ranking of levers against each other under a budget. The ranking for Ekene is stark, and the reasons generalize.

## The big lever: the viscosity ratio

The polymer case moved breakthrough displacement efficiency by about 0.068, from 0.5088773453049006 to 0.5771964898801638, by dropping the mobility ratio from 1.2 to 0.3. And the Associate tier's sensitivity table shows the same lever swinging the other way with brutal force: leave the water thin and let the oil be heavy instead, and at 5 cp oil the efficiency at breakthrough falls to 0.4299040270894154, at 10 cp to 0.3693282019820123. The spread across the viscosity axis, from heavy-oil worst case to polymer best case, is on the order of twenty saturation points of the column. Nothing else in the model comes within a factor of ten of that.

This is why the viscosity ratio earns the word "big." It multiplies the fractional flow ratio at every saturation simultaneously, so the whole curve, and with it the tangent geometry, moves together.

## The small levers: dip and rate

Module 4 priced gravity on this field. At the design rate, running the flood updip rather than flat improves breakthrough efficiency by roughly seven ten-thousandths, and cutting the rate to a quarter of design roughly quadruples that gain to a few thousandths, still forty times smaller than the polymer gain. Dip and rate are real physics and the sign discipline matters, a downdip flood gives the same few ten-thousandths back, but on a low-relief, moderate-permeability sand at commercial rates they are trim tabs, not rudders. The general lesson: gravity terms scale with $k \sin\alpha / q_t$, so they belong to steep, permeable reservoirs flooded slowly, and Ekene is none of those things.

## The walls: endpoints

$E_{Dmax}$ stayed at 0.6153846153846154 through every case in this course, because no lever in the displacement model touches $S_{wc}$ 0.35 or $S_{or}$ 0.25. The Associate table's one exception proves the rule: the only row that moved the ceiling was the one that changed $S_{or}$ itself. Chemistry that attacks residual oil, surfactants, miscible injectants, lives entirely outside this model and outside this course's locks. Within the model, endpoints are walls: you choose how to approach them, never where they stand.

## The recommendation, and its missing half

Assemble the Expert's one-paragraph recommendation for Ekene. The flood is already near-neutral at M 1.2, so it does not need rescuing; it works. Polymer at multiplier 4 is the only lever that materially improves it, worth about seven saturation points at breakthrough and a much cleaner water-cut profile; the screening warning says the real gain will be somewhat smaller and names the three studies to commission. Dip and rate scheduling are not worth operational compromise on this field. Residual-oil chemistry is out of scope.

What this paragraph deliberately cannot say is whether polymer is worth DOING, because that is a cost question: polymer price per barrel of incremental oil, injectivity risk, water handling savings from the delayed breakthrough. The Suite's Economics module owns that arithmetic, and the honest hand-off is to deliver it the two screening cases, base and polymer, as production profiles and let the cash flow decide. A displacement engineer who answers the economic question from the displacement model alone has exceeded their evidence.

## The misconception to avoid

The misconception is symmetry: assuming that because every lever appears in the same equation, every lever is worth studying equally hard. The fw machinery gives dip, rate, viscosity, and endpoints similar algebraic billing, and they differ in practical effect by two orders of magnitude on this field. Billing is not magnitude. Rank your levers on the field you actually have before allocating study budget, and expect the ranking itself to change field by field: on a steep high-permeability reservoir with heavy oil, the trim tab and the rudder can trade places.
