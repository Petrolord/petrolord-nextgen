# The crest just reaches connate

Every course on this field eventually meets the number 0.35. The volumetric booking used $S_w = 0.35$ in every one of its 169 oil cells; the displacement tier's kr curves start at $S_{wc} = 0.35$; the material balance history was built on the same figure. This lesson climbs to the top of the Ekene structure and finds out what that number actually is: not an average, not a typical value, but the saturation of the single highest point in the field.

## Climbing to the crest

The mapped crest of the structure sits $20.2818603515625$ m above the 1560 m contact, the maximum oil column the geoscience ladder booked. The Ekene sand's crest saturation is a capstone number, so climb it here on lesson 2's teaching sand (180 md, 18 percent porosity, the same plugs, fluids and column). Add its entry height and the crest stands

$$20.2818603515625 + 3.513961667746027 = 23.795822019308527 \ \text{m} = 78.07028221557916 \ \text{ft}$$

above the free water level. Run the chain from lesson 1 in reverse order: the capillary pressure there is $78.07028221557916 \times 0.07133527522935783 = 5.569165069081978$ psi, the dimensionless value is $J = 5.569165069081978 / 3.289624970000998 = 1.6929483208173388$, and the designed power law solves for saturation:

$$S_w = 0.25 + \frac{0.25}{1.6929483208173388} \times 0.75 = 0.3607535284417169$$

The crest of the teaching sand drains to $S_w = 0.3607535284417169$. Run the same chain on the Ekene sand's own factor and you find how close its crest comes to the booking's flat 0.35: that comparison is the point of this lesson, and the number is yours to produce.

## What that means for the flat model

Hold the two pictures side by side. The volumetric model paints 0.35 across the entire oil leg, top to bottom. The capillary model says 0.35 is the saturation the column achieves only at its highest point, where capillary pressure has had the most height to build. One metre below the crest the rock is slightly wetter; five metres down, wetter still; at the contact, fully wet. Every cell in the flat model except the crest itself is assigned a saturation drier than the physics allows it.

The direction of the error follows immediately, and it only takes a sentence: since 0.35 is the driest the column ever gets, a flat 0.35 overstates the oil fraction everywhere below the crest, and the true hydrocarbon volume in any one column is less than the flat model books for it. How much less is a quantitative question about averaging saturation over height, and it is the Expert tier's question, graded there; this tier's job is to see clearly why the sign of the correction cannot be anything but negative.

There is also a gentler reading, worth giving the original booking. On a structure where the column is short and the crest dominates the closure, using the crest saturation everywhere is a defensible first pass, and the NG5 booking was built before any capillary data existed in the story. The point is not that the booking was careless; it is that a saturation-height model tells you which single number a flat model has silently chosen, and here it chose the most optimistic one on the curve.

## The misconception to avoid

Do not flip the correction's sign. Hearing "there is a transition zone" some people conclude the field holds MORE oil than the flat booking, reasoning that the zone contains oil the sharp-contact model ignored. Wrong way round: the sharp-contact model already filled the whole leg with oil at crest dryness. The transition zone does not add oil below the contact; it adds WATER above it. The capillary correction to a crest-saturation flat model is always downward.
