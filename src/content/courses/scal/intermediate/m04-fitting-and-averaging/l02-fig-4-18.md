# Figure 4-18

Everything so far has run on curves this course designed, where the truth was planted and the machinery could be tested against it. This lesson fits the machinery to the curve that started the whole subject: the drainage J-function for unconsolidated sands published by Leverett in 1941, carried here as the reproduction in Ahmed's Reservoir Engineering Handbook, Figure 4-18.

The original is a plate, not a table. The platform's golden for it is therefore a set of figure reads: nine points lifted from the drainage branch, each carrying a stated read tolerance of 0.05 in J, because that is how finely a curve on a printed grid can honestly be read.

| $S_w$ | J (read) |
|---|---|
| 0.20 | 0.60 |
| 0.25 | 0.53 |
| 0.30 | 0.50 |
| 0.40 | 0.46 |
| 0.50 | 0.44 |
| 0.60 | 0.43 |
| 0.70 | 0.425 |
| 0.80 | 0.42 |
| 0.90 | 0.41 |

The curve asymptotes near $S_w \approx 0.09$, which is the read for the irreducible saturation, and flattens to a plateau just above $J = 0.4$.

## Fitting a plate

Hand the nine reads to `fitJPowerLaw` with $S_{wirr} = 0.09$ and it converges to

$$a = 0.3922841458642378, \qquad b = 0.17888262635639235$$

with a log-space $r^2$ of 0.9548556748366249. The largest absolute miss against any of the nine reads is 0.027530745172516413 in J, comfortably inside the 0.05 read tolerance. So the power law represents the published curve as well as the reads themselves deserve: the fit is not the bottleneck, the plate is.

Notice what a fit like this is for. Nobody needed the two parameters to know the curve; the nine reads already carry it. The parameters exist so the curve can travel: into a report, into a comparison, into the tabulated-or-power-law choice the engine offers downstream.

## Two curves, two shapes, one law

Put the fitted Leverett curve next to the Ekene design and read the exponents side by side.

The Ekene sand has $b = 1.0$: a genuine hyperbola, rising steadily as saturation falls, with meaningful capillary contrast across the whole mobile range. The unconsolidated Leverett sands have $b$ near 0.18: a shape that is almost flat across most of the saturation axis and only turns upward close to the irreducible end. Nearly all of that curve is plateau.

This is the point of the lesson. The Leverett claim, the one module 2 tested to machine precision, is that rocks of different permeability and porosity collapse onto one dimensionless curve. It is a claim about SCALING, not about shape. Which shape the collapsed curve has is a property of the rock family: its sorting, its pore-size distribution, its cementation. Unconsolidated well-sorted sand gives a flat plateau because most of its pores are the same size and drain over a narrow pressure interval. A rock with a broader pore-size distribution spreads its drainage over a wider pressure range and steepens the curve.

So the exponent $b$ is doing real geological work. It is a one-number summary of pore-size sorting, and two reservoirs with the same $b$ are making a stronger statement of kinship than two reservoirs with the same permeability.

## What the read tolerance disciplines

The 0.05 tolerance is not decoration; it decides what the golden can and cannot assert. It can assert that the engine's fitted curve passes within 0.05 of every read, and it does, with margin. It cannot assert the fourth decimal of $a$, because the underlying plate never carried that much information.

This is the same honesty the rest of the platform practices with published anchors. A printed table is typed as printed and matched at the printing's own precision. A figure is read at the grid's own resolution and matched at the read tolerance. The full-precision values the engine returns are recorded so the FIT is reproducible, but the CLAIM stops where the source stops.

## The misconception to avoid

The classic error is to treat Figure 4-18 as a universal correlation: look up J for your saturation, scale by your rock, and call the result your capillary pressure. Leverett himself never claimed that, and module 2's collapse test showed why it cannot be true: each rock family collapses to its OWN curve. The figure is the curve for clean unconsolidated sands. Use it on a cemented, poorly sorted reservoir and you are not applying a law, you are borrowing a different rock's pore geometry. The correct use of the figure is the one this module makes: as a published, machine-checkable exercise of the machinery, and as a shape benchmark to compare your own fitted $b$ against.
