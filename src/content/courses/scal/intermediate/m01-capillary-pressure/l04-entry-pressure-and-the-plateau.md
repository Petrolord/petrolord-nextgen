# Entry pressure and the plateau

A drainage curve has three regions, and each one answers a different engineering question. This lesson walks them on the Ekene reservoir rock, the 250 md, 20 percent porosity sand carrying the field's oil-brine system at $\sigma = 26$ dyn/cm and $\theta = 30$ degrees, whose curve the later modules will use for everything from fluid contacts to booked saturations.

## The entry pressure

Start at the wet end. At $S_w = 1.00$ the Ekene reservoir curve does not read zero. It reads

$$P_c = 0.7355825053402938 \ \text{psi}$$

That finite value at full water saturation is the entry pressure, sometimes called the displacement or threshold pressure. Physically it is the price of admission: the non-wetting phase cannot enter the rock at all until its pressure exceeds the water pressure by enough to squeeze through the largest throats the rock offers. Below that threshold, oil pressed against a water-full rock simply sits at the boundary. Nothing invades.

Notice that even a good sand posts a nonzero entry pressure. A quarter to three quarters of a psi sounds like nothing, and inside a flowing well it would be. But entry pressure is a static gate, and module 5 will show that this particular fraction of a psi, converted through the density contrast between oil and brine, is what separates two depths that engineers routinely conflate. The number is small; the confusion it prevents is not. That story needs the height conversion, so it waits.

## The plateau

Push past entry and the curve enters its working middle: saturation falls steadily while pressure climbs gently. A long, flat-ish plateau is the signature of a well-sorted rock, many throats of similar size being invaded one pressure class at a time. A steep, short middle marks poor sorting. The Ekene sand's plateau is gentle, which is consistent with everything the geoscience ladder found in this clean sand.

The plateau is where most of the pore volume changes hands, and its gentleness has a consequence worth naming now: where the plateau is flat, a small change in capillary pressure moves saturation a long way. Hold that thought until the transition zone lesson, where flat plateaus become thick transition zones.

## The wall

Keep pushing and the curve stops cooperating. As water retreats into pendular rings, grain contacts and the finest micro-porosity, each further decrement of saturation demands disproportionately more pressure, and the curve turns upward into a wall. On the Ekene design the wall stands at $S_{wirr} = 0.25$: the irreducible water saturation, the asymptote the drainage curve approaches and never crosses. No finite pressure dries the rock past it.

The lab tables respect the wall from a safe distance. All three Ekene plugs were measured on a saturation grid from 0.30 down to no lower, fifteen rows ending a full five saturation points above the asymptote, because chasing the wall costs exponentially increasing pressure for vanishing information.

Feel the wall's approach in the numbers. At $S_w = 0.30$, the reservoir curve reads 11.03373758010441 psi. Divide by the entry pressure:

$$\frac{11.03373758010441}{0.7355825053402938} = 15$$

Exactly fifteen, and the exactness is no accident: it is a property of the designed Ekene curve shape that module 2 will expose. From the wet end to $S_w = 0.30$ the curve climbs fifteen-fold, and the climb steepens without limit as saturation falls the last five points toward 0.25.

## Two waters, one rock

You have now met two special water saturations that are not the same number. The irreducible saturation of this curve is 0.25. The connate water saturation the displacement tier used, the $S_{wc}$ of the Ekene relative permeability set, is 0.35. This is not an inconsistency in the fixture, and it is emphatically not an error to hunt down. The capillary asymptote is what the rock can be drained to under unlimited pressure; the connate value in the kr set is what a particular part of the reservoir actually drained to under the pressures its position provided. Why the crest of a real structure sits at one number while the curve's wall stands at another is a question about height, and module 5 answers it properly. For now, keep the two numbers in separate pockets.

## The misconception to avoid

The misconception is that the oil first appears wherever the rock is, so to speak, willing, and that a good rock is willing everywhere, making entry pressure a curiosity of tight formations only. Every rock has a finite entry pressure, the Ekene sand included, and its consequence is structural: there is a band of rock that pressure differences have not yet qualified the oil to enter. Treating entry pressure as zero quietly erases that band, and with it the distinction between two contacts that module 5 will spend a full lesson keeping apart.

## Worked example

Read the Ekene reservoir curve at its two published ends and interpret each. At $S_w = 1.00$: 0.7355825053402938 psi, the entry pressure, the cost of the first throat. At $S_w = 0.30$: 11.03373758010441 psi, fifteen times entry, the cost of approaching the wall. An oil column would need to supply nearly eleven psi of capillary pressure at a point in the rock before the water there could be driven down to 30 percent, and the pressures available at modest heights above a contact are far smaller than that. The immediate, practical reading: most of a modest oil column cannot be anywhere near irreducible water. Quantifying that statement is precisely the business of module 5.
