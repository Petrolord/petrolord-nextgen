# Pressure

Temperature bent the brine a little. Pressure does something far more violent, and it does it to the gas. This lesson holds the temperature at 60 degC and the gas gravity at 0.6, which are the Ekene values, and moves the pressure alone.

## Gas at three pressures

| p (MPa) | rho (kg/m3) | K (MPa) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 10 | 69.5252 | 17.7074 |
| 25 | 172.6668 | 55.7187 |
| 40 | 237.8996 | 111.3929 |

The middle row is the Ekene gas. Pore pressure rises with depth, so the three rows stand for a shallow reservoir, the Ekene depth, and something considerably deeper. The gas itself is identical in all three. Same molecules, same gravity, same temperature. Only the squeeze changes.

Keep the unit in view. Gas bulk modulus is quoted in MPa throughout this course, because in GPa it would be a string of leading zeros. Brine and the mineral frame are quoted in GPa. Mixing those two habits up is the single most common error on the capstone, and this table is where the habit is formed.

## Both numbers climb steeply

The density column climbs from 69.5252 kg/m3 to 237.8996 kg/m3 across the table. A gas has no fixed volume of its own, so pressure packs the molecules closer and the mass per cubic metre rises accordingly. At 10 MPa the gas in the pore space is very close to weightless next to the brine at 1017.8249875 kg/m3. At 40 MPa it is not. A deep gas is a real mass, and it shows up in the density of the mixed pore fluid.

The stiffness column climbs harder. It goes from 17.7074 MPa to 55.7187 MPa to 111.3929 MPa, so the deep gas is several times stiffer than the shallow gas. The reason is the same packing. A gas resists compression only through the collisions of its molecules, and squeezing more molecules into the same space raises that resistance quickly. Gas is the one fluid in this course whose stiffness is mostly a statement about pressure rather than about chemistry.

Neither column is anywhere near the brine. Even at 40 MPa, the stiffest gas in the table is 111.3929 MPa against a brine bulk modulus of 2697.8113 MPa at the Ekene conditions. At 25 MPa the brine is 48.42 times stiffer than the gas. The gap narrows with depth and it never closes.

## Why the same gas is two different seismic targets

Put those numbers into what you already know about mixing. Wood's equation averages the compliances of the phases, so the softest phase in the pore space dominates the result, and the gas is always the softest phase present. What changes with depth is how soft.

A shallow gas sand carries a pore fluid built around a 17.7074 MPa gas. Against a brine sand next door at 2697.8113 MPa, the contrast in the pore fluid is enormous, and it survives the dilution that porosity applies when the fluid is placed inside a rock frame. This is the setting where gas produces the textbook bright spot, a strong soft reflection you can pick without much argument.

A deep gas sand carries a pore fluid built around a 111.3929 MPa gas. The gas is still by far the softest thing in the pore space, so the fluid is still soft, but the difference between the gas case and the brine case has shrunk, and the rock frame at depth is stiffer as well and hides more of what is left. The same accumulation that would have been unmissable at 10 MPa can be a subtle dimming at 40 MPa.

That is why experienced interpreters ask about depth before they get excited about an amplitude. It is not caution for its own sake. The fluid physics genuinely changes underneath them.

## What pressure to use

Two pressures are floating around any reservoir discussion and they are not the same thing.

Pore pressure is the pressure in the fluid itself, and it is the one that belongs in a fluid calculation. It is what the 25 MPa in this course means. It comes from formation pressure measurements, from mud weights, and from the pore pressure prediction work that has its own course in this module.

Effective stress is the overburden minus the pore pressure, and it is what the rock frame feels. It controls how stiff the dry frame is, and you will meet it properly at the Professional tier when the frame stops being a mineral mix and starts being a real rock with grain contacts. It does not belong in a Batzle and Wang fluid call.

A depleted reservoir separates the two clearly. Produce it and the pore pressure falls, so the fluids soften along the rows of the table above, while the effective stress rises and the frame stiffens. The two effects push the seismic response in opposite directions, which is one reason time lapse work is harder than it looks.

## Exercise

Read the pressures 10, 25 and 40 MPa as shallow, Ekene depth and deep. Write one sentence on what happens to a gas sand's contrast against a nearby brine sand as you go down that list, and name the two engine numbers you would quote to support it. Then say which pressure belongs in a Batzle and Wang fluid calculation.

As a self check: the contrast falls as you go deeper, because the gas bulk modulus rises from 17.7074 MPa at 10 MPa to 55.7187 MPa at 25 MPa and 111.3929 MPa at 40 MPa, while the brine it is being compared against stays near 2697.8113 MPa, so the gap between the two closes with depth. The gas density rises over the same range from 69.5252 to 237.8996 kg/m3, so the deep gas also stops being a negligible mass in the mixed pore fluid. The pressure that belongs in the fluid calculation is the pore pressure, because it is the pressure the fluid itself is under. Effective stress is what the rock frame feels, and it belongs to the frame calculation at the tier above.
