# What that means for a prospect

The last three lessons are a rock physics result. This one turns it into what a prospect team actually decides, because that is where the tier's value lands.

## The chain of inference

A prospect team looking at a bright amplitude reasons in a chain, and the chain has a weak link that this tier can locate.

The amplitude is bright, therefore the impedance contrast is large, therefore the reservoir is soft and light, therefore it holds hydrocarbon rather than brine, therefore the prospect is charged, therefore drill it.

Every step of that is defensible except the strength of the third, and the tier has now quantified it. The reservoir being soft and light is consistent with hydrocarbon and also with higher porosity, and if it is hydrocarbon, the amount is not determined.

## What the tier lets you say

Three statements, in decreasing order of confidence.

That the modelled brine case gives an impedance of 7.200e6 and the modelled gas case 5.924e6, so a 17.7 percent contrast is what a full gas column should produce. This is the strongest statement the tier makes and it is the one worth putting in the summary.

That the same 17.7 percent, or nearly it, would be produced by a few percent of residual gas, so the amplitude does not discriminate saturation.

And that a porosity uncertainty of plus or minus 0.05 moves the predicted gas velocity by more than 300 m/s, which is larger than the entire oil to gas separation, so the amplitude does not discriminate fluid type either.

## The two errors this prevents

The first is over-reading an anomaly. A team that treats a bright amplitude as a saturation measurement will book a volume it cannot support, and the rock physics that produced the prediction will be cited as the authority for it.

The second is under-reading a dim one. A prospect with a modelled contrast of only a few percent, where the frame is stiff or the porosity low, will produce a weak amplitude whether or not it is charged, and the absence of a bright spot is then not evidence of absence. The Biot coefficient from module two is what tells you which regime you are in: at 0.80 the Ekene sand is fluid sensitive, and a tight cemented rock at 0.4 would not be.

That second error is the more expensive one in mature basins, where the obvious bright prospects are drilled first and what remains is the rock that does not shout.

## What to do next

The tier's own answer is to stop asking the amplitude for things it cannot give and to go and get them elsewhere.

For fluid type: a flat spot, pressure data, or offset well control.

For saturation: accept that it is not determinable from seismic and carry a range, letting the volumetric study handle it as an uncertainty rather than a number. The reservoir course upstairs is built to do exactly that.

For the rock: improve the porosity, because it is the input worth five times what the mineral model is worth and it is the input a petrophysicist can actually improve.

## Worked example

Write the rock physics paragraph for the Ekene prospect as it would appear in a prospect summary.

The Ekene sand is modelled from a logged brine point at 3200 m/s, 1800 m/s and 2250 kg/m3 with a porosity of 0.25 and a mineral modulus of 37 GPa. Inverse Gassmann gives a dry frame of 7.35 GPa and a Biot coefficient of 0.80, so the rock is strongly fluid sensitive.

A full gas substitution predicts 2905.7 m/s and 2038.7 kg/m3, an impedance fall of 17.7 percent, with a shear velocity rising to 1891.0 m/s and the velocity ratio falling from 1.778 to 1.537. An oil case predicts an impedance fall of 8.5 percent.

The prediction is dominated by the assumed porosity: a range of plus or minus 0.05 moves the gas velocity from 2710 to 3034 m/s, which exceeds the separation between the oil and gas cases. The amplitude should therefore be treated as evidence of hydrocarbon presence rather than of fluid type or saturation, and a five percent residual gas saturation would produce 77 percent of the full velocity response.

## Exercise

A team asks for a rock physics prediction of the saturation in a prospect from its amplitude, so that a volume can be booked. Draft the two sentence reply.

Self check: the amplitude cannot support a saturation estimate, because a five percent gas saturation produces about 77 percent of the full velocity response and the remaining 95 percent of the gas moves the velocity by only a further 0.33 percent. What the modelling can supply is the expected contrast for a full column, 17.7 percent in impedance here, and the statement that the observed anomaly is or is not consistent with it, leaving saturation to be carried as a range by the volumetric study.
