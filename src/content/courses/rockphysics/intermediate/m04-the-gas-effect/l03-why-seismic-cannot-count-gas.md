# Why seismic cannot count gas

The last two lessons showed that the velocity response saturates within a few percent of gas and then turns around. This lesson states the consequence plainly, because it is the single most commercially important fact in rock physics.

## The claim

A seismic amplitude anomaly caused by gas tells you that gas is present. It does not tell you how much.

## The evidence, from this rock

At five percent gas the velocity is 2915.26 m/s. At one hundred percent gas it is 2905.70 m/s. Those differ by 9.6 m/s, which is 0.33 percent.

Any measurement that cannot resolve 0.33 percent in velocity cannot distinguish a rock that is 5 percent gas from one that is entirely gas. Seismic cannot resolve anything like that, once wavelet, noise, processing and the uncertainty in porosity and frame are accounted for.

The impedance is slightly better behaved because the density keeps falling: 6,528,547 at five percent gas against 5,923,875 at full gas, a separation of 9.26 percent. That is measurable in principle. It is not measurable in practice against a porosity uncertainty that moves the prediction by more than that on its own.

## Fizz gas

The name for the problem is fizz gas: a small, non-commercial gas saturation that produces nearly the full seismic anomaly.

Residual gas saturations of a few percent are common. They occur where a trap once held gas and leaked, where gas has come out of solution near a fault, and where a small charge arrived and stopped. None of those is worth drilling and all of them look, on amplitude alone, much like a full gas column.

This is not a theoretical risk. It has been drilled many times and it is one of the standard causes of a dry hole on a bright amplitude.

## What does distinguish them

Three things, none of which is amplitude on its own.

The AVO response carries a little more information than the stacked amplitude, because the gradient depends on the shear velocity, and the shear velocity is a density reading that keeps changing all the way to pure gas. That is the Expert tier's subject, and it helps, without solving the problem.

A fluid contact visible on the seismic is much stronger evidence. A flat spot is a reflection from the contact itself, and a contact implies a column rather than a smear of residual gas.

And the geometry of the anomaly matters: an amplitude conforming to structure, with its base at a consistent depth across the prospect, is evidence of a column, while a patchy anomaly that ignores structure is not.

## The honest statement

A rock physics study that has done its job produces a statement of this shape.

The observed amplitude is consistent with a gas saturated sand at the modelled porosity. It is equally consistent with a residual gas saturation above about five percent. The modelling cannot distinguish these cases, and the distinction should be made from the conformance of the anomaly to structure, the presence or absence of a flat spot, and any available pressure data.

That is a more useful deliverable than a predicted saturation, because a predicted saturation from amplitude is not a real quantity.

## Worked example

Work out what a saturation estimate from velocity would actually be worth on this rock.

Suppose a survey delivers an interval velocity of 2870 m/s with an uncertainty of plus or minus 50 m/s, which is optimistic.

Read the curve. Velocities near 2870 occur at a water saturation around 0.85 and again around 0.15, on opposite sides of the minimum. Widening to plus or minus 50 m/s takes in almost the entire range from Sw 0.9 down to Sw 0.

So the saturation estimate is somewhere between 10 percent gas and 100 percent gas, which is another way of saying there is no estimate. The reservoir volume implied by those two ends differs by a factor of ten.

## Exercise

A prospect shows a strong amplitude anomaly conforming to structure, with a clear flat spot at the mapped spill point. State what can and cannot be concluded about saturation.

Self check: the conformance and the flat spot are good evidence of a genuine hydrocarbon column down to the contact rather than a residual saturation, since residual gas does not produce a contact reflection and does not conform to a spill point. What still cannot be concluded from the amplitude is the saturation within that column, nor whether the fluid is gas or oil, since a full gas case and a five percent gas case differ by 0.33 percent in velocity and oil and gas differ by only about 10 percent in impedance.
