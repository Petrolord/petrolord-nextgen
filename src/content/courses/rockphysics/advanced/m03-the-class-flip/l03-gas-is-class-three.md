# Gas is class three

The gas case at Ekene is class III, and unlike the brine case that call is robust. This lesson works through it and says why the two calls are not equally firm.

## The numbers

$$A = -0.06282494068620303, \qquad B = -0.2565633444602355$$

The intercept is below -0.02, so the class is decided by the sign of the gradient, and the gradient is negative. Class III.

## What class III means on a gather

A negative reflection at all offsets, a trough on standard polarity, that gets stronger as offset increases.

That is the response interpreters look for and the one that produces the classic bright spot. It stacks well, because every offset contributes with the same sign, so a class III interface is bright on a stacked section as well as on a gather.

At Ekene the reflection runs from -0.0628 at zero degrees to -0.1602 at 40 degrees, so the far offsets are about two and a half times the near.

## Why the call is robust

Two independent margins.

The intercept is -0.0628, which is more than three times the default threshold of 0.02. Widening the class II band all the way to 0.05 leaves the case comfortably outside it.

The gradient is -0.2566, and it would have to reach zero to move the case to class IV. The previous module showed what that takes: a shear velocity error of over 20 percent.

Compare the brine case, whose intercept sits 1.72 times above the threshold and moves to class II when the band widens to 0.04. One case is a convention away from a different name and the other is not.

## What the class does not tell you

That the sand holds gas.

Class III is a statement about the elastic contrast at an interface. Anything that makes the lower halfspace much softer and lighter than the upper one produces it, and gas is one such thing among several.

A poorly consolidated brine sand under a hard shale can be class III. A coal, a volcanic ash, an overpressured shale: all can produce a large negative intercept with a negative gradient.

That is why the tier below's saturation lesson matters here. The class flip is strong evidence of a fluid effect at a known interface where the wet case has been modelled and is class I. It is much weaker evidence on its own, without the wet case for comparison.

## Worked example

Work out how far the gas intercept would have to move to change the class, and what would have to be wrong to move it that far.

To reach class II the intercept would have to rise from -0.06282 to above -0.02, a change of 0.043.

The intercept is dominated by the density term, at -0.0916 against the velocity term's +0.0288. Raising the intercept by 0.043 means raising the density half by about that much, which means the gas case density would need to rise from 2038.71 to about 2222 kg/m3.

From the density bookkeeping, that would need a porosity of about 0.033 rather than 0.25, which is not a reservoir. Alternatively it would need the pore fluid to be far denser than gas, which is to say not gas.

So the class III call for this rock survives any plausible error in its inputs, and that is a useful thing to be able to say in a report.

## Exercise

State two things other than gas that could produce a class III response at a shale over sand interface.

Self check: an unconsolidated brine sand with a low frame stiffness and low density relative to a hard shale would do it, as would a genuinely different lithology such as a coal or an ash, which are both much softer and lighter than a shale. Both produce the same elastic contrast the class describes, which is why the class flip between a modelled wet case and a modelled gas case is stronger evidence than the class of the observed response alone.
