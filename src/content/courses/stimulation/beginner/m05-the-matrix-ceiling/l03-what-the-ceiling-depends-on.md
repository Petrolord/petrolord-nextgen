# What the ceiling depends on

Five things set the rate, and on the day of the job you control exactly one of them.

{{panel:st-acid-explorer}}

## The five terms

Permeability and thickness sit in the numerator as a product. Doubling either doubles the ceiling. In the published case they are 1 mD and 100 m, and they are the reservoir's to give. You can choose which interval to open, which changes the thickness and matters a great deal, but you choose it in the completion design rather than on the pump truck.

The pressure margin is the difference between the fracturing pressure of 38131950.890444934 Pa and the reservoir pressure of 23730685.440133728 Pa. Closure belongs to the earth stress. Reservoir pressure drifts downward with depletion, which quietly WIDENS the margin and raises the ceiling on an old well. Neither number moves on the day.

Viscosity sits in the denominator and is the one term the engineer owns. Acid is close to water, so the ceiling as computed is a near best case. Gel it, foam it, or add a viscous diverter, and the ceiling falls in direct proportion.

The logarithm of the radius ratio is geometry, and it is weak. The published values are a drainage radius of 300 m and a wellbore radius of 0.108 m. Logarithms flatten large changes, which is why an underreamed hole buys far less injectivity than its size suggests.

Skin is the fifth term. It is the subject of the next lesson.

## Yours, or the reservoir's

| term | who sets it |
| --- | --- |
| permeability | the reservoir |
| thickness | the completion, chosen before the job |
| pressure margin | the earth stress and depletion |
| viscosity | you, on the day |
| radius ratio | geometry, and it barely matters |
| skin | the damage now, and the acid later |

## How weak a logarithm really is

Module 2 already showed this on the damage radius. Taking the damaged radius from 0.9 m to 3 m is more than a tripling, and the skin only rises from 8.481054144800364 to 13.296945362104108.

The radius ratio in the rate equation behaves the same way. That is the useful lesson: in radial flow, distances matter through their logarithm, so large geometric changes buy small hydraulic ones. Permeability, thickness and viscosity are linear, and they are where the leverage is.
