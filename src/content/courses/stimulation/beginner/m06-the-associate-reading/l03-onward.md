## The fracture, properly

This tier treated the fracture as a boundary, a pressure you must stay under. The Professional tier crosses it deliberately and asks what the rock does next.

**Net pressure** is the first idea. The pressure inside a growing fracture above the closure stress is what holds it open, and it is small compared with the pressures involved. In the published case, closure sits at 38131950.890444934 Pa and the PKN net pressure is 2889735.9944400033 Pa. A few percent of the total decides the whole geometry, which is why fracture design is so sensitive to inputs that look secondary.

**Two dimensional width models** come next. PKN and KGD make opposite assumptions about which direction the fracture is confined in, and they disagree by a lot. On the published case the PKN average width is 0.004015981871358954 m and the KGD average width is 0.0104637691458403 m, a ratio of 2.6055319672793997 at a half length of 150 m. Choosing between them is a judgement about height containment, not a numerical preference, and the tier teaches how to make it.

**The material balance** decides how long the job takes. Fluid pumped either stays in the fracture or leaks off into the rock, and leakoff wins more often than newcomers expect. In the published case the fluid efficiency is 0.1728566723633056, meaning most of what is pumped never stays in the fracture at all. That single number sets the injection time, the pad fraction and therefore the pump schedule.

## Further on

Beyond that sits the proppant: what holds the fracture open after the pumps stop, how much conductivity survives the closure stress, and the dimensionless conductivity that decides whether a long thin fracture or a short fat one is worth more. That is where the tier ends up, and it is a genuinely surprising result.

## Elsewhere in the academy

Geomechanics supplies the stress profile that sets closure and decides whether the fracture stays in zone. Petrophysics supplies the permeability, porosity and thickness this course consumes.
