# The story so far

Five modules, and one region of rock about a metre thick.

## One idea, not five

Everything in this tier happens in the near wellbore. The published case has a wellbore radius of 0.108 m, damage out to 0.9 m, an acid front planned to 0.6 m, and a carbonate wormhole radius of 0.391324751 m. The drainage radius is 300 m.

So the entire tier is an argument about the first metre of rock, inside a reservoir a thousand times wider. That is not a limitation of the engine. It is where the pressure drop concentrates in radial flow, and it is why a thin damaged ring can cost more than a large volume of mediocre reservoir.

Once you accept that, the tier reduces to a single question. Do you clear the near wellbore, or do you go around it? Acid clears it. A fracture goes around it. Everything else is detail about which of those two you can afford.

## What each module put in place

Module 1 drew the line between the two answers and said what the engine models and what it hands back to the laboratory.

Module 2 gave damage a number. Hawkins turns a permeability RATIO and a damaged RADIUS into a skin. Skin is linear in the ratio and logarithmic in the radius, so how bad the damage is matters far more than how far it reaches, and a measured skin is a sum over every near wellbore effect rather than a clean reading of damage alone.

Module 3 took the acid route. Sandstone and carbonate are different jobs. Sandstone is planned volumetrically as pore volumes of a treated annulus, so the volume grows with the SQUARE of the target radius, and the published job stops at 0.6 m against damage at 0.9 m and therefore leaves a residual skin of 1.621860432.

Module 4 took carbonates. A wormhole is an instability, not uniform dissolution. Its radius comes from a volumetric balance with a laboratory calibrated pore volumes to breakthrough, and the return on pumped volume diminishes: 8 m3 buys a skin of -1.287406553, and four times that volume, 32 m3, only reaches -1.9511423998762085.

Module 5 put a ceiling on the whole thing. Steady radial Darcy sets the fastest a matrix job can go, and skin sits in its denominator, so the damaged well is the slow one.

## What holds it together

Two shapes recur. Radius enters through a logarithm, so geometry gives poor returns. Volume enters through an area, so it gives expensive ones. Every judgement in this tier is one of those two curves.

## What you can claim

That you can turn a damage description into a skin, plan an acid volume for a stated front, read the residual skin honestly, run the carbonate alternative, and put a defensible upper bound on the pump rate.

Not yet a fracture. That is the Professional tier.
