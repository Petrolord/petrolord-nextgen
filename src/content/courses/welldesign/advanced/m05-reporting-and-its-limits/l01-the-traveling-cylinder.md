# The traveling cylinder

The plot that shows where the neighbour is, from the driller's point of view.

## The idea

Stand at a station on the reference well, facing along the hole. Look around. Where is the offset well?

The traveling cylinder plot answers exactly that. It is a polar plot centred on the reference well, with the angle around it measured from high side, and the radius the distance to the offset.

As you travel down the reference well, the offset appears to move around the plot, and the track it traces is the picture.

## What it shows that a ladder does not

**Direction.** A separation factor is a magnitude. The cylinder shows which way the neighbour is, which is what a directional driller needs in order to steer away from it.

**Approach geometry.** A neighbour that stays at the same bearing while the distance shrinks is a parallel well closing in. One that sweeps around the plot is crossing. Two very different situations with similar minimum factors.

**Multiple offsets at once.** Every neighbour can be drawn on the same plot, which is how a congested platform section is actually looked at.

## The two bearings

The engine returns two directions at every reference station.

**The horizontal bearing** to the offset: a compass direction, which is what a plan view shows.

**The toolface bearing**: the direction to the offset measured around the reference hole from high side, which is what the cylinder plot uses and what a driller sets a toolface against.

The second is the operational one. If the offset is at a toolface bearing of 90 degrees, steering with a toolface of 270 moves directly away from it.

## Reading a track

**A point that stays put and gets closer** is a parallel well. The mitigation is to change inclination or azimuth to open the gap, and the toolface bearing says which way.

**A point that sweeps from one side to the other** is a crossing. The closest approach is at the sweep, and it is usually brief.

**A point that spirals** is a well being crossed at a shallow angle, which is the most dangerous geometry because the wells stay close for a long interval.

## Where the scale is

The radius is usually plotted logarithmically or with a compressed outer region, because the interesting range spans from a few metres to hundreds.

Some presentations plot the separation FACTOR as the radius instead of the distance, which puts the no-go threshold at a fixed circle. That is a defensible variant and it changes what the plot is showing: distance is geometry, factor is geometry over uncertainty.

## Its limitation

It is a projection onto a plane perpendicular to the reference well at one station.

An offset that is nearly parallel and slightly above appears at a small radius and a high-side bearing, which is correct, and the plot gives no sense of how long it stays there. The ladder does that.

The two plots are complementary and a report should carry both.

## The misconception to avoid

"The traveling cylinder shows the offset well's path." It shows the offset's position relative to the reference at each reference station, in the reference's own rotating frame. A straight offset well traces a curve on it, and a curved one can trace a straight line. The plot is about the relationship, not about either well.
