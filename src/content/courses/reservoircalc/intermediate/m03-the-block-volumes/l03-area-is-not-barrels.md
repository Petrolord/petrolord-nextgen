# Area is not barrels

The west block holds 69.2 percent of the oil bearing cells and 81.2 percent of the barrels. Those two percentages describe the same partition of the same field and they differ by twelve points. This lesson is about that gap, which is the most useful single reading the block panel offers.

## The two ratios

Count the cells: 117 west against 52 east, a ratio of exactly 2.25 to 1.

Count the barrels: 9.855617 against 2.283591 MMstb, a ratio of 4.3158 to 1.

The barrels are almost twice as lopsided as the area. Since the chain applies the same four constants to both blocks, nothing below gross rock volume can be responsible. The whole gap is in the rock volume, and rock volume is area multiplied by column.

## Where the gap comes from

The mean oil column is 15.452865 m in the west and 8.056122 m in the east, a ratio of 1.918 to 1.

Multiply the two ratios together:

$$2.25 \times 1.918 = 4.316$$

which is the barrel ratio. That identity is exact and it is the whole explanation. A block's volume is its area times its mean column, so the volume ratio between two blocks is the product of their area ratio and their mean column ratio.

The west block wins twice. It has more cells, and the cells it has are thicker.

## Why the columns differ

The structure at Ekene shallows toward the west and north, and the mapped crest at 1539.72 m sits in the western half of the field. Oil column at any cell is the distance from the top surface down to the contact or the base, whichever comes first, so the shallower the top, the taller the column.

The west block therefore contains the tall part of the accumulation by construction. The east block is the flank: a rim of cells where the top has fallen most of the way to the contact, so the columns are short and the cells are pale on the map.

This is the ordinary case rather than a peculiarity of Ekene. A fault that cuts across a structure almost always gives one block the crest, and the block with the crest holds a share of the volume larger than its share of the area.

## Reading it off the panel

The two share tiles sit side by side at the bottom right for this reason.

Step the fault from 1000 m to 2200 m and watch them. At every position the share of barrels in the west exceeds the share of cells in the west, and the gap between them is widest in the middle of the range, where the fault is cutting through the flank of the structure.

At a fault of 1000 m the west holds 10.7 percent of the cells and 9.4 percent of the barrels. That is the exception that proves the rule: with the fault that far west, the west block is a small corner of the flank, so it holds thin cells and the gap runs the other way.

## Why it matters for a decision

A field report that gives compartment areas without compartment volumes is worse than useless, because area is the intuitive quantity and it is systematically misleading about where the oil is.

The specific failure runs like this. Somebody looks at a compartment map, sees that the eastern block covers nearly a third of the field, and reasons that a well there is worth roughly a third of the field's production. The correct number is under a fifth, because the eastern cells are thin.

The reverse error is just as easy. A small compartment sitting on the crest can hold a share of volume far above its share of area, and dismissing it on area alone leaves a good well undrilled.

## Worked example

Estimate the east block's barrels from the west block's, using only the two mean columns and the two cell counts, then check against the engine.

The west books 9.855617 MMstb on 117 cells at 15.452865 m of mean column. Barrels per cell metre are therefore

$$\frac{9.855617}{117 \times 15.452865} = 0.005452 \ \mathrm{MMstb \ per \ cell \ metre}$$

The east has 52 cells at 8.056122 m, so it should hold $0.005452 \times 52 \times 8.056122 = 2.2841$ MMstb.

The engine gives 2.283591 MMstb, which agrees to four significant figures, the residue being the rounding in the intermediate figures. The conversion factor from cell metres to barrels is the same in both blocks because the properties are the same in both blocks, which is exactly the assumption the Expert tier removes.

## Exercise

Two compartments hold 400 and 100 oil bearing cells on the same grid, and book 30 and 12 MMstb. Compute the ratio of their mean oil columns, and say which compartment you would drill first if a well costs the same in either.

Self check: the volume ratio is 2.5 to 1 and the area ratio is 4 to 1, so the mean column ratio is $2.5 / 4 = 0.625$; the smaller compartment has columns 1.6 times taller than the larger one. On volume alone you would drill the larger, but the smaller one holds 12 MMstb in a quarter of the area with taller columns, which usually means better wells, so the answer depends on rate as well as volume and the area figures alone would have hidden the question.
