# The crest belongs to one block

A partition divides more than volume. It divides the features of the structure, and the most consequential of those is the crest. This lesson looks at what each block got.

## The two maxima

The tallest oil column in the west block is 20.2818603515625 m. The tallest in the east block is 16.5079345703125 m.

The western figure is the field maximum, which the Associate tier booked. So the partition did not merely give the west block more oil. It gave it the single best cell in the field, and the east block's best cell is 19 percent shorter.

## Why the crest matters more than its volume

One cell of 100 m by 100 m is a small part of a field. The crest matters because of what it implies about everything around it and about what a well there would do.

The crestal cells are where the oil column is tallest, so a well drilled there penetrates the most oil bearing rock, which usually means the highest production rate and the longest plateau. They are also the last part of the accumulation to be reached by rising water during production, so a crestal well produces dry oil for longer.

For appraisal the crest carries a different weight again. It is the part of the map where being wrong is most expensive, because a crest that turns out to be lower than mapped removes oil from every cell around it.

## A crest the west block did not earn

There is a caveat here that the Associate tier established and that the partition makes sharper.

The mapped crest at 1539.72 m is 1.2819 m shallower than the shallowest top pick in the field, which is 1541 m at Ekene-3. No well found rock that shallow. The crest is spline overshoot: an artefact of the interpolator, which fits a smooth surface through the control points and can overshoot beyond them.

The west block's maximum oil column of 20.2818603515625 m is measured down from that overshoot. So the block's best cell is a cell whose height above the contact is partly an interpolation artefact, and the partition has now attached that artefact to one owner's booking rather than to the field's.

That is worth stating plainly in any report of a partitioned field. The block that holds the crest holds whatever uncertainty the crest carries, and it is usually the block whose number is quoted most confidently.

## Worked example

Compare the two blocks on three measures at once at the capstone settings.

The west has 117 cells, a mean column of 15.452865 m and a maximum of 20.281860 m. The ratio of its maximum to its mean is 1.31.

The east has 52 cells, a mean of 8.056122 m and a maximum of 16.507935 m. Its ratio of maximum to mean is 2.05.

The east block is much less uniform. Most of its cells are thin, and it has a small area of taller column near the fault around Ekene-6 pulling its maximum up. The west block is more evenly thick.

That difference has a development consequence. A single well in the east block placed anywhere other than that small thick area finds a fraction of the column its block's maximum suggests, whereas a well almost anywhere in the west block finds something close to the western mean. Compartment averages hide that, and the maximum against mean ratio is a cheap way to reveal it.

## Exercise

Set the panel's fault to 1500 m and record both blocks' mean and maximum columns. Then state which block you would drill first, and what you would want to know before committing.

Self check: at 1500 m the east block holds the crest and the larger volume, so it is the first target on volume alone. Before committing you would want to know how much of that crest is real rather than interpolated, since the field maximum is measured from a mapped crest 1.2819 m shallower than any well pick, and a crestal well is exactly where that overshoot would cost the most.
