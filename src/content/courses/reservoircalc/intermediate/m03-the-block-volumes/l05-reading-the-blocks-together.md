# Reading the blocks together

Two columns of numbers invite two separate readings, and separate readings miss most of what a partition tells you. This lesson is about the comparisons worth making across the columns rather than down them.

## Four comparisons

At the capstone settings the four readings worth taking are these.

The first is the sum against the field. The blocks must reproduce the total, which they do: 117 and 52 cells give 169, and 9.855617 and 2.283591 MMstb give 12.139208. That is a check, not a finding.

The second is the share of barrels against the share of cells: 81.2 percent against 69.2 percent. Whenever these differ, the blocks hold different shapes of rock, and the gap measures how differently.

The third is each block's mean column: 15.452865 m against 8.056122 m. This is the explanation for the second comparison and it is the reading that transfers best to a development discussion, because a mean column is close to what a well will find.

The fourth is each block's maximum against its own mean: 1.31 in the west, 2.05 in the east. This measures how uniform each block is, and it warns you when a block's average is unrepresentative of most of its area.

## The reading in one paragraph

Put those together and the Ekene partition at 1800 m reads like this.

The field's 12.139208 MMstb divides into a large, thick, fairly uniform western compartment holding 9.855617 MMstb over 1.17 square kilometres, and a small, thin, uneven eastern compartment holding 2.283591 MMstb over 0.52 square kilometres. The western compartment contains the crest and all three of its wells found oil. The eastern compartment is a flank rim whose volume is concentrated near the fault, and two of its three wells are dry at this contact.

That paragraph is the deliverable of this tier. It is what somebody planning wells actually needs, and none of it is visible in a single field total.

## What a decision maker will ask next

Three questions follow from that paragraph, and it is worth knowing which of them this tier can answer.

Can the eastern compartment carry a well? This tier gives the volume, 2.283591 MMstb, and the mean column, 8.06 m, which are two of the three inputs. The third is economics, which belongs to another course.

How confident are we in the split? This tier can answer that, and module two already did: a hundred metres of uncertainty in the fault position is worth about 0.9 MMstb, and the tie break convention on the boundary column is worth the same again.

Do the two compartments share a contact? This tier can compute the consequences of any answer, and module five does. It cannot supply the answer, which comes from pressure and fluid data.

## Worked example

Here is the same reading applied to a different fault position, so that the method is clearly separable from the answer.

Set the fault to 2000 m. The cells split 142 west and 27 east; the barrels split 11.444229 and 0.694979 MMstb.

Sum check: 169 cells, 12.139208 MMstb. Good.

Shares: the west holds 84.0 percent of the cells and 94.3 percent of the barrels. The gap has widened from twelve points to ten, and the east block is now a marginal sliver.

Mean columns: the west block's mean has fallen slightly, because it has absorbed thin flank cells, while the east block's mean has fallen sharply, because the cells it kept are the thinnest in the field.

The paragraph now reads: almost the whole field is one compartment, with a residual eastern sliver of 0.694979 MMstb that is very unlikely to justify a well of its own. That is a different development story from the one at 1800 m, and it came from moving a line 200 m.

## Exercise

Write the one paragraph summary for a fault at 1400 m, using the panel. Include the sum check, both shares, both mean columns, and one sentence on what it implies for wells.

Self check: at 1400 m the cells split 65 west and 104 east and the barrels 5.193290 and 6.945918 MMstb, which sum correctly to 169 and 12.139208. The west holds 38.5 percent of the cells and 42.8 percent of the barrels, so the shares are close and the blocks are similar in shape. Both compartments are large enough to be developed, which makes this the fault position at which the partition matters most to the well count, since neither block can be dismissed.
