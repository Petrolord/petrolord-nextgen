# What a sealing fault does

A fault is a surface in the rock across which the two sides have moved relative to each other. Whether it matters to a volumetric estimate depends entirely on whether fluids can cross it, and that is a separate question from whether the rock is broken.

## Sealing and non sealing

Faults come in both kinds and the same fault can be both, in different places and at different times.

A fault seals when something across the fault plane stops flow. Most often that is juxtaposition: the displacement has moved reservoir sand against shale, so a fluid trying to cross the fault meets a rock it cannot flow through. It can also be the fault itself: shale smeared along the fault plane during movement, or cement precipitated in the fault zone, can leave a low permeability skin only centimetres thick that behaves as a barrier for millions of years.

A fault fails to seal when reservoir is juxtaposed against reservoir and nothing has clogged the fault plane. Then the fault is a feature of the structure and not a barrier at all, and the accumulation on both sides is one tank.

Nothing in a volumetric calculation can tell you which of these you have. The evidence comes from elsewhere: pressure measurements taken on both sides, fluid contacts that differ across the fault, production interference tests, or the geometry of the juxtaposition worked out from the displacement.

## What sealing changes and what it does not

If a fault seals, three things change and one thing does not.

The volume in place does not change. The rock is where it was, the pore space is where it was, and the oil is where it was. This point is worth insisting on because the arithmetic will make it obvious later and it is still the part people get wrong when reading a report. Partitioning a field does not create or destroy barrels.

What changes first is drainage. Oil in a sealed compartment can only reach a well drilled into that compartment. A well in the west block does not produce the east block, no matter how long it is left on production and no matter how good the well is.

What changes second is pressure. Compartments deplete independently. Produce the west block and its pressure falls while the east block sits at its original pressure. That is a diagnostic as well as a nuisance, because a pressure difference measured across a fault is one of the cleanest pieces of evidence that the fault seals.

What changes third is the contact. Two compartments that have never been in fluid communication have no reason to share an oil water contact. They were charged separately, they may hold different fluids, and their contacts are set by their own spill points and their own charge histories.

## What it means for a booking

Put those together and a compartmentalised field is a set of small fields that happen to be adjacent.

Each compartment needs its own well or wells to be produced at all. Each carries its own development cost. Each has to be large enough to pay for the well it needs, and a compartment that is not large enough is oil that will stay in the ground however carefully it was mapped.

That is why the Professional tier exists. The Associate booking of 12.139208 MMstb is a true statement about the rock. It is not, on its own, a statement about a development, and a reader who takes it as one will assume a single well or a single drainage plan can reach all of it.

## Worked example

Consider a simplified version of the situation this tier will actually compute. A field books 12 MMstb. Somebody proposes one well, which for the sake of argument can drain the whole field if the field is one tank.

Now suppose a sealing fault splits the field 80 to 20. The well is drilled into the larger compartment. The booking has not changed and the recoverable oil under this plan has fallen by a fifth, because the smaller compartment has no well in it.

Somebody then asks whether a second well is justified for the smaller compartment. That question is now the whole development decision, and answering it needs the compartment's own volume, not the field's. A field total cannot answer it. This is the practical reason a partition is worth the trouble of computing.

## Where the Ekene fault comes from

For this tier the fault is given: it runs north to south at an easting of 1800 m and it seals. That is a teaching simplification and it is worth being explicit about what it hides.

In real work the fault would come from seismic interpretation, and it would be a surface rather than a vertical plane, with a dip, a displacement that varies along it, and ends where it dies out into unbroken rock. Whether it seals would be argued from juxtaposition and pressures rather than assumed. Its position would carry uncertainty of tens of metres at best.

None of that changes the arithmetic of partitioning, which is why this tier can proceed with a vertical plane at a known easting. All of it changes how much confidence the partition deserves, which module four returns to.

## Exercise

A field has been produced for two years from a single well. A second well is drilled 3 km away, across a mapped fault, and comes in at the original reservoir pressure. State what that observation tells you about the fault, and what it tells you about the volume booked before the second well was drilled.

Self check: original pressure across the fault after two years of production on the other side is strong evidence that the fault seals, because a connected tank would have depleted throughout. The booked volume itself is unaffected, since the rock and its oil have not moved. What is affected is the recovery expected from the original well, which was implicitly draining a tank that turns out to be smaller than the field.
