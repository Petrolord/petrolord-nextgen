# Deep offshore and frontier under the Act

{{panel:pia-hct-calculator}}

The hydrocarbon tax has two terrains carved out of it by the Act itself. This lesson reads the carve-out, sets it beside the rates the engine returns, and shows what a deep offshore or frontier field still pays.

## The text

The Petroleum Industry Act 2021, s.260(3): "(3) This Part shall not apply to a frontier acreage until it is reclassified under section 68 (3) of this Act and to deep offshore."

Two terrains, two different conditions. Frontier acreage is outside the Part until it is reclassified under s.68(3). Deep offshore is outside it with no condition in the Act. The Nigeria Tax Act 2025 keeps the frontier carve-out in s.65(4): "(4) This Part shall not apply to a frontier acreage until it is reclassified under section 68 (3) of the Petroleum Industry Act."

Deep offshore in a year under the Nigeria Tax Act 2025 is a different question. That Act brings deep offshore into its hydrocarbon tax Part, and the rate it applies is one of the three open readings of this course. The Expert tier takes it up; everything in this lesson stays in years under the Act alone for deep offshore.

## What these fields still pay

The carve-out removes one instrument. Royalty and companies income tax remain.

Frontier acreage pays a flat 7.5 percent production royalty and no royalty by price, since para 11(2) says "(2) There shall be no royalty by price for frontier acreages." The Ekene frontier case (synthetic, 2026, oil at 120 USD/bbl) returns a liquids royalty rate of 0.075000 and a royalty by price of 0.000000.

The Ekene deep offshore lease (synthetic, 60,000 bopd) pays a liquids royalty rate of 0.054167. In 2025, a year under the Act alone, the engine still computes its hydrocarbon tax base, a chargeable profit of 795646660.199557, and charges it at zero. Its companies income tax that year is 291253998.059867.

That pattern is worth holding onto. A zero hydrocarbon tax on these terrains comes from the rate. The base, the deductions and the allowances are all still computed, so a later change of rate has a base to act on.
