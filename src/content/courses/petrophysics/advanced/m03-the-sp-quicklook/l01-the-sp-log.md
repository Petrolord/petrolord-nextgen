# The SP log

The spontaneous potential log is the oldest electrical measurement still in routine use, and it is unusual among logs in that the tool does not emit anything. It simply records a natural DC voltage between an electrode moving up the borehole and a reference electrode at surface. The rock and the drilling mud generate that voltage themselves, and the way they generate it is what makes the SP a source of formation water resistivity.

## Where the voltage comes from

When a well is drilled with a water-based mud, the mud filtrate invades permeable beds and comes into contact with the formation water already there. Two electrolytes of different salinity now touch, and wherever that happens, small electrochemical voltages appear. Two components dominate.

The first is the membrane potential. Shales behave as ion-selective membranes: their clay surfaces carry a negative charge, so they let cations such as Na+ pass while blocking anions such as Cl-. Where a shale separates the salty formation water from the fresher mud column, cations migrate through the shale from the saltier side toward the fresher side, and a voltage builds across it. This is the larger of the two components.

The second is the liquid-junction potential. At the boundary between invaded zone and virgin formation, filtrate and formation water are in direct contact with no membrane between them. Ions diffuse from the saltier fluid into the fresher one, but Cl- is more mobile than Na+, so the charge separates slightly and another, smaller voltage appears at the junction.

There is also an electrokinetic or streaming component, generated by filtrate physically flowing through the mudcake under the pressure differential. In normal drilling conditions it is small, and the quicklook chain this module builds deliberately ignores it. What remains is a purely electrochemical signal, and that is the signal that carries salinity information.

## Polarity and the shale baseline

Both electrochemical components depend on the salinity contrast between mud filtrate and formation water, and both reverse if the contrast reverses. The practical intuition to fix in your mind is this: when the mud filtrate is fresher than the formation water, which is the common case and gives $R_{mf} > R_w$, the SP deflects in the NEGATIVE direction against clean permeable beds, relative to the reading in shale.

Against shale, the SP holds a fairly steady value called the shale baseline. The curve has no absolute calibration; only deflections from that baseline mean anything. Opposite a clean, permeable, water-bearing sand the curve swings left (negative) by an amount set by the salinity contrast, then returns to the baseline in the next shale. A saltier-than-formation filtrate flips the deflection positive, and a filtrate that matches the formation water produces almost no deflection at all.

## Why this yields Rw

The size of the deflection is not decorative. Because the electrochemical voltages are set by the ratio of the two water salinities, the full deflection in a thick clean bed is, to a good approximation, proportional to the logarithm of the resistivity ratio of the two fluids. Salinity and resistivity are two faces of the same property, so a measured deflection plus a known mud filtrate resistivity pins down the formation water resistivity. That is the entire strategy of this module: read the deflection, know $R_{mfe}$ from the mud report, solve for $R_{we}$.

The next lessons make each step precise: what exactly to read (the static SP), the temperature coefficient that converts millivolts into a resistivity ratio, and the rearranged equation that delivers $R_{we}$. On the typewell brief, this route lands within 0.0001 ohm.m of the Arps-corrected laboratory sample, which is the kind of independent agreement the Expert tier is built around.

## Worked example

No equation yet, but calibrate your polarity instinct on three cases:

1. Filtrate fresher than formation water ($R_{mf} > R_w$): cations flow through the shale membrane toward the fresh side; the SP deflects negative against the clean sand. This is the typewell geometry.
2. Filtrate and formation water equally saline ($R_{mf} \approx R_w$): no contrast, no driving force, the curve barely leaves the shale baseline, and the SP is useless for Rw work.
3. Filtrate saltier than formation water ($R_{mf} < R_w$): the driving force reverses and the deflection is positive. The equation still works; only the sign changes.

## Exercise

A well is drilled with fresh mud. Opposite sand A the SP deflects 90 mV negative from the shale baseline; opposite sand B, at similar depth and thickness, it deflects only 15 mV negative. Both sands are clean and water bearing. State which sand holds the saltier formation water, and why. As a self-check: sand A. A larger negative deflection means a larger salinity contrast against the same filtrate, so sand A's water is saltier (lower $R_w$) than sand B's. If a third sand showed a small POSITIVE deflection, its formation water would have to be fresher than the mud filtrate.
