# The three-tier workflow

You have now climbed the whole petrophysics ladder: the Associate loop, the Professional's method choices, and the Expert's parameter work. This closing module steps back and shows the three tiers as what they always were, one workflow seen at increasing depth. Nothing you learned at a lower tier was discarded on the way up. Each tier kept the machinery of the one below it and converted one more assumption into evidence.

## What the Associate tier established

The Associate course built the core loop: shale volume from gamma ray, porosity from the density log, water saturation from Archie, cutoffs, and zone summaries. Every parameter arrived as a given: the clean and clay anchors, the matrix and fluid densities, and above all $R_w = 0.05$ ohm.m. The loop itself is permanent. Whatever else changes on the way up the ladder, the final act of any evaluation is still this loop: curves in, flagged samples out, zone summary booked.

At that tier the typewell's SAND_A booked 18.0 m of net pay with a pay-average water saturation of 0.361. Hold on to those numbers; they are about to reappear.

## What the Professional tier added

The Professional course made the method choices explicit and made you defend them. Porosity stopped being a single number from a single tool: you computed it from density, from the Wyllie and Raymer-Hunt-Gardner sonic transforms, and from the neutron-density combination, and you booked one with a stated reason. Saturation stopped assuming clean rock: Simandoux and Indonesia handled the conductive clay that Archie cannot see. And one parameter moved from given to fitted: the Pickett plot in the water leg returned $a R_w = 0.0500$ ohm.m and $m = 2.000$ from a six-point least-squares fit, confirming the Associate givens from the data itself.

## What the Expert tier earned

This course took the last step: the parameters themselves are now earned rather than accepted. $R_w$ arrived by three independent routes. The laboratory sample, corrected to formation temperature with Arps, gave 0.049910 ohm.m. The SP quicklook gave 0.049831 ohm.m. The Pickett fit, carried up from the Professional tier, gave 0.0500 ohm.m. Three routes, three different physical measurements, one answer within 0.4 percent. The adopted value was then validated where it must hold, in the water leg, where the mean Archie saturation came back at 0.9991, indistinguishable from the 1.0 a correct $R_w$ predicts. Finally the value was stress-tested in the booking: with the triangulated $R_w$, SAND_A books 18.0 m at $S_w$ 0.3609; with the raw uncorrected sample it books 16.5 m, and the difference is pure parameter error.

## One assumption at a time

Read down the ladder and the unifying principle is plain:

* Associate: run the loop correctly with given parameters.
* Professional: choose methods deliberately and validate what the data can validate.
* Expert: earn the parameters, prove them where the answer is known, and measure what they do to the booking.

Each tier converts one more assumption into evidence. The typewell's own numbers tell the story in a single line: a given $R_w$ of 0.05 became a fitted $a R_w$ of 0.0500, then a triangulated 0.0499 to 0.0500 from three routes, and at every stage the honest booking for SAND_A stayed at 18.0 m net and a water saturation near 0.361. The answer did not change because the given happened to be right. The point of the ladder is that you now know it was right, and you can show why.

## Why the order matters

The ladder runs in this order because each tier's question only makes sense once the one below is settled. There is no use triangulating $R_w$ if you cannot run the loop that consumes it, and no use comparing saturation models if the porosity feeding them is a single unexamined number. When you evaluate a new well in practice you will run all three tiers in one sitting, usually in an afternoon, but you will run them in this order: loop, methods, parameters, and then the booking with its sensitivities.
