# How much exposure it takes to say anything

{{panel:ss-intervals-explorer}}

With zero events, how many hours does a crew need before its central 95 percent upper limit comes down to a target rate? Each row below was derived and then checked by calling the engine at those hours:

| target per 200,000 | hours needed, derived | engine upper limit at those hours |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 2.000000 | 368887.945411 | 2.000000 |
| 1.000000 | 737775.890823 | 1.000000 |
| 0.500000 | 1475551.781646 | 0.500000 |
| 0.250000 | 2951103.563291 | 0.250000 |

Every check returns the target exactly. The arithmetic and the engine agree.

## The arithmetic

At zero events the central 95 percent count upper limit is 3.688879454114, whatever the hours. The rate upper limit is that count limit times 200,000 over the hours. Turn that around, and the hours needed to bring the limit down to a target are the count upper limit times 200,000 over the target. For a target of 1.000000 that gives 737775.890823 hours.

The relationship is simple and unforgiving. Halve the target and the hours double. A crew that wants its clean record to rule out a rate of 0.250000 per 200,000 hours needs 2951103.563291 hours without a single recordable, which is four times the hours needed to rule out 1.000000.

## Setting the ABO crew against the table

The ABO crew worked 41300 hours, and its upper limit sits at 17.863823. To bring that limit down to 2.000000 it would need 368887.945411 hours with no recordable, roughly nine times what it has. A clean record on the ABO hours says the crew's rate is probably not enormous. It says little more than that.

UGHELLI, for scale, logged 2318640 hours in a year. Even a site of that size, with a year of zero recordables, would fall short of the hours needed to rule out 0.250000 per 200,000 hours at 95 percent. Zero events carries information in proportion to the hours behind it, and a small workforce simply cannot collect enough hours in a year to make a zero say much.

## What this means for targets

A target that says zero recordables this year is a statement about a count. What it can prove about the rate depends entirely on the hours, and the table shows how many are needed before a zero means anything close to a low rate. For a small crew, a year of zero is good news worth reporting with its upper limit and too little evidence on its own to call the crew's performance excellent.

This is the other face of the IMO ladder. There, a fixed rate grew more certain as the events accumulated. Here, the events never arrive, and certainty can only come from the hours.

## Pooling helps zero too

Sum then divide applies here as well. Several crews that each log a short clean year can be pooled into one exposure, and the pooled zero carries the pooled hours. That is legitimate when the crews do comparable work, and it is the Associate rule applied to a count of zero.
