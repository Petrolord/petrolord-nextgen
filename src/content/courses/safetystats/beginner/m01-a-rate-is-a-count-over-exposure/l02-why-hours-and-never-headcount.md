# Why hours and never headcount

{{panel:ss-rates-explorer}}

Two crews of 40 people each had one recordable case apiece in the same year. Counted per head they are identical: 2.500000 recordables per 100 people for both. Counted per hour of exposure they are different, and the engine reads the day crew at 2.500000 and the rotation crew at 1.717033 per 200,000 hours.

| crew | hours | recordables | rate per 200,000 hours | recordables per 100 people, derived |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| day crew | 80000 | 1 | 2.500000 | 2.500000 |
| rotation crew | 116480 | 1 | 1.717033 | 2.500000 |

The difference comes entirely from the hours. The day crew worked 80000 hours and the rotation crew worked 116480. The same 40 people on a longer rotation spend more time exposed to the work, so the same single event is spread over more hours and the rate is lower.

## The ratio is the hours

Divide the day crew's rate by the rotation crew's rate and the answer is 1.456000. Divide the rotation crew's hours by the day crew's hours and the answer is 1.456000 again. That is no coincidence. With the count held equal, the only thing left to move the rate is the exposure, and the two rates stand in exactly the inverse ratio of the hours.

A per head figure hides that. A reader cannot tell that one group was on site for far more hours, and so had far more opportunity for something to go wrong. A per hour figure puts that opportunity into the denominator.

## Why the engine has no headcount input

The engine takes hours and has no headcount input at all. That is deliberate. A headcount is a snapshot: it counts people on a list without saying how long any of them worked. Part time staff, rotation patterns, overtime, a crew that left in the third month and a crew that joined in the ninth all look the same to a headcount. Hours carry all of that information in one number.

The base of 200,000 hours does describe people, which is where confusion starts, and a later lesson in this module takes it apart. The hours in the denominator are the actual exposure of the actual workforce.

## Reading a per head figure in the wild

When a report gives an injury figure per employee, or per 100 workers, ask two questions before comparing it with anything. First, what hours did those workers actually put in? Second, were contractors counted in the people and left out of the events, or the other way round? The first question is the one this lesson answers. The second is a trap a later tier takes up in full, and it has the same root: the events and the exposure must come from the same workforce.

A rate built on hours can be compared with another rate built on hours on the same base. A figure built on heads can only be compared with another figure built on heads with the same working pattern, and that almost never holds.

## Exercise

Using the table, multiply 1 recordable by 200,000 and divide by 116480 hours, and check that you reach the engine's 1.717033. Then divide 116480 by 80000 and confirm it equals the 1.456000 ratio of the two rates. Finally, write one sentence a site manager could read that explains why two crews with identical headcounts and identical event counts have different rates.
