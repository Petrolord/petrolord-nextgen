## The mistake, restated

The mean of rates gives every unit the same weight, whatever its exposure. On KWALE the jetty carried 61480 hours, a share of 0.024805 of the pooled hours, and in the mean of rates it carried a weight of one third. Its site rate of 3.253090 pulled the mean far above the rate of the workforce as a whole. The pooled rate is the rate of the whole workforce; the mean of rates weights a jetty crew like a flow station.

## One level up

A group that averages its sites' rates makes the same mistake at a larger scale. So does a corporate dashboard that averages its business units, or an industry body that averages its members' rates. Small units with high rates, thin months, short-lived projects and minor sites all carry a full vote in a mean. The result can sit anywhere, and on every stream in the table it sits above the pooled figure.

IOGP itself pools. Its five-year FAR is the sum of fatalities over the sum of hours, and the engine reproduces its 0.826095. The mean of 0.833228 is the figure IOGP would print if it averaged its own yearly rates, and it does not. The gap there is small because no year's hours are a tiny fraction of another's. The gap on KWALE is large because the jetty's hours are a small fraction of the flow station's.

## Why a mean still gets used

The mean of rates is easy. Each unit reports one number, and the head office averages them without ever collecting hours. That is exactly why the error spreads. A group that wants the pooled rate has to collect the counts and the hours from every unit and add them, which is more work and needs the denominators to be defined the same way everywhere.

The engine makes the choice for you and shows its work. Its pooled and rolling rates are sum then divide, and it returns the mean of the period rates beside them, labelled, so a reader can see the gap. Its note reads: "meanOfPeriodRates is the unweighted mean of the per-period rates over periods with hours; it is NOT the pooled rate".
