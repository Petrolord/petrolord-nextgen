# Reclassification lowers a rate and leaves the harm

{{panel:ss-rates-explorer}}

AMUKPE's site recorded 13 recordables in 2238950 hours, a rate of 1.161259 per 200,000 hours. Move 2 of them to first aid and the count is 11, the rate 0.982603. The 58 days lost do not move, so the severity rate on the same hours is 5.181000 before the reclassification and 5.181000 after it.

| what | before | after |
| --- | --- | --- |
| recordables | 13 | 11 |
| recordable rate per 200,000 | 1.161259 | 0.982603 |
| days lost | 58 | 58 |
| severity rate per 200,000 | 5.181000 | 5.181000 |

## What moved and what did not

The recordable rate fell. The hours did not change, the work did not change and the days lost did not change. The only thing that changed was which box two cases were put in. A rate that falls while the harm does not is a change in counting.

The severity rate is the tell here. It counts days lost times the base over the hours, and the days lost belong to the injured people whatever the case is called. When the recordable rate falls and the severity rate holds still, the two figures together say the injuries are the same and the labels are different.

## When reclassification is right

Reclassification is not always wrong. A case first logged as a medical treatment case may, on review, have needed only first aid, and correcting the record is part of keeping a good register. The definitions are real and the edges are fiddly.

The trap is the pattern. Reviews that only ever move cases down, reviews that happen near the end of a reporting year, or reviews that cluster at sites with a target to hit: each is a reason to read the recordable rate with suspicion. The question for any reclassification is whether the same review, applied to cases on the other side of the line, would ever move one up.

## The engine counts what it is given

The engine checks that a count is a whole number and that the hours are above zero. It cannot check whether an injury was recordable, whether a restricted day was really a lost day, or whether a case belongs in first aid. The count arrives classified. Its refusals catch a fractional count, a negative count and zero hours. Nothing in it refuses a count that was classified wrongly.

That makes the analyst the only check. The note that reports a recordable rate should also report any reclassifications in the period, how many cases moved and in which direction. A reader who sees a fall of 2 cases alongside 2 cases reclassified to first aid knows exactly how to read it.

## Pair a count with a harm

The practical defence is to report two figures that respond differently to a change in labels. A recordable rate and a severity rate on the same hours, on the same base, do that. So does reporting the days lost beside the case count. When one moves and the other does not, the note has to explain why.
