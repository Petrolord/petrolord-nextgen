# Same base and same definition first

UGHELLI's recordable rate is 0.776317 per 200,000 hours. The IOGP TRIR for 2024 is 0.809223 per 1,000,000 hours. Set side by side, UGHELLI looks slightly better than the industry. On the IOGP base UGHELLI reads 3.881586, which is 4.796684 times the IOGP figure.

## The factor of five

The Associate tier taught that OSHA TRIR and IOGP TRIR share three letters and differ by a factor of five. The million-hour figure over the OSHA figure is 5.000000, because the count and the hours stay put and only the base moves. This is the reason the engine takes no default base. A comparison across the two bases is off by exactly that factor, and it runs in the flattering direction for whoever quotes the smaller base.

UGHELLI on 200,000 hours against IOGP on 1,000,000 hours is the trap in its purest form. The honest comparison puts both on one base first. Once that is done the story reverses: UGHELLI's 9 recordables in 2318640 hours read 3.881586 per million hours against IOGP's 0.809223.

## The definition has to match too

A shared base is necessary and still leaves a question. IOGP's TRIR counts fatalities, lost workday cases, restricted workday cases and medical treatment cases, per million hours. A company's own "recordable" may be drawn differently. It may leave out restricted work cases, or count contractors differently, or follow a regulator's definition that differs from IOGP's at the edges.

The engine cannot help here. Its incidence rate takes a count that arrives classified, and the name printed beside the number, whether TRIR, LTIF or DART rate, is not something the engine checks. The case class is the caller's count. UGHELLI recorded 0 fatalities, so on that one class its count and IOGP's cannot disagree. The other three case classes are where a mismatch would hide. So the definition check is a reading exercise: put the company's definition beside IOGP's and list every difference before any number is compared.

## A checklist before any benchmark

Before a site rate is set beside an industry figure, three things have to be the same.

The base. Both rates on 200,000 hours or both on 1,000,000.

The definition. The same case classes counted, and the same treatment of fatalities.

The workforce. Company and contractor hours and events both in, or both out, as the traps module will show.

Only when all three match does the ratio mean anything. On UGHELLI, the base check alone turned an apparent result into its opposite.
