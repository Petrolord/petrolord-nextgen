# The inch of water, and where the conversion lives

The differential you measure is in inches of water column. The equation wants a
pressure in psi. Something has to convert between them, and where that something
lives decides whether you can check it.

## A constant you cannot see

In this module the conversion is a module-private constant inside metering.js.
It is not exported, so no screen prints it and no caller can pass a different
one. That is a reasonable engineering decision and it has a consequence: you
cannot read the number off the result. A figure you cannot read is a figure you
cannot argue with, so the only honest way to state it is to make the engine tell
you.

## Measuring a constant by asking the engine

The method is worth learning because it works on any constant a module hides.
Run the engine twice, at two sets of inputs that share nothing else, and divide
the differential it returns in psi by the differential you gave it in inches of
water.

   run one: 63.800000 in H2O returned 2.304922 psi, so the factor is 0.0361273
   run two: 17.900000 in H2O returned 0.646679 psi, so the factor is 0.0361273

The two agree exactly, which is what makes this a constant rather than a
coincidence. One run would have given you a ratio. Two runs at different
differentials giving the same ratio to every digit printed is evidence that the
engine is applying a fixed factor and is doing nothing else on the way.

## Why this matters more than it looks

Inches of water column is a height of a fluid, and a height of a fluid is a
pressure only once you have fixed which fluid and at what temperature. Water at
one reference temperature and water at another do not weigh the same, and the
published conversion factors differ in the fourth significant figure because of
it. A transmitter calibrated on one reference and an equation coded to another
disagree by a little, permanently, on every flow the run ever reports.

Nothing about that is visible on the result sheet. The engine returned 2.304922
psi for 63.800000 in H2O and gave no reason. So the useful habit is the one this
lesson just performed. When a conversion is buried, measure it, write down what
you measured, and keep it beside the calibration sheet for the transmitter. If
the two are on the same reference you have a documented match. If they are not,
you have found a bias in every ticket the meter has produced, and you found it
with two runs and a division.

## Exercise

Run one and run two share no input other than the fluid model. Say what you
