# What you may quote, and what you may never quote

The last lesson of this tier is about sourcing. Everything above rests on it, and it is the part that transfers most directly to work outside this course, because every engineer eventually has to defend where a number came from.

## What the engine returns is quotable

A figure the engine returns, at the precision it is printed, is a figure you can put in a document. This course prints its classes at fixed precisions on purpose: shell thicknesses, ratios and percentages to six decimals, and volumes in barrels, vent rates in scfh and annual losses to four, because a number in the thousands carries no information in its millionths. Quote at that precision. Rounding further looks tidy and moves a figure outside the tolerance the course sets around it.

## A message is quoted exactly

A refusal, a warning or a note is quoted verbatim, inside backticks, as the engine's own words. This file pins fifty engine strings verbatim and each was asserted to contain a fragment of its own text when the file was built, so a reworded engine message fails the build rather than quietly teaching wording a learner will never see on the screen.

Some of those messages are phrased in a way a house style guide would change. They are still quoted unchanged, because the words a learner meets on the screen are the words worth learning.

## A relationship is quoted, never derived

This is the rule that costs the most and saves the most. Where this course states that one figure is a multiple, a fraction or a difference of another, that comparison was computed and printed as a relation carrying both values, their difference and their ratio. Where no relation exists, the two figures are printed and nothing is said about the gap between them.

Your own arithmetic over two published figures is not a source, however obvious it looks. It is easy to subtract two numbers correctly and still be wrong about what the difference means, because they may not be on the same basis, at the same condition or in the same units.

## What is never a source

A comment in the source code of an engine is not a source. It sits next to the code, it is usually well written, and it often describes what the code did at some earlier time. The same is true of a repair note, a review document or an internal record of what somebody found while working on the software. Those tell you about the history of a package. They do not tell you what it returns today, and only what it returns today is teaching truth.

The test is simple. Can you make the package produce this figure, right now, by calling it. If yes, quote it. If no, say where it really comes from, or do not write it at all.

## Exercise

Take any figure you have quoted from this tier and name the digest section and row it came from. Then say which of the three rules above you would be breaking if you wrote a sentence comparing it with a figure from a different section.
