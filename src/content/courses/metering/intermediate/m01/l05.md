# The critical pressure ratio factor

The allowable drop is built out of two things: how much pressure the valve recovers downstream of its vena contracta, and how far the pressure at that vena contracta can fall before the liquid starts to vaporise. The second of those is what the liquid critical pressure ratio factor carries, and it is written FF.

## What it does

FF sets how much of the difference between the inlet pressure and the vapour pressure the valve is able to use. It is a property of the liquid rather than of the valve, and it is computed from the vapour pressure and the critical pressure of the fluid being handled.

The engine returns three figures worth holding on to. At the BELEMA vapour and critical pressures FF is 0.892161. As the vapour pressure approaches zero FF is 0.960000. At the critical point FF is 0.680000.

## Reading those three figures

The first is the working value for the valve this tier marches. It is the number sitting inside the allowable drop of 179.220032 psi that every choked row of that march uses.

The second and the third are the ends of the range. A liquid a long way below its critical pressure, which covers cold hydrocarbon and water service, sits near the value the engine returns as the vapour pressure approaches zero. A liquid being handled close to its critical pressure sits near the value the engine returns at the critical point. Light hydrocarbons at high temperature are where this matters.

## Why it belongs in a course about boundaries

FF is one of the few places in valve sizing where a number that looks like a fudge factor turns out to be a physical statement. It says that the liquid cannot have its pressure pulled all the way down to the vapour pressure before it flashes, because vaporisation begins at a fraction of that value under the conditions inside a valve throat.

That fraction is what moves the choking boundary. Change the fluid and keep the valve, and the outlet pressure at which the flow chokes moves, because the allowable drop moves with FF. It is the reason a valve that behaved perfectly on one service misbehaves on another that looks similar on the datasheet, and it is the reason the vapour pressure is an input this engine refuses to work without.
