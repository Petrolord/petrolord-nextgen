# Skin is not damage

Six things that produce a positive skin, and only one of them is drilling damage.

## Why this matters

The skin factor is a residual. It is whatever pressure drop the ideal radial solution failed to predict, collected into one number. Anything that makes the well harder to flow into than a fully penetrating, undamaged, vertical, Darcy-flowing cylinder lands in it.

So the diagnosis, which is what gets acted on, is a separate act from the measurement.

## Partial penetration

A well completed across 20 ft of a 45 ft sand still drains the whole sand once radial flow is established, but the flow has to converge vertically to reach the perforations. That convergence costs pressure, it is constant once established, and it therefore looks exactly like skin.

The magnitude depends on the completed fraction and on the vertical to horizontal permeability ratio. For a well completed across half the interval in rock with a vertical permeability a tenth of horizontal, the pseudoskin is easily plus 5 or more.

An acid job does nothing about it, because there is nothing wrong with the rock.

## Deviation

A well that crosses the interval at an angle presents more sandface than a vertical one and gives a NEGATIVE pseudoskin, typically between minus 1 and minus 3 for moderate deviations, more for high angles.

That is worth knowing because it can mask real damage. A deviated well with plus 4 of genuine damage and minus 3 of geometric benefit reports plus 1 and looks fine.

## Perforations

Perforation geometry, density, phasing and depth all contribute. Perforations that do not penetrate past the crushed and invaded zone leave the well flowing through the damage; more and deeper perforations reduce the skin without changing the reservoir at all.

This is a real component and it is the one most often fixed by re-perforating rather than acidising.

## Non-Darcy flow

At high velocity, near the wellbore, the pressure drop stops being proportional to rate. In gas wells this is routine and in high-rate oil wells it happens too.

Because it is rate-dependent, it appears in a single-rate test as part of the skin and cannot be separated from the rest of it. The apparent skin is S + D q, where D is the non-Darcy coefficient, and separating the two needs tests at more than one rate. The engine's gas analysis returns exactly this apparent skin and says so, and the Expert tier's final module explains why the course grades nothing about D.

## Phase effects

If the flowing pressure at the well falls below the bubble point, gas comes out of solution near the wellbore, the relative permeability to oil falls, and the well develops a positive skin that has nothing to do with the completion. It changes when the drawdown changes, and it goes away if the well is choked back.

The same thing happens with condensate banking in gas condensate wells, where it can be very large.

## Actual damage

Mud filtrate invasion, clay swelling, fines migration, scale, asphaltene deposition, and the crushed zone around perforations. This is the one an acid job addresses, and in a report it should be argued for rather than assumed.

## How to tell them apart

Not from the pressure transient. The transient gives one number and all of these sum into it.

The separation comes from knowing the well: the completed interval against the net pay, the deviation survey, the perforation record, the flowing pressure against the bubble point, and whether the skin changes with rate. Those are all documents, not measurements, and gathering them is part of a test interpretation rather than an optional extra.

## The misconception to avoid

"The test says the skin is plus 6.5, so the well needs stimulating." The test says the residual pressure drop is equivalent to a skin of plus 6.5. What produced it is a question the test cannot answer. Wells have been acidised repeatedly, at real cost, for skins that were partial penetration all along.
