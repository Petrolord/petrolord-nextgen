# Superposition, properly

One rate change was the Associate tier. Real wells have dozens.

## The general form

The diffusivity equation is linear, so responses to rate changes add. For a history of rate steps, each starting at its own time,

    dp(t) = (dp_per_pd / q_ref) * sum over steps j of (q_j - q_{j-1}) * pwD(t - t_j)

Each step contributes the constant-rate dimensionless response, evaluated at the time elapsed SINCE THAT STEP, weighted by the CHANGE in rate at that step.

Steps that have not happened yet contribute nothing. A step where the rate did not change contributes nothing, which is why the engine's `rateStepsFromHistory` collapses repeated rates.

## The engine's implementation

`superposeDeltaP` takes four things: a function giving the dimensionless response at an elapsed time in hours, the rate steps, the evaluation time, and the pressure per unit dimensionless pressure per unit rate.

It walks the steps in order, stops at the first one that has not started yet, accumulates the rate change times the response at the elapsed time, and multiplies by the conversion at the end.

That is the whole of it, and it is worth noticing what it does NOT contain: nothing about buildups, nothing about shut-ins, nothing about test types. A shut-in is just a step to zero rate. The machinery is the same.

## Why this is the honest way to build a fixture

The three-rate case in this module is generated by exactly this function, applied to the homogeneous model at the planted permeability, skin and storage.

That matters for the course's claim to reproducibility. The data are not asserted; they are produced by the engine, from parameters stated in the lesson, using a function whose implementation you can read. And then the SAME engine's multi-rate analysis is run on them and asked to get the parameters back.

Engine generates, engine recovers, and the gap between the two is the teaching result.

## Flow periods and their types

`detectFlowPeriods` turns a rate history into a list of periods with a start, an end, a rate and a type: flow for positive rate, shut-in for zero, injection for negative.

The classification is trivial and the utility is not. Real rate histories arrive as a list of daily rates and the analysis needs periods, with the shut-ins identified, before anything else can happen. Getting the period boundaries wrong is the commonest cause of a multi-rate analysis producing nonsense, and it is a bookkeeping error rather than an analysis one.

## Where the linearity assumption bites

Superposition needs a linear system, and two things break it.

**Storage that changes between periods.** A well shut in at surface has a different storage coefficient when it is flowing than when it is building up, because one involves a compressible column and the other a rising liquid level. Superposing a constant-storage solution across a rate change is then wrong at early times after the change.

**Rate-dependent skin.** In gas wells the non-Darcy component of the skin depends on rate, so the skin is not the same in two periods at different rates. Superposition assumes it is.

Both damage the early data after each rate change, which is the same part of the record that storage damages anyway.

## What multi-rate analysis buys

Two things a single-rate test cannot give.

**More reliable parameters**, because the same reservoir is being interrogated several times and the answers have to be mutually consistent.

**Rate-dependent effects**, which are invisible in a single-rate test by construction. If the apparent skin rises with rate, that is non-Darcy flow, and separating it needs at least two rates. This is the basis of the isochronal test in gas wells.

## The misconception to avoid

"Multi-rate analysis is for badly behaved tests." It is for real production histories, which is what most wells have instead of a designed test. A well with two years of monthly rates and a pressure gauge has a multi-rate dataset, and treating it as one is usually better than waiting for a shut-in that will not be authorised.
