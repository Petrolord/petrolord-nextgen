# Two answers from three points

Two accepted methods, the same data, and the one that fits better gives the bigger number.

{{panel:wt-regression-explorer}}

## The result

Take the three flow-after-flow points from the previous lesson. Fit both methods. Evaluate both at atmospheric pressure.

The two absolute open flows differ by about four percent.

And the back-pressure fit's r squared is 0.9999999995813558, while the LIT fit's is 0.9929731227041356. The method with the far better correlation coefficient returns the LARGER open flow.

## Why they disagree

The two equations have different shapes.

Rawlins-Schellhardt is a power law: a straight line on log-log axes. Over the three tested points it fits essentially perfectly, because three points define a line with one degree of freedom left over and the data were generated by something close to a power law.

The LIT form is a quadratic in rate. Over the same three points it fits well but not perfectly, because the true relationship is not exactly quadratic either.

Extrapolate both a long way beyond the data, which is what AOF does, and the shapes diverge. The power law keeps growing at a fixed exponent; the quadratic's turbulence term grows faster and holds the rate down.

## Why the better r squared does not settle it

Because r squared measures agreement over the range where there are data, and AOF is a value outside that range.

The tested pressure-squared differences run to about 2.1 million. The AOF is evaluated at about 3.8 million, nearly twice the largest tested value. Nothing in the fit constrains behaviour there, and the fit statistic cannot report on a region it never saw.

This is the same lesson as the Associate tier's r squared discussion, one level up. A statistic that measures fit quality inside the data range is silent about extrapolation, and every important number here is an extrapolation.

## Which one to believe

The LIT form, for extrapolation, on physical grounds: it is derived from the flow equation with an explicit turbulence term, and turbulence really does grow with the square of rate. The power law has no mechanism, and its exponent is a fitted number that happens to describe the tested range.

The back-pressure form, for comparison against historical values and contract precedent, because that is what the contract was written against.

Reporting both is the honest answer, and it costs one extra line.

## The uncertainty this exposes

A four percent disagreement between two accepted methods on noiseless data is a floor on the uncertainty of an AOF. Real data have scatter, real reservoir pressures are uncertain, and real tests have fewer well-stabilised points than anybody would like.

So an AOF quoted to four significant figures is claiming a precision that the choice of method alone contradicts. Two or three figures, with the method named, is the defensible form.

## The parallel with everything else in this course

The pattern has now appeared five times.

The window choice moved the permeability by a factor of 3.7. The boundary halved it. The regime confusion multiplied it by five. The rate history inflated it by 40 percent. And here two accepted methods differ by four percent on three clean points.

In every case the input data were correct and the arithmetic was correct. The differences came entirely from decisions about how to analyse, and in every case the software would have produced whichever answer it was asked for without complaint.

## The misconception to avoid

"With clean data and correct arithmetic there is one answer." There is one answer per method, and the methods are not equivalent outside the range where they were compared. Where a result is an extrapolation, the method is part of the result and belongs in the report next to the number.
