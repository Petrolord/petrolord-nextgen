# Onward to Professional

You can now take one well, choose a window, fit it, judge the fit, book an EUR at a stated limit, and say when the well gets there. That is a complete piece of engineering work, and thousands of reserves entries in the world rest on nothing more than it done carefully. If you have the Associate capstone behind you, the skill is real.

It is also, deliberately, the easy case. Every well in this tier was clean. One regime, noise-free data, a fit that recovered the planted truth exactly, and a decline exponent that the data pinned down without argument. Real fields are not usually that generous, and the Professional tier is about what you do when they are not.

Here is what it takes up, and why each piece exists.

**The b problem.** In this tier $b$ arrived as a fitted output you read off a tile. At Professional it becomes the hardest number in decline analysis. You will look at how the engine actually searches for $b$, why an early stretch of data barely constrains it, what happens when the search wants a value the model cannot honestly support, and why a wrong model family can still report an excellent goodness of fit over a short window. The practical lesson is that $b$ is a judgment made under evidence, not a measurement, and the evidence for it accumulates with time on production.

**Fit windows.** You met the primary window as a rule handed to you: stop at the flood. At Professional you will learn to choose windows yourself and to defend them, because a window boundary is a statement about which physical regime you believe the well is in. Small moves in a start date can change the answer substantially, which sounds alarming until you realise it is the model telling you where one regime ends and another begins. You will also work the reverse case, where a well has changed and a new window is the only honest response.

**Forecast mechanics.** This tier used closed forms, which are exact. Software forecasts step day by day or month by month and produce answers that differ slightly from the exact integral, always in a predictable direction. Professional works out which direction, why, how large the effect is, and which of the two numbers belongs in a report. It is the discipline of knowing what your tool actually computed rather than what you assumed it computed.

**Type curves.** New wells have no history to fit. Borrowing decline character from wells that already have one is standard practice and a standard way to be badly wrong. You will pool wells, normalize them, fit the cloud, apply the result to a well, and then confront the trap that gives the technique its reputation: a borrowed curve can match a well's history beautifully and still book a materially wrong volume, because history says nothing about a tail it never observed.

**Portfolio roll-ups.** One well is a booking. A field is a portfolio, and adding wells up introduces failure modes that have nothing to do with decline curves: series that look like volumes but are sums of rates, column headings that do not mean what they say, and scenario totals that quietly mix conventions. Professional teaches you to read an aggregated result sceptically before you sign it.

The tier closes the way this one did, with a workflow, a walkthrough and a capstone across all four Ekene producers rather than a single well.

None of that requires anything you have not already got. It requires the same habits applied where the answers stop being obvious: name your window, state your limit, read every parameter rather than the headline fit statistic, and never publish a number without the assumptions that produced it.
