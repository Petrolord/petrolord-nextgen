# Onward to Expert

You arrived at this tier able to book one clean well. You leave it able to say why the rows you fitted are the right rows, to tell a fitted decline exponent from a fitted artefact, to know which of two legitimate forecast numbers is in your cell, to borrow decline character from another well without pretending the borrowing is free, and to add four bookings together while remaining honest about what the sum is. That is the working core of production forecasting, and it is enough to be genuinely useful on a real asset team.

It is also still, in one specific way, a private skill. Everything you have justified so far, you justified to yourself. The Expert tier is about the point where your numbers meet other people: auditors, partners, reserves committees, and the published record of the discipline.

**Literature validation.** So far the answer key has been the field itself, because the Ekene data was constructed with known truth inside it and the engine recovers it exactly. Real work has no answer key, so the profession uses a substitute: worked examples published in the standard references, with printed inputs and printed answers. Reproducing them is how you demonstrate that a tool computes what it claims. Expert works through examples from Weaver and from Ahmed's reservoir engineering handbook, including a classic gas-well case, and the exercise is not arithmetic practice. It is the habit of validating a tool before trusting it, and of citing the page you validated against so that someone else can repeat the check.

**The b lever and its governance.** You learned in module 1 that b is barely identified early and that a search boundary can produce one. Expert asks the next question: what does b do to the volume you book? The answer is uncomfortable enough that the reserves profession has written rules about it. Holding everything else fixed and moving b alone changes booked EUR by a large multiple, all of it from the tail, none of it from data anybody has observed. This is why SPEE's recommended evaluation practice on decline parameters exists, why b values above one are treated as claims requiring justification rather than as fit outputs, and why terminal-decline conventions are imposed on hyperbolic forecasts in practice. Expert works the numbers, then works the governance that surrounds them.

**Confidence intervals.** Your fits in this tier returned R2 values of exactly 1, because the data carries no noise. That is a teaching convenience with a sharp edge: a fit statistic near perfection tells you about agreement with your points, and says nothing about how well the parameters are pinned down. Expert separates those ideas. You will look at what a parameter interval actually measures, which is scatter, and confront the fact that noise-free data produces intervals near zero without making the parameters any more true. Then you will work intervals on real, scattered literature data, where they mean something, and learn the sentences an interval does not license you to say.

**Risked reserves.** Every number in this tier has been a single value. Reserves are not reported that way, because a single value cannot carry the range of outcomes an asset actually faces. Expert introduces distributions in place of points, the standard low, mid and high quantiles the petroleum convention attaches to them, and how a field-level range is built from well-level uncertainty without the naive assumption that the wells miss independently. It also draws a line you will need: Monte Carlo is a tool for exploring a distribution, and it is not a tool for producing an auditable number unless the sampling is reproducible. Where a closed form exists, the closed form is the answer.

**Refitting after a flood.** The Ekene waterflood has been the boundary of every window you drew, and you have never looked past it. Expert does. A flood response has a ramp, a plateau and a decline of its own, and the honest analysis of a flooded well involves choosing a new window entirely, understanding that the oil stream and the gross fluid stream decline differently as water cut climbs, and knowing that moving a window start by a few months can be the difference between recovering a well's true post-response behaviour and fitting the tail of the ramp. That module also hands cumulative production across to material balance, where the field's decline stops being a curve fitted to rates and starts being a volume the reservoir has to account for.

None of this requires new mathematics beyond what you have. It requires you to defend your numbers to a hostile reader, to quantify what you do not know, and to accept that some of the most consequential parameters in a booking are judgments under governance rather than measurements. If this tier taught you to justify a window, the next one teaches you to justify a range.

## Exercise

Before you start the Expert tier, write two pages for yourself. On the first, list every number you have produced in this course whose value depends on a judgment rather than a measurement, and beside each one name the judgment. On the second, take your Professional capstone submission and, for each of the six fields, write the sentence you would say to an auditor who asked "how do you know?" Mark the sentences you could not defend for more than one follow-up question. Those marks are your reading list for what comes next.
