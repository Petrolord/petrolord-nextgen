# What a free air burst leaves out

{{panel:cq-harm}}

The Kinney and Graham fit describes one idealised event: a charge of TNT detonating in open air, far from any surface. A real accidental explosion is rarely that. This lesson names the two differences that matter most, says how the engine expects the analyst to carry each, and marks what lies outside the engine altogether.

## A burst at the ground

A charge that bursts at the ground reflects its shock off the surface, and the engine does not model that reflection. Its header says the matter is the caller's choice of charge weight: an analyst who judges a burst to be at ground level carries that judgement in the TNT mass typed, and says so in the note.

That is a real responsibility. The fit will return a confident number to six decimals whether or not the charge sat on the ground. The number describes the free air event; its fitness for your event rests on the charge you chose.

## A vapour cloud is not TNT

A butane cloud is a different explosive from TNT, and the congestion around it shapes its blast. The engine bridges the gap with one input only. The yield factor carries the whole difference.

For BONGA's 3000 kg of butane the TNT mass runs from 596.086957 kg at a yield of 0.02 to 5960.869565 kg at 0.2. Nothing else in the engine responds to how cluttered the plant is. A tightly packed module and an open tank farm with the same fuel mass differ in the calculation only if the analyst gives them different yields.

## What lies outside the engine

The TNO multi-energy method, which treats congestion directly, is not in the engine. Its blast charts are graphical and no published curve fit was found. The Kingery-Bulmash fits are not in the engine either; their coefficients were not obtained. Neither is taught here as computed, and the next module lists both among what the engine does not do.

## What the note says

A consequence note built on this fit states three things beside every overpressure: that the model is a free air burst; how the analyst allowed for a ground burst, if at all; and the yield factor or stated TNT mass, with its source. A reviewer can then judge the event against the one the fit describes.

Then write the three sentences a consequence note would carry for the higher yield case, naming the free air model, the ground burst allowance you made, and where the yield came from.
