# What a datum is

A datum, in flattening, is a chosen depth that a chosen top is forced onto in every well. Both halves of that sentence are choices you make, and the panel will not tell you that you chose badly. It will draw whatever you ask for, confidently.

## Two choices inside one word

Saying "the datum" hides the fact that you set two things.

The **flattening top** is which surface gets levelled. On the Ekene section that is TOP_A for everything this tier grades. Choosing it is a geological decision, and the next three lessons are about what it commits you to.

The **datum depth** is the number that surface is forced onto. Here it is 1450 m. Choosing it is a presentation decision, and it commits you to very little.

Those two choices are not equally consequential, and treating them as equally consequential is the first mistake to unlearn.

## Why the depth is the smaller choice

Move the datum depth and you add the same amount to every well's shift, because the shift is the datum minus a pick and the picks did not move. Every displayed depth on the panel then changes by that same amount, in the same direction, together. The whole picture slides. Nothing inside it deforms.

So the geometry of a flattened panel is fixed by the flattening top alone. The datum depth sets where that geometry is printed on the axis, and nothing else. Every interval is unchanged, the growth range of 14 m is unchanged, the count of 3 wells is unchanged, and even the displayed span of 150 m is unchanged, because both the shallowest and the deepest displayed pick moved by the same amount.

That is worth stating plainly. The 1450 m in this tier's setting is a convention. It was chosen to sit above every TOP_A pick so that the whole section below the datum reads downward from a clean round line, and any other number above the picks would have produced the same geology on a differently labelled axis.

Which is not a reason to be casual about it. A datum depth is part of the description of a view, and a displayed depth quoted without it cannot be reconstructed by anybody else.

## Why the top is the larger choice

Change the flattening top and the panel is a different picture, not the same picture in a different place.

The reason is that each well's shift is now derived from a different pick, so the wells move by different relative amounts. Flatten Ekene on TOP_A and the four shifts differ from one another by exactly the relief on TOP_A, which is 35 m. Flatten the same four wells on TOP_SAND and they differ by the relief on TOP_SAND, which is 49 m. Different relative movement means different geometry below the line, and it means a different question is being asked.

Levelling a surface also spends it. Once TOP_A draws as one flat line across four wells, the panel contains no information about relief on TOP_A, because you removed it deliberately. That 35 m still exists and it is still readable, in the structural view, where it belongs. It is not recoverable from the flattened one.

## The overloaded word

Two unrelated things in this workflow are called a datum, and mixing them is a real error rather than a vocabulary quibble.

The **depth reference datum** is the physical point that measured depths are counted from: the kelly bushing, the rotary table, the drill floor, or mean sea level. It is a property of the well, it was fixed when the well was logged, and no interpretation decision changes it. Two wells quoted against different reference datums are not comparable until one is converted, which is a Well Data Manager problem rather than a correlation one. Every Ekene well shares one reference datum, so the picks in the table compare as they stand.

The **flattening datum** is the display construct this lesson is about. It exists only inside a view, it changes the moment you change your mind, and it never touches the tops table.

A statement like "TOP_SAND is at 1503 m" is unusable until you know which of those two datums it is spoken against. Under the flattening datum of this tier it is a displayed depth in Ekene-2. As a measured depth it is nowhere in the dataset.

## What flattening on a top commits you to

Three commitments come with the choice, and all three should be things you would defend out loud.

You are claiming the surface was near enough level at one moment. Levelling it restores the geometry of that moment only if it was level then, so the surface should be one that is expected to have been near horizontal over the area, such as a widespread flooding surface, rather than one built by erosion.

You are claiming the pick is reliable in every well. The flattening pick is the anchor for its entire column. An error of 10 m in that one pick moves every other surface in that well by 10 m on the display, in the same direction, and the flattened surface still draws perfectly flat while it does so. The one surface that cannot reveal an error in the flattening pick is the flattening surface.

You are choosing what the panel is about. Levelling TOP_A puts the whole section below it on a common starting line, so the panel becomes an argument about what accumulated below TOP_A. That is this tier's question. A different top would make it a different argument.

## What it does not commit you to

Flattening does not commit you to believing anything about the data. The tops table is unchanged, the measured depths are unchanged, and switching back to the structural view is free and lossless. That is exactly why looking at both views is the standard of care rather than a nicety. You lose nothing by checking, and the two views constrain each other.

## Exercise

Write down the two choices hidden inside the phrase "flatten on TOP_A at 1450 m", and say for each one what it changes on the panel. Then answer this: if a colleague hands you a flattened panel with no caption, which of the two choices could you recover by eye from the picture alone, and which could you not.

Self-check: the two choices are the flattening top, TOP_A, and the datum depth, 1450 m. The top sets the geometry of the panel, because it decides how much each well moves relative to the others. The depth slides the whole picture along the axis and changes no interval, no growth number and no displayed span. From an uncaptioned panel you can recover both, in the sense that the flattened surface is the one drawing as a perfectly flat line and the datum depth is the value on the axis where it draws. What you cannot recover is whether that flat line is flat because the surface is flat or flat because somebody levelled it, and that is the ambiguity a caption exists to close.
