# Why flatten at all

Before any arithmetic, the motive. Flattening is not a drawing preference and it is not a way of making a panel look tidy. It exists because a raw section carries two geological signals on the same axis, and one of them is loud enough to hide the other.

## Two signals, one axis

A correlation panel has one vertical axis and it is depth. Every surface drawn on it sits at a depth that is the sum of two separate histories.

The first history is structural. The whole column at a given well has been carried up or down by whatever the structure did, and it carries every surface in that well with it, together, as one piece. This is why the deeper part of a column in a structurally low well is deep: it is riding on everything above it.

The second history is depositional. The separation between two surfaces in a well is the thickness that accumulated between the time the lower one formed and the time the upper one formed. That separation belongs to the well, and it has nothing to do with how deep the well ended up.

Read the panel and your eye receives the sum. A surface that draws deep in one well tells you nothing on its own about whether the interval above it is thick, because deep and thick look the same when the whole column has been pushed down the page.

## The size of the problem on this section

Put the two signals side by side in numbers, from the Ekene table.

The structural signal on TOP_SAND is 49 m of relief, from the shallowest occurrence to the deepest. The depositional signal in the interval above it, TOP_A to TOP_SAND, runs 48, 53, 46 and 60 m across the four wells, so the whole variation to be seen is 14 m from thickest to thinnest.

Fourteen against forty-nine. The thing you want to read is roughly a quarter of the size of the thing that dominates the picture, and the panel is not even scaled to 49 m, because it has to accommodate the full depth range of every pick in the dataset. At that scale, 14 m of thickness variation is drawn thinner than the interpretive judgement that produced the picks.

That ratio is why the answer to "can I just look harder" is no. The eye is being asked to compare small vertical gaps between lines that are themselves sloping steeply and by different amounts, and it is poor at that comparison.

## What removing the structural component means

Flattening removes the structural component on one chosen surface, and nothing else.

The mechanism is a rigid vertical slide. Each well's whole column moves up or down as a single block until the chosen surface lands on a chosen depth. Nothing inside a well is stretched, squeezed, or reordered, and no surface can cross another as a result of the move.

Two consequences follow directly from that rigidity, and they are the reason the operation is safe.

The chosen surface becomes flat across the section by construction. That flatness is not a discovery. You put it there, and the panel can no longer be used as evidence about relief on that surface.

Every interval inside every well survives untouched, because both ends of an interval move by the same amount. The TOP_A to TOP_SAND interval is 48, 53, 46 and 60 m before flattening and 48, 53, 46 and 60 m after it. That invariance is what makes flattening a display transform rather than a data transform, and it is what lets you trust a thickness read off a flattened panel.

## What is left when the structure is gone

Once the chosen surface is flat, the separation between the wells at any other surface cannot be relief on the chosen surface, because that has been subtracted out. What remains is the thickness of the material between the two surfaces, well by well, drawn at a scale that no longer has to accommodate the structural range.

That residual is the signal. If a section were a layer cake, deposited on a floor that was not moving while it accumulated, flattening on any surface would produce a stack of flat lines, because every interval would be identical everywhere. A flattened panel that is not flat is telling you that the interval changed thickness across the section, and thickness changed because the room available to fill changed while it was filling.

That is the geological claim in one sentence: an interval that varies in thickness across a section is a record of the accommodation varying while the sediment arrived. Ekene has 14 m of that variation between TOP_A and TOP_SAND, and the modules ahead turn that statement into a measurement.

## Why not just subtract in a table

You could compute all four intervals from the pick table with a pencil and never draw anything. For four wells and one interval that is a reasonable thing to do, and you should be able to do it. It is also not a substitute for the panel, for two reasons.

The panel shows the pattern rather than the list. Four numbers in a column do not tell you at a glance whether the thickening runs systematically across the section or jumps around, and a systematic trend is a completely different geological claim from a scatter.

The panel checks itself. The flattened surface must draw as one flat line, and a well that fails to sit on it is a well with a problem you would not have seen in the table.

## The claim you are making

Flattening on a surface asserts that the surface was, near enough, level at one moment in time, and that restoring it to level restores the geometry of that moment. That claim is defensible for a widespread marine flooding surface and much weaker for an erosional one, where different amounts of section have been removed at different wells. The arithmetic will not warn you. It produces shifts for any surface you give it, whether the claim holds or not, which is why the choice of surface is an interpretation and not a setting.
