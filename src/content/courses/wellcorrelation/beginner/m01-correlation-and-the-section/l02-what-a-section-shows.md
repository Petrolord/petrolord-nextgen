# What a section shows

A correlation section, sometimes called a correlation panel, is the working drawing of this discipline. Almost everything you do in this course happens on one. Before you build a section it is worth taking it apart and naming each piece, because a panel that you can read part by part is much harder to misinterpret than one you absorb as a single picture.

## The anatomy of a panel

**Wells as vertical columns.** Each well in the section is drawn as its own narrow column, and the columns sit side by side in a chosen order, left to right. That order is a decision you make. Most often it follows a line across the field, so the section reads like a walk from one end of the structure to the other, but nothing in the software enforces it.

**Depth increasing downward.** Every column shares the convention that deeper rock is drawn lower on the page. This sounds too obvious to state until you meet a display that does otherwise, and it matters for reading dip: a surface that steps downward toward the right is getting deeper toward the right.

**A log curve per column.** Each well column carries at least one log curve, most commonly gamma ray, drawn as a wiggle down the column. The curve is there for character. You correlate on the shape of a curve, on a distinctive spike or a sharp blocky base or a stack of coarsening upward cycles, far more than you correlate on absolute depth. Two wells at the same depth prove nothing; two wells showing the same recognisable pattern are an argument.

**Formation tops marked in each well.** A top is a named depth in one well, drawn as a small horizontal marker across that well's column at the depth it was picked, and usually labelled with its name. The tops are the interpretation. The curves are the evidence for it.

**Correlation lines joining the tops.** When the same top name has been picked in more than one well, the panel joins those markers with a line running across the section. That line is the visual statement that these picks are the same geological surface. A section with four tops picked in four wells will show four such lines, one per named surface, and their shape across the panel is the fastest summary of the field's structure you will ever get.

**Zones between the lines.** Any two correlation lines enclose a band of the section, and that band is a zone: the rock between one surface and the next, traced across every well. Zones are typically shaded so that you can see at a glance where the interval fattens and where it pinches.

## Two ways to view the same section

The same set of wells and tops can be drawn in more than one way, and the choice changes what the panel emphasises without changing a single pick.

**Structural view.** Each well is hung on its own true depth scale, so every top is drawn at the depth it was actually measured. This is the honest, unmodified picture. Structure is visible directly: if the correlation lines slope, the beds slope. The cost is that thickness changes are hard to judge by eye, because a zone that sits deep in one well and shallow in another has its shape dominated by the structural difference rather than by its own thickness.

**Flattened view.** You choose one top and pin it to a fixed datum depth, then shift every well vertically by whatever amount that pinning requires. On the flattened panel the chosen surface is a perfectly horizontal line, and everything else is drawn relative to it. Structure is removed from the display, and what remains is the thickness behaviour of the intervals. A zone that thins toward one end of the field shows that thinning immediately, with nothing to hide it.

Neither view is more correct than the other. They are two presentations of one dataset, and the working habit is to look at both. Module 3 covers the structural view in detail and module 4 covers flattening, including the arithmetic of the shift and the discipline that keeps the two depths from being confused.

## A section is a display, not a map

This is the point most worth carrying out of the lesson. It is tempting to read a correlation panel as if it were a scaled cross-section of the earth, but the horizontal axis is not distance. It is well order.

The columns are usually spaced evenly across the page regardless of how far apart the wells actually are. Two wells 300 m apart and two wells 8 km apart can occupy identical gaps on the panel. Reorder the wells and the same data produces a visually different structure. Nothing about the apparent slope of a correlation line is a true dip angle, because the run of that slope is a layout choice rather than a measured length.

The vertical axis, by contrast, is real depth in metres, and it is quantitative. So the rule for reading a panel is: trust the vertical, treat the horizontal as ordering only. Any statement about how steeply something dips, or how quickly a sand thins per kilometre, has to come from the well coordinates and a map, not from the shape of the panel. What the panel gives you, and gives you better than anything else, is the correlation itself: which surface is which, in what order, and how the intervals between them change from well to well.

## Exercise

Sketch a correlation panel by hand for three wells and two tops, and label six things on it: the well columns, the depth direction, a log curve, a top marker, a correlation line, and a zone. Then answer two questions in writing. First, if you swapped the left and right wells in your sketch, which of those six things would change and which would not? Second, why can you not measure a dip angle from your sketch? As a self-check, swapping wells changes only the apparent shape of the correlation lines and the zone, never the depths or the picks themselves; and you cannot measure dip because the horizontal spacing is well order rather than true distance.
