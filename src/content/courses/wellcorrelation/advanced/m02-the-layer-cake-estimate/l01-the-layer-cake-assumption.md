# The layer-cake assumption

A layer cake has flat layers of even thickness. Cut it anywhere and every slice looks the same. Applied to a stratigraphic section, the picture says that each unit keeps its thickness across the area, so the surfaces bounding it are parallel and the whole stack can be tilted or gently folded without any layer thickening or thinning.

That picture is the assumption this module runs on. It is worth stating precisely, because the loose version of it hides where the risk lives.

## The precise statement

The layer-cake assumption, in the form you are about to use, is this. The vertical distance between two named surfaces is the same in every well on the section, so a distance measured in one well transfers unchanged to another.

Written that way, one consequence is immediate. If the interval is the same everywhere, then a well that lacks the lower surface can be given one: take the interval from wells that have both surfaces, add it to the upper surface in the well that stopped short, and you have a depth. The missing pick is recoverable because the section carries the information at every other location.

A second consequence matters just as much. If the interval really is constant, it does not matter which upper surface you start from. Start from TOP_A or start from TOP_SAND and you land in the same place, because under a true layer cake every interval is constant, including the one between your two candidate starting markers. A layer-cake section has no method dependence at all.

Hold on to that second consequence. It is the test you will apply to this section later.

## Why it is the natural first move

The layer-cake estimate is not a beginner's error to be talked out of. It is the correct place to start, for three reasons.

It uses the data you have in the most direct way available. Three wells carry both surfaces, so three measurements of the interval exist. Averaging them and applying the result is the shortest path from measurement to prediction, with the fewest steps in which something can go wrong.

It is transparent. Anyone reading your work can see the three intervals, the mean and the addition. There is nothing to take on trust, and a reviewer who disagrees can point at the exact step they object to.

It is the baseline everything else is judged against. A more elaborate method is worth using only if it differs from the simple one, and you cannot know whether it differs until the simple one has been computed. The layer-cake number is the reference the rest of this tier measures against.

## When the assumption is reasonable

Sections do exist that behave close enough to a layer cake for the assumption to be sound.

Blanket deposition over a stable shelf produces units of similar thickness over long distances, because the accommodation being filled was similar everywhere. Deep marine muds draped over a wide area behave the same way. A short well spacing helps a great deal, since two wells a few hundred metres apart give the section little opportunity to change between them. Post-depositional deformation is friendly to the assumption as well: tilting and gentle folding move surfaces without altering the thickness between them, which is exactly why flattening works, and it is why a section with strong structural relief can still be an excellent layer cake.

The reliable signature is empirical rather than conceptual. If you measure an interval in several wells and get nearly the same number every time, the assumption is supported for that interval, in that area, over that well spacing. That is a statement you can check, and it is the only version of the argument worth making.

## When it is not reasonable

The assumption fails wherever the accommodation being filled varied across the area during deposition.

Growth faulting is the standard case. A fault active while sediment accumulated creates more space on its downthrown side, so units thicken toward the fault and thin away from it. Deltaic and clastic shelf settings, which is where sections like this one usually come from, are full of that behaviour. Differential compaction over an underlying high, salt movement, channel and lobe geometries that change thickness over short distances, and unconformities that cut down more in one place than another all break it in the same way, by making the interval a function of position.

The consequence is always the same. The bounding surfaces stop being parallel. Once they are not parallel, the distance between them depends on where you measure it, and a mean interval borrowed from three locations is a summary of three places rather than a property of the section.

## What the Ekene section says

The Professional tier already asked this question of these wells and got an answer. The TOP_A to TOP_SAND interval, the one interval all four wells can supply, is 48, 53, 46 and 60 m. The growth range is 14 m, and Ekene-4 carries the thickest value.

That is not a layer cake. The one interval measurable in every well varies by 14 m across four wells, which means those two surfaces are not parallel across this section.

So you are entering this module knowing the assumption is imperfect here. That is the honest position, and it is not a reason to skip the module. The layer-cake estimate remains worth computing, for the three reasons above, and its known imperfection is what makes the rest of the tier meaningful. You will compute it, use it, and then measure how much its imperfection costs.
