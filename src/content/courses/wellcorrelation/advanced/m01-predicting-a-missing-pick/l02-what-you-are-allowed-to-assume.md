# What you are allowed to assume

Prediction is not guessing and it is not measuring. It is a third thing, and it has a shape: a stated assumption plus arithmetic. The arithmetic is the easy half and it is checkable by anyone. The assumption is the half that decides whether the answer is worth anything, and it is the half that usually goes unwritten.

This lesson sets the rules of the tier. What you are permitted to assume about the Ekene section, what you are not permitted to assume, and why writing the assumption down beside the number is the thing that makes a prediction defensible rather than merely confident.

## The permitted assumption

You may assume that the section behaves between wells roughly as it behaves at wells.

That is the foundation under every correlation ever drawn. A correlation line between Ekene-1 and Ekene-2 claims that the surface named TOP_A at 1500 m in one hole is the same surface as the one named TOP_A at 1512 m in the other, and that it does something continuous in the rock between them. Nobody has seen that rock. The claim is an assumption, and it is a good one, because sedimentary surfaces are laterally extensive and because the logs on either side look alike.

Extending it to a well that stopped short is the same assumption, applied one step further. If the interval between TOP_A and TOP_B behaves in a regular way across the three wells that carry both surfaces, it is reasonable to expect it to behave in a related way at the fourth. You are borrowing a relationship observed at several places and applying it at a nearby place.

That borrowing is legitimate. It is what every prognosis, every contour and every seismic tie does. It is not a licence to borrow anything you like.

## The forbidden assumption

You may not assume an interval is constant when you have already measured it varying.

This is not a general caution. It is specific to this section and it has a number on it. The Professional tier measured the TOP_A to TOP_SAND interval in all four wells and found 48, 53, 46 and 60 m. The growth range, the maximum minus the minimum, is 14 m. Ekene-4 carries the thickest of the four at 60 m and Ekene-3 the thinnest at 46 m.

So a claim that any interval in this section is the same everywhere is not an untested convenience. It is a claim the data has already contradicted for the one interval where all four wells could be checked. You cannot make it innocently here, and you cannot make it quietly.

What you can do is make it openly, as an approximation, with the contradiction acknowledged. That is a different act, and it is respectable. Saying "I will treat the TOP_A to TOP_B interval as constant across the section, knowing that the TOP_A to TOP_SAND interval varies by 14 m over these wells" gives your reader everything needed to judge and to disagree. Saying "TOP_B is at this depth" gives them nothing.

## Every prediction has the same two parts

Write your predictions in the form the tier grades them in.

**The assumption.** A sentence that could be false, stated so plainly that a reviewer could argue with it. "The interval from TOP_SAND down to TOP_B is roughly the same in Ekene-4 as its average in the three wells that carry TOP_B" is such a sentence. It names the interval, it names the wells, and it can be wrong.

**The arithmetic.** A depth Ekene-4 actually has, plus a mean interval measured elsewhere. Two numbers and one addition. Any reader can reproduce it in their head, which means any reader can check it.

Keeping the two apart has an effect that repays the effort. When somebody later disagrees with your predicted depth, the disagreement lands on the assumption, where it belongs, and not on you. When a fifth well arrives, you know exactly which part of the work has to be redone. And when two predictions disagree, as two of yours are about to, you can see immediately that the arithmetic is not at fault, because the difference lives entirely in which assumption each one made.

## Assumptions you might reach for and should not

**That the missing surface follows the structure of the surfaces above it.** This is the layer-cake assumption in disguise and module 2 examines it properly. It is usable, and it is not free.

**That the deepest well has the deepest everything.** Ekene-4 has the deepest TOP_A at 1530 m, so it is tempting to expect it to have the deepest TOP_B too. That expectation may turn out to be right, and it is not evidence. Structural position and interval thickness are separate quantities, and a section that grows is one where they can move against each other.

**That the mean of three wells is the value at the fourth.** A mean is a summary of three observations. Treating it as the truth at a place you did not observe is the assumption, not a way of avoiding one.

**That an unlikely outcome can be excluded.** Nothing you compute here rules out a fault, an unconformity or a channel between Ekene-4 and its neighbours. The section shows no evidence for any of them, and absence of evidence in four wells spaced across a field is weak evidence of absence. Say the prediction assumes no structural or stratigraphic surprise between the wells, and let the reader weigh that.

## Naming the assumption is the deliverable

The habit to carry through the rest of this tier is small and it changes everything downstream. Every predicted number you write gets a clause attached that says what had to be true for it to hold.

A depth with no clause reads as a fact. Six months later, nobody can tell it from a pick, and its uncertainty has evaporated. A depth with a clause stays visibly conditional for as long as it exists, which is exactly as long as it should.

## Exercise

Write out, in one sentence each, the assumption behind two different ways of predicting Ekene-4's TOP_B: one that leans on TOP_A and one that leans on TOP_SAND. Do not compute either prediction. Then say which measured fact in this section makes it certain that the two sentences cannot both be exactly true.

Self-check: the first assumes the TOP_A to TOP_B interval in Ekene-4 matches its average in the three wells that carry TOP_B. The second assumes the TOP_SAND to TOP_B interval in Ekene-4 matches its average in those same three wells. They cannot both be exactly true because Ekene-4's TOP_A to TOP_SAND interval is 60 m while the section's four values are 48, 53, 46 and 60 m, so Ekene-4 is not an average well between those two markers. If both assumptions held exactly, Ekene-4 would have to carry the average spacing between TOP_A and TOP_SAND as well, and it does not.
