# Reporting a predicted pick

The arithmetic of this tier is small. Two means, two additions, one subtraction. The part that takes practice is what leaves your desk afterwards, because a predicted depth travels further than the person who made it and it loses its qualifiers on the way. A number written down as 1671 m in a table will be read as a pick by the third person who sees it, and nothing in the number itself will stop that.

So the report is the work. A predicted pick is reported correctly when six things are present, and it is reported badly when any one of them is missing.

## The six elements

**1. The predicted depth, or the range.** Give the number in metres and say what frame it is in. Ekene-4's TOP_B is predicted between 1671 m and 1682 m measured depth. If you are quoting a single value, quote the one you prefer with a reason, not the average of the two. The average of 1671 m and 1682 m is 1676.5 m, and no method produced that depth.

**2. The method.** Say what you did, in enough words that a reader could repeat it. Projecting a mean interval down from a marker is one sentence to describe: the mean TOP_A to TOP_B interval of 141 m added to Ekene-4's TOP_A at 1530 m gives 1671 m. Where two methods were used, describe both, because the second one is where the range came from.

**3. The anchor marker.** Name the marker each estimate was projected from, and confirm that the target well actually has it. Ekene-4 carries TOP_A at 1530 m and TOP_SAND at 1590 m, so both anchors are real picks in that well. This element looks trivial until somebody projects from a marker the target well does not have, at which point the whole estimate is built on a depth that was itself invented.

**4. The wells the interval came from.** Say how many and which. The intervals here come from Ekene-1, Ekene-2 and Ekene-3, which are the three wells that carry TOP_B. Ekene-4 contributes its anchor and nothing else, because it has no TOP_B to measure an interval to. Three wells is a small sample and the reader is entitled to know that before deciding what to do with the result.

**5. The spread.** Give the disagreement between the estimates in metres and say what it is. The spread is 11 m, and it is the measured disagreement between two methods on the same three wells. Do not present it as an instrument tolerance, a confidence interval or a statistical error, because it is none of those. It is what happened when the same data were worked two defensible ways.

**6. An explicit label that this is a prediction.** Write the word. A predicted TOP_B is not a pick, it does not go in the tops table, it does not extend the correlation line, and it does not change the count of wells carrying all four tops, which stays at 3 of 4. The label is the only thing standing between your estimate and somebody's contour map.

## The shape of a report sentence

Here is the whole thing in one sentence, which is usually all the space you get:

Ekene-4 TOP_B is predicted at 1682 m measured depth, projected from that well's TOP_SAND pick at 1590 m using the mean TOP_SAND to TOP_B interval of 92 m from the three wells that carry TOP_B, with a layer-cake estimate from TOP_A of 1671 m giving a spread of 11 m, so the prediction is a range of 1671 m to 1682 m and not a pick.

Count the elements in that sentence and all six are there. Depth, method, anchor, wells, spread, label. It is not elegant and it does not need to be, because it is a sentence built to survive being copied into somebody else's document.

If you have room for a second sentence, use it to say why you prefer the estimate you led with. The from-TOP_SAND estimate is the shorter projection, 92 m against 141 m, and it starts from a marker below the part of the section that thickens most in Ekene-4, so it carries less of the growth error. That reasoning is worth writing down, because it is the part a reader cannot reconstruct from the numbers.

## What a bad report looks like

Bad reports are usually short rather than wrong. TOP_B 1671 in a spreadsheet cell is a bad report, and it is a bad report specifically because everything about it is correct. The depth is one the method produced. The unit is implied by the column. The only thing missing is every qualifier, and the cell now looks exactly like the three cells above it that hold real picks.

Two other failures are worth naming. Reporting the average of the two estimates hides the disagreement, which was the most informative thing you found. Reporting a single estimate with no range invites the reader to treat it as measured, and a single number with no range is less useful than two numbers that disagree, because the two at least tell you how much to trust either.

## Exercise

Write the report sentence for the layer-cake estimate as the preferred value rather than the from-TOP_SAND estimate, keeping all six elements. Then answer in two sentences: which element is missing from the note "Ekene-4 TOP_B est. 1676 m", and what would a reader most likely do with that note.

As a self check: a correct sentence reads that Ekene-4 TOP_B is predicted at 1671 m measured depth, projected from that well's TOP_A pick at 1530 m using the mean TOP_A to TOP_B interval of 141 m from the three wells that carry TOP_B, with a from-TOP_SAND estimate of 1682 m giving a spread of 11 m, so the prediction is a range of 1671 m to 1682 m and not a pick. The note "Ekene-4 TOP_B est. 1676 m" is missing the method, the anchor marker, the wells, the spread and a real prediction label, since est. is a qualifier a reader can drop without noticing, and 1676 m is close to the average of the two estimates rather than a depth either method produced. A reader would most likely enter it in the tops table as a pick and contour it, which is how a predicted depth becomes a mapped surface with nothing left on it to say where it came from.
