# Deck area in square metres

{{panel:marine-voyage-calculator}}

A supply vessel's open after deck carries the cargo that does not pour: containers, baskets, tanks, skips and bundles of pipe. The first limit on a voyage is whether that cargo has room. This lesson shows how the engine measures deck cargo and the deck that holds it.

## Deck cargo is measured by area

Aas, Halskau and Wallace describe deck cargo as measured in square metres, with containers and baskets placed side by side and none stacked on another. The engine takes that idea by concept. Each installation's deck cargo is stated as an area in m2 and a weight in tonnes, and the deck's capacity is an area too. There is no stacking and no volume: two containers of the same footprint need twice the deck.

## The Ekene milk run's deck cargo

Each installation states the deck area its cargo needs on one voyage, and a milk run carries them all at once.

## The deck's capacity

PSV Ekene Star has 800 m2 of deck. Not all of it can take cargo: the engine multiplies the deck area by a stated usable fraction, 0.75 for the Ekene vessels, to find the deck area capacity. That gives the PSV 600.000000 m2, so the milk run's 540 m2 of cargo uses a fraction of 0.900000 of it. The next lesson looks at the usable fraction itself.

The AHTS has a smaller deck of 550 m2, and at the same fraction the same 540 m2 of cargo does not fit on it, a result module five reads in full.

## What an area bound does not check

Treating deck cargo as an area is a planning bound. It does not compare a basket's length with the deck's width, keep a lane clear for the crane, or balance the load. A cargo that fits by area can still fail on the real deck, and the deck crew's own plan decides that. How individual units are packed onto the deck, unit by unit, is a question the Professional tier takes up.
