# The peak time is not fixed

You already know this fact. The Associate tier ran the teaching well at three frequencies and showed that the strongest amplitude lands at 1580 ms TWT at 15 Hz, at 1642 ms TWT at 25 Hz and at 1646 ms TWT at 40 Hz, and it said in as many words that changing the frequency changes both the size of the peak and its position. Nothing in this module is going to overturn that.

What changes at this tier is what you are expected to do about it. The Associate tier could treat the moving peak as an interesting caution at the end of a lesson. Here the peak time is a deliverable. The capstone asks you to produce four of these numbers, two amplitudes and two times, at two named frequencies, and a reader who receives them has to be able to trust the pairing. That turns a caution into a procedure.

## The readings you have to produce

Hold the three pairs in front of you, because the rest of the module works on them rather than on deriving them again.

| Wavelet frequency | Strongest amplitude | At TWT |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 15 Hz | 0.1573149710893631 | 1580 ms |
| 25 Hz | 0.07300488650798798 | 1642 ms |
| 40 Hz | 0.0362229160964489 | 1646 ms |

Two of those three rows are graded, the 15 Hz row and the 40 Hz row. The 25 Hz row is the one you have been living with since the Associate tier and it is graded nowhere, which makes it the most available wrong answer in the exercise.

## Operating the frequency control is a task

The panel has a frequency control, and up to now you have used it to look at something. At this tier you use it to produce a record, and that deserves a procedure rather than a habit of clicking around.

Set the frequency first, before you read anything. Then take the amplitude and its two-way time out of the same panel state, in the same glance, as a pair. Write them down together on one line with the frequency at the front of that line. Only then change the frequency, and repeat.

The order matters because of what it prevents. If you read all the amplitudes first and collect the times afterwards, you have two lists that have to be matched up later from memory, and the matching is where the error enters. Reading in pairs means there is never a moment when a number exists in your notes without the frequency and the time it belongs to.

## The quality control that goes with it

Crossed readings are the characteristic failure of this task, and they are dangerous because both halves of a crossed pair are genuine numbers from this well.

Suppose a report says the strongest amplitude is 0.1573149710893631 at 1646 ms TWT. Every element of that sentence appears somewhere in the table. The amplitude is real, the time is real, and no check on either one individually will flag anything. The sentence is still wrong, because those two numbers came from different runs.

The check that catches it is a check on the pair. On this well the amplitude falls as the frequency rises while the peak time moves later, so the largest amplitude belongs to the earliest time and the smallest amplitude belongs to the latest. A large amplitude reported at a late time contradicts itself. Get into the habit of reading your own record back and asking whether the two numbers on the line could have come from the same wavelet.

## What has to travel with the number

A peak reading is not one quantity. It is four things that only mean something together.

It is an amplitude. It is a two-way time. It is the dominant frequency of the wavelet that produced both. And it is the fact that all of this came from a synthetic rather than from the seismic itself, which tells the reader that it is a modelled response to logs from one well rather than a measurement of the survey.

Drop any one of those and the record degrades in a specific way. Without the frequency the reader cannot compare your number with anyone else's. Without the time the amplitude cannot be located. Without the amplitude the time is a pick with no evidence attached. Without the word synthetic the reader may take a modelled trace for observed data.

This is the same discipline you already apply to a depth. Nobody writes 1650 and expects the reader to work out whether it is feet or metres. A peak amplitude quoted without its frequency has exactly that defect, and it is less visible because the number looks complete on its own.

## Why it has to be a habit

The reason to make this automatic rather than to remember it when it seems important is that nothing warns you. A crossed pair, a frequency left off a line, a number copied out of an old note: none of these produces a display that looks wrong, an error message or a value out of range. They produce a clean report that a colleague will act on.

The rest of the module works out what happens when they do. Open the panel, run the procedure once at each of the three frequencies, and check your own record against the table above.
