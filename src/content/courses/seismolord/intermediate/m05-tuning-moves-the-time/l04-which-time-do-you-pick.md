# Which time do you pick

This is the lesson the module exists for. The Associate tier could observe that the peak moves and leave it at that, because it was building a synthetic rather than committing to anything. You are sitting in front of a section with a synthetic beside it, you have to put a pick somewhere, and a pick is a commitment that other people will build on. So which time do you pick, and how do you write it down so that the next person can check you.

## The three cases, as a decision rather than an observation

The separations from the previous lesson are what the decision turns on. Against the fixed coefficient at 1582 ms TWT, the brightest event is about 2 ms away at 15 Hz, 60 ms away at 25 Hz and 64 ms away at 40 Hz.

Read that as three offers. One frequency offers you an answer where following the brightness happens to land on the real contrast, within one sample at the 2 ms sample rate. Two frequencies offer you an answer that sits about 60 ms off it. Nothing on any of the three displays tells you which kind of offer you are looking at, and you have to choose a rule before you know which case you are in.

## Why the 15 Hz case is the dangerous one

The instinct is to treat 15 Hz as the good case and the other two as the bad cases. That is the wrong reading, and getting it right is most of the value of this lesson.

Nothing about the 15 Hz run is more correct. The brightest event coincides with the strongest coefficient there because at that bandwidth the wavelet happens to gather a set of neighbours whose sum peaks near that interface. It is a coincidence of this well at this frequency. If you learned your habits on this well at 15 Hz you would conclude that brightness finds the strongest reflector, you would carry that conclusion to the next well, and it would be wrong there without announcing itself.

A method that gives the right answer for the wrong reason is worse than one that gives a visibly wrong answer, because you never go back and check it.

## Pick a reflector you can defend

The working rule is short. Tie to a reflector you can defend, rather than to whatever is brightest.

A reflector you can defend is one you can name in terms of the well. It has an impedance contrast behind it that you can point at in the reflectivity series. It has a depth, and that depth sits at a formation top or a lithology change you can identify on the logs. You can say which curve made it and what the contrast is. On this well the coefficient at 1582 ms TWT is such a feature: it is the largest single contrast in the logged interval, it comes from the impedance log, and its time comes from the time-depth function, and none of that changes when somebody reprocesses the seismic.

Brightness is not a defence. When somebody asks why you picked where you did, "it was the strongest event" answers a question about the display and not about the earth. The follow-up question is which wavelet the display was made with, and if the answer to that is not on record, the pick cannot be reproduced.

That is the practical test to apply to your own picks. If a colleague rebuilt the synthetic from the same logs at a different bandwidth, would they land on the same feature you did. If your pick is anchored to a coefficient, yes. If it is anchored to whatever was brightest, the answer depends on which bandwidth they chose.

## State the wavelet with the pick

The second half of the rule is a bookkeeping habit, and it costs nothing.

Any amplitude you report and any time you read off a synthetic gets the wavelet frequency written beside it. Not in a separate document, not in the project notes somebody will not open, but in the same line as the number. "Peak amplitude 0.0362229160964489 at 1646 ms TWT" is an incomplete record. "Peak amplitude 0.0362229160964489 at 1646 ms TWT, 40 Hz Ricker" is a complete one.

The reason is the same reason a depth carries a unit. Two people comparing 1580 ms TWT against 1646 ms TWT with no frequencies attached will conclude that one of them made an error of 66 ms, and they will go looking for it in the time-depth function or the shift. With the frequencies attached they will see in a second that both numbers are right and that they were reading different experiments on the same rock.

This is also why the capstone asks for the peak amplitude and the peak time at two named frequencies rather than one. It is not a way of making the exercise longer. It is the assessment refusing to accept a peak reading without the frequency it belongs to.

## What to write down for a tie

A tie record that survives contact with other people has four things in it: the shift you applied, the correlation that shift achieved, the wavelet you used, and the feature you tied to described in terms of the well rather than in terms of the display. The next module builds that record and checks it.
