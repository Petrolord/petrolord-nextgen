# Amplitude is not reflectivity

This is the conclusion the module was built to reach. It is one sentence, and the whole of the module is the evidence for it.

**A bright event is not a strong reflector.**

## The argument in full

The argument is short enough to hold in your head, so hold it there rather than remembering the slogan alone.

Three synthetics were built from one well. The reflectivity series was identical in all three: the same coefficients, the same sizes, the same signs, at the same times, produced from the same sonic and density curves through the same arithmetic. Nothing about the earth changed between the runs, because nothing about the earth was allowed to.

One input changed. The dominant frequency of the wavelet went from 15 Hz to 25 Hz to 40 Hz.

The strongest amplitude on the trace went from 0.1573149710893631 to 0.07300488650798798 to 0.0362229160964489. The largest is over four times the smallest.

So a quantity that changed by a factor of over four was produced by a set of reflectors that did not change at all. Whatever the peak amplitude is measuring, it is not the strength of a reflector, because the strength of every reflector in that well was held constant while the number moved.

That is a clean argument. There is no appeal to a special case, no unusual well, and no processing artefact. It follows from one controlled experiment with one variable.

## What brightness actually indicates

If amplitude is not reflector strength, what is it?

It is the size of a sum. Each sample of the trace adds up the contributions that reach it from every reflection coefficient within the wavelet's reach. A large amplitude means those contributions happened to reinforce at that sample. A small amplitude means they did not, either because few reached it or because those that did partly cancelled.

So a bright event is evidence that something arranged itself favourably at that point. The favourable arrangement can come from several sources: one genuinely large contrast, several moderate contrasts spaced to add, or a wavelet long enough to gather a package of layering into a single sum. From the display alone you cannot tell which, because all three look the same. They look like a bright event.

The reverse case deserves equal weight and gets less attention. A dim event can be a weak contrast, or it can be a strong contrast standing next to a neighbour of opposite sign that cancelled it. Nothing on the section marks the difference, and the dim case is the more expensive mistake, because a bright anomaly at least invites you to go and look at it.

## The professional consequences

Three, and they are the reason this sits in a professional tier.

**Amplitude is not portable between wavelets.** Two amplitudes are comparable only if the wavelets that produced them are comparable. Across a survey merge, a reprocessing, a bandwidth change, or between a synthetic and the seismic it was built for, that condition needs checking rather than assuming.

**Ranking by brightness ranks the wavelet's preferences.** If you sort prospects by peak amplitude you have sorted by how well each one's reflectivity happened to suit the wavelet in that volume. That ordering may agree with the ordering by contrast strength, and nothing on the map tells you whether it does.

**The well tie is the instrument that separates the two.** The reflectivity series is the only thing in your workflow that belongs to the rock alone. Tie the well, put the reflectivity next to the trace, and you can see which bright events correspond to large coefficients and which are sums. That is the practical reason this tier spent three modules getting the synthetic into the right place before it started asking what the amplitudes mean. An untied synthetic cannot answer the question.

## Say it carefully

Two statements that sound alike and are not.

"This event is bright" is an observation about the trace. It is repeatable, it is measurable, and it is true of that dataset.

"This event is a strong reflector" is a claim about the earth. It requires knowing what the wavelet contributed, and the trace alone does not tell you.

The discipline is to keep saying the first until you have earned the second.

## One more thing, and it belongs to module 5

There is a second half to this experiment that has been held back deliberately.

So far you have watched the peak amplitude change with the wavelet. The peak also **moves in time**. The place on the trace where the strongest event sits is not the same place at 15 Hz as it is at 40 Hz, and it moves for the same reason the amplitude changes: a different wavelet gathers a different set of neighbours into the sum, and the sample where the sum is largest is not where it was.

That is a more serious problem than the amplitude one, because a time is what you pick, map and hand to a drilling engineer. Module 5 measures the movement, sets it against the reflectivity, and works out what it costs a tie. Stop here and let it have that.
