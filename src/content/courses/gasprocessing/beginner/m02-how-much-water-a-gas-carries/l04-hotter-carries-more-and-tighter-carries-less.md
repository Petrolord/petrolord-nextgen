# Hotter carries more, tighter carries less

One division gives the whole surface. Because temperature only enters through the numerator and pressure only enters as the divisor, the behaviour of the water content in a gas is completely described by two directions.

Two readings and two directions. Go down any column and the content rises, because the vapour pressure in the numerator rises with temperature. Go across any row and the content falls, because the same vapour pressure is being divided by a larger total pressure.

## Why the two directions are not symmetrical in practice

They look alike on the page and they are quite different in a plant. Pressure is set by whatever the pipeline contract and the compression arrangement demand, and a dehydration unit rarely gets to choose it. Temperature is often movable, because an aerial cooler or an exchanger ahead of the contactor is an ordinary piece of equipment.

So of the two handles in the division, the one a designer can actually reach is usually the numerator. Cooling the gas before it meets the glycol takes water out for nothing, and every pound taken out that way is a pound the glycol loop never has to carry. That is dehydration bought with equipment that sits before the contactor, and it never appears in the glycol balance at all.

## The surface is one equation, four times over

Nothing in the table above was solved for. Every one of those sixteen cells is the same division evaluated at different inputs, so the surface has no features in it. A learner who has met water content as a chart will expect a family of curves that were drawn from measurements and can bend where the measurements bent. This is not that. It is one formula, and its smoothness is a property of the formula rather than evidence about the gas.

That is worth saying plainly, because smoothness is persuasive. An answer that varies gently across a table looks like one that has been checked across it. It has been evaluated across it, which is a different claim.

## Reading the table honestly

Record the figures and read the directions. Do not divide one cell by another and describe the result as how the surface behaves, because this course prints the cells and does not print the ratios between them. A ratio nobody computed reads exactly like one somebody did, and that is how a plausible sentence about a surface turns out to be wrong by a factor.

What you can say safely from this table is what it actually shows. At every pressure in it, a hotter gas carries more water. At every temperature in it, a tighter gas carries less. Both statements hold across the whole surface, and neither of them needs a number that is not printed.

## Exercise
