# More stages against more solvent

A staged column has two dials and they are bought in completely different currencies. Stages are steel and height. The absorption factor is solvent, and the pumping and regeneration that come with it. The surface below is what each one buys.

## Read the columns rather than the rows

A row of this table is a survey of columns that do not exist together. Nobody builds one absorber and then walks its solvent circulation across the whole span of that row. A column of it, by contrast, is a real object being made taller, and that is the comparison an engineer actually faces.

Above an absorption factor of one every column climbs towards total removal as the stages are added. That is the behaviour the word absorber suggests, and it is what makes stage count feel like the natural design variable. At and below one it does not, and the next lesson is about nothing else.

## Two ways to reach one number

The surface is worth reading for pairs that land in the same place, because they are the choices a project is actually deciding between. At 3 stages a column at A = 3 sits at 0.975000000. At 12 stages a column at A = 1.5 sits at 0.997417616 and at 8 stages a column at A = 2 sits at 0.998043053.

None of those three columns is right or wrong. They are three different balances between capital and operating cost, and the relation is entirely indifferent to which one is chosen. What the relation will not let you do is pretend a starved column can be rescued with height, and that is the constraint the rest of this module is built on.

## What the surface does not carry

Nothing here prices anything. There is no capital cost of a tray, no cost of circulating a gallon, and no regeneration duty attached to a higher absorption factor. Those numbers come from the amine and glycol balances in the other modules of this tier, and putting the two halves together is the work. The surface tells you what is physically available. It says nothing about what it costs.
