# Dew point depression as the product

Two things move the outlet water number and they pull in opposite directions. The temperature fell, which dries the gas. The pressure also fell, which wets it. The engine can be asked each question separately, and the answer to the second one surprises most readers.

## Four states, one stream

| state | psia | degF | water the gas can hold, lb/MMscf |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| at the inlet | 1180.000000 | 96.000000 | 33.801656743 |
| cooled, but still at inlet pressure | 1180.000000 | 59.683517 | 10.176445163 |
| let down, but not yet cooled | 640.000000 | 96.000000 | 62.321804620 |
| at the cold separator | 640.000000 | 59.683517 | 18.762820770 |

The first and last rows are the real inlet and the real outlet. The middle two are the two halves of the change, each taken on its own.

## The third row is the point

Letting the gas down without cooling it would let it hold 62.321804620 lb per MMscf, against 33.801656743 at the inlet. The gas holds more water after the expansion than before it.

The reason is in the method. The saturated water content is the vapour pressure of water over the total pressure, converted to a mass. The vapour pressure depends on temperature alone. Drop the total pressure and hold the temperature, and the mole fraction of water rises because its numerator has not moved and its denominator has fallen.

So expansion does not dry a gas. Expansion cools a gas, and the cooling dries it. The second row says what the cooling alone buys with the pressure held at the inlet value: 10.176445163 lb per MMscf. The fourth row is the same cooling with the pressure let down as well, and it is the one the plant actually sees: 18.762820770 lb per MMscf.

## Why the product is a depression

This is why what a dew point skid sells is described as a depression rather than as an outlet water content. The outlet content is a consequence of the state the gas ends at, and that state is a pressure as well as a temperature.

The cooling on this let-down is 36.316483434 degF, and it is the property of the unit. The water content is the property of the state.

## Reading the table as an experiment

The four rows are a designed comparison rather than four unrelated answers. The first and fourth are the two states the plant has. The second and third exist only to isolate one change each, which is a thing an engine can do and a plant cannot.

That is worth noticing as a method, because the same shape works anywhere two inputs move together. Ask the engine for both single changes as well as the combined one, and the combined answer stops being a number you have to take on trust.

## The trade a designer faces

A deeper let-down buys more cooling and drops the pressure further, and the two effects on the outlet water content work against each other. The engine answers at any pair of states you give it, and the four-row reading above is the shape of the question to ask.
