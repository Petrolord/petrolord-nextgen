# What the forms leave out

Everything that actually happens between the divisions, and the two places to put it.

## The missing hours

Read the drilling form again. Interval divided by rate. There is no term in it for any of the following.

**Connections.** Every stand added is minutes of not drilling, and over a long section it is hours. The engine's cost per metre formula takes connection hours as a separate argument, 4 hours in its worked example against 100 drilling hours, but no activity duration has such a term.

**Surveys.** Stationary time at intervals down the hole, none of it in the rate.

**Bit changes within a section.** A trip is its own activity in a programme. A bit change halfway through a section that you did not plan is not.

**Hole problems.** Reaming, circulating bottoms up, working through a tight spot, fishing, sidetracking.

**Waiting.** On weather, on a boat, on a part, on a decision.

## The two places it goes

There are exactly two honest homes for all of it.

The first is a flat activity, for time you know about and can plan. A blowout preventer test, a logging run, a planned bit trip: name it, give it hours, put it in the list.

The second is the non-productive time allowance. It multiplies every productive duration by one plus a fraction, and it is the catch-all for what you cannot name in advance but know will happen.

## Read the allowance carefully

The golden programme carries a 12.5 percent allowance. It stretches 384 productive hours to 432 elapsed hours and adds 48 hours.

Now look at what that fraction means. Those 48 hours are 11.11 percent of the 432 elapsed hours, not 12.5 percent of them. The allowance is a stretch applied to productive time, so a fraction of 12.5 percent shows up as a smaller share of the finished schedule.

Get that backwards, by reading the fraction as a share of elapsed time, and you will consistently plan a shorter well than the one you have specified. The mistake grows with the fraction: at a 50 percent allowance the same misreading is worth 8 whole days.

## The honest position

The forms are not wrong. They are narrow on purpose. What makes an estimate honest is not a cleverer duration formula but a truthful list of flat activities and an allowance you can defend from real well records.
