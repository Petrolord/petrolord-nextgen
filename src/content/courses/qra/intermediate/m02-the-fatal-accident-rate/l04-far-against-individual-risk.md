# FAR against individual risk

{{panel:qr-societal}}

{{panel:qr-event-tree}}

By now this course has three ways to state how dangerous work is. The individual risk per annum follows one person through a year. The PLL counts expected deaths per year across a population. The FAR divides that PLL by the hours the population is exposed. They are easy to confuse because all three are small numbers about death, and each answers a different question. This lesson sets them side by side and says which question belongs to which measure.

## Three measures, three questions

| measure | the question | unit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| IRPA | how exposed is this one person over a year | per year |
| PLL | how many deaths are expected in this population each year | fatalities per year |
| FAR | how many deaths per hour of exposure, on a common base | per 100,000,000 exposed hours |

The IRPA is built from location-specific individual risk and occupancy. The PLL and the FAR are built from scenario frequencies and the expected number of deaths N. Both routes start from the same scenario frequencies and the same probabilities of death, stated inputs that come from the consequence course, but they combine them differently.

## Two facilities, two figures

The EREMOR operator from the Associate tier carries an IRPA of 0.000017541379 per year over 1000 hours on the process deck, 800 in the control room and 2560 in the accommodation. The JISIKE crew carries a PLL of 0.002420000000 fatalities per year and a FAR of 2.016667. These describe different facilities and different people, and this course computes no conversion between them. What they show is the shape of each answer: the IRPA is a per-person figure you would read against a person's roster, and the FAR is a group figure you would read against an industry's recorded rates.

## Where the hours differ

The IRPA divides hours at a place by the 8760 hours in a year, because it asks what share of the year a person spends exposed. The FAR sums the hours a group is exposed and uses them as the denominator directly, because it asks for a rate per 100,000,000 exposed hours. The same roster enters both, doing different work. An operator who doubled their hours on the process deck would carry twice the process deck contribution to their IRPA. A crew whose hours doubled with no change to the PLL would halve its FAR. That last sentence is the warning: a FAR can fall because the denominator rose, while nobody is any safer.

## What each hides

The IRPA hides the population: one person can carry a small individual risk while a scenario that could kill forty at once sits in the background. The PLL hides the distribution: a frequent event killing one and a rare event killing many can contribute equally. The FAR hides whoever is not on the clock, because visitors and neighbours have no exposed hours in it. That is why a full assessment reports all three, and why this tier goes on to the F-N curve, which shows how many die at once.

## Judging the figures

Whether an individual risk is tolerable is a question for the Expert tier, which bands it against published limits. This tier computes and compares the societal figures and bands nothing.

## Exercise

The JISIKE crew's hours are 2000 per person a year. Divide those hours by the 8760 hours in a year to find the share of the year each crew member is exposed, then write two sentences: one saying how that share would enter an IRPA, and one saying why the FAR of 2.016667 never uses it.
