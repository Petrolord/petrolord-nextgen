# What one decibel is worth

{{panel:hy-noise-dosimeter}}

One decibel on the TWA multiplies the OSHA noise dose by 1.148695 and the NIOSH noise dose by 1.258925. The engine measures both as the ratio of the noise doses at two TWAs one decibel apart. A decibel is worth about fifteen percent more allowance on OSHA and about a quarter more on NIOSH, and that is the single most useful fact for reading a control proposal.

The reason is the decibel exchange rate. On OSHA five decibels double the noise dose, so one decibel is the fifth root of that doubling. On NIOSH three decibels double it, so one decibel is the cube root, a bigger step.

## The ratio in the table

| TWA, dBA | OSHA noise dose, percent | NIOSH noise dose, percent |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 87.000000 | 65.975989 | 158.489319 |
| 88.000000 | 75.786283 | 199.526231 |
| 90.000000 | 100.000000 | 316.227766 |
| 95.000000 | 199.996999 | 1000.000000 |

From 87 to 88 dBA the OSHA noise dose rises from 65.975989 to 75.786283 percent. The ratio is the 1.148695 above. On NIOSH the same step goes from 158.489319 to 199.526231 percent, the ratio 1.258925. The ratio is the same whichever decibel you pick on a given criterion, because the formula is exponential in the TWA.

## Why this matters for controls

A control that takes three decibels off a TWA is worth three steps. On OSHA that is 1.148695 multiplied by itself three times, a factor a little over one and a half. On NIOSH it is 1.258925 three times over, which is a doubling almost exactly: the NIOSH noise dose halves. The same engineering change is worth more against NIOSH than against OSHA, because the NIOSH criterion counts each decibel as more allowance.

The ratio also warns against rounding. A TWA quoted to the nearest whole decibel carries up to half a decibel of rounding, and on NIOSH that is a noticeable fraction of the noise dose. This is one reason the course prints TWAs to six decimals and grades them at their printed precision.

## Small differences, large percentages

Readers new to decibels expect a change from 88 to 90 dBA to be a small change, because two out of ninety looks small. The table says otherwise. On OSHA it takes the noise dose from 75.786283 to 100.000000 percent. On NIOSH it takes it from 199.526231 to 316.227766 percent. A decibel scale is logarithmic, and the noise dose undoes the logarithm. Always turn a proposed change in level into a ratio of noise doses before you judge whether it is worth doing.

The same logic runs backwards. A new machine that adds one decibel to an area's TWA adds about a quarter to every NIOSH noise dose recorded there, and nobody standing in the room would notice the difference by ear.

Carry the ratios with you. They turn every decibel on a survey sheet into a share of an allowance, on whichever criterion the site works to.

## Exercise

Take the OSHA ratio of 1.148695 and multiply it by itself five times. Compare your result with the ratio of the OSHA noise doses at 95 and 90 dBA, 199.996999 over 100.000000 percent. Then do the same for NIOSH, raising 1.258925 to the third power and comparing it with 199.526231 over 100.000000 percent, and say which decibel exchange rate each result confirms.
