# Reading it on the gauge

Using the limit during an operation.

{{panel:wc-tolerance-explorer}}

## Where it appears

Next to the casing pressure box on the kill sheet, with the mud weight it applies to.

## The three moments it is checked

**At shut-in.** Compare the SICP against it. If the SICP is higher, the shoe is already taking fluid.

**During the kill.** The casing gauge is watched against it continuously, and the peak comes when the influx reaches the choke.

**Before starting.** The kick tolerance check asks whether the influx, expanded up to the shoe, would produce a casing pressure above it.

## What to do when it is approached

**Reduce the pump rate**, holding the casing pressure constant while the rate comes down. Less friction in the annulus means less pressure at the shoe for the same bottom hole pressure.

That is the only lever available that does not underbalance the well, and it is the standard response.

## What NOT to do

Open the choke to bring the casing pressure down.

That lowers the bottom hole pressure and admits more influx, which makes the casing pressure rise again with more gas in the hole. It is a feedback that goes the wrong way.

## When the rate reduction is not enough

The situation is beyond the standard procedure. The remaining options are all specialist: bullheading the influx back into the formation, setting a barite plug, or a relief well.

Each of them is a decision made by a well site supervisor with a specialist on the phone, and none of them is a calculation in this course.

## The margin people actually work to

Not the MAASP itself but a fraction of it, often 80 or 90 percent, so that the operator has room to act before the limit is reached.

That fraction is an operator's policy rather than a computed number, and it should be written on the sheet alongside the limit.

## The number this engine gives

The MAASP itself, at a stated mud weight and fracture gradient. The policy margin and the response to approaching it are procedural.
