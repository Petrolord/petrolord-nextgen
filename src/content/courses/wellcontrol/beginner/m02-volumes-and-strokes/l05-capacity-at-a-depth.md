# Capacity at a depth

The number that turns a volume into a height.

{{panel:wc-volume-explorer}}

## The quantity

The annulus capacity per metre at a stated depth, in square metres. It is the cross-sectional area of the annulus there.

## The two that matter

**At the bit**, because that is where the influx sits when the well is shut in. Pit gain divided by this capacity is the influx height.

**At the shoe**, because that is where the influx sits when it has been circulated up to the weakest point.

On this course's wells both are the same for the two wells, because the casing and the drill pipe are shared:

    at the bit:  0.013522756 m2
    at the shoe: 0.025517668 m2

## Why they differ by nearly two

Because the bit end has drill collars in it, which are 0.17145 m in a 0.2159 m open hole, and the shoe has drill pipe in it, which is 0.127 m in a 0.2204974 m casing.

A tight annulus around the collars and a wide one in the casing.

## What that does to an influx

A given volume of influx is TALLER at the bit than at the shoe, by the ratio of the two capacities.

Three cubic metres of influx is 221.8482706948928 m tall in the collar annulus and would be 117.56560409748478 m tall in the casing annulus. The same volume, and the ratio between the two heights is 1.8870168056206083.

## Why the height is what matters

Because the pressure the influx fails to provide is the density deficit times gravity times the HEIGHT. A tall influx is a large pressure deficit; a short one is a small one.

So the same pit gain is more dangerous when the influx is sitting in a tight annulus, which is where it always starts.

## The assumption underneath

That the influx occupies the annulus fully across its cross section, as a single continuous column.

Real influxes are dispersed, migrate at different rates in different parts of the annulus, and mix with the mud. The single-column assumption is the standard one and it is one of the model's stated limits.
