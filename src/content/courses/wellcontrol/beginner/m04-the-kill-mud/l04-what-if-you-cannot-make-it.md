# What if you cannot make it

Three situations where the kill weight is not achievable, and what happens then.

{{panel:wc-tolerance-explorer}}

## One: the kill weight would fracture the shoe

The kill mud is heavier than the mud the shoe can hold. Circulating it in would break the formation down at the casing seat.

That is a kick tolerance failure, and it is exactly what the Expert tier computes. The check is whether the mud weight at which kick tolerance reaches zero is below the required kill weight.

**What is done.** Not the standard procedure. The options are all worse: a bullhead squeeze, a barite plug, a cement plug, or drilling a relief well.

## Two: there is not enough barite on the rig

A logistics failure. It is more common than it sounds on a remote location.

**What is done.** The driller's method: circulate the influx out with the original mud while the kill mud is being brought in or built. The influx is removed and the well remains shut in on a heavier gradient than before.

## Three: the well will not take the mud

Lost circulation while trying to kill. The formation takes the kill mud, the annulus level falls, and the well goes underbalanced again while mud is being lost.

That is the worst case in this course: an underground blowout, where one formation is feeding another through the wellbore.

**What is done.** It is a specialist problem, and it is beyond anything this engine computes.

## The planning answer to all three

Set the casing before the kick tolerance runs out.

The mud weight at which kick tolerance falls to zero is a hard limit on how deep a section can be drilled, and it is computed at the design stage from the fracture gradient and the pore pressure prognosis.

A well designed with that check made properly does not reach any of the three situations above.

## What the calculation does NOT protect against

A pore pressure higher than predicted. The kick tolerance was computed against a prognosis, and if the formation is a great deal stronger than expected, the margin that looked adequate was not.

That is a geomechanics uncertainty rather than a well control one, and it is why an offset well's data is worth so much.
