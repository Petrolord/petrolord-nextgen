# Shut in, and why quickly

The one action, and the arithmetic behind the urgency.

## The action

Stop drilling, pick up off bottom, stop the pumps, close the blowout preventer, and read the pressures.

The order and the details vary between hard shut-in and soft shut-in procedures, and the principle does not: close the well before doing anything else.

## Why quickly

Because the influx keeps coming in as long as the well is underbalanced, and the well gets more underbalanced as the influx grows.

Suppose the pressure at the bottom is 200 kPa below the pore pressure. Influx enters and displaces mud. Every metre of annulus that becomes influx instead of mud reduces the bottom hole pressure by the density difference times gravity times that metre.

For a 1440 kg/m3 mud and a 240 kg/m3 gas, that is about 11.8 kPa per metre. So 17 m of gas in the annulus doubles the underbalance.

## The consequences of a bigger influx

**A higher shut-in casing pressure**, which may exceed what the casing shoe can take.

**More gas to expand** on the way out, which is the whole subject of kick tolerance.

**A longer, harder kill**, with more of the annulus to displace.

Every one of them scales with the volume that came in, and the volume that came in scales with the time between the well going underbalanced and the well being shut in.

## The number that matters

The pit gain at shut-in. It is the influx volume, and it is the single input that decides how bad the rest of the operation will be.

Rig procedures are written to minimise it: continuous monitoring, a flow check at every connection, a driller trained to shut in without asking, and a drill.

## Why not diagnose first

Because you cannot. The shut-in drill pipe pressure is the measurement that tells you the formation pressure, and it does not exist until the well is shut in.

Every minute spent deciding whether it is really a kick is a minute of influx, and the cost of shutting in on a false alarm is a few minutes of rig time.
