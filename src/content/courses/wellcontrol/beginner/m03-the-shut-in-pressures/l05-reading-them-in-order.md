# Reading them in order

Three numbers, and the sequence that makes them useful.

{{panel:wc-killsheet-explorer}}

## The three

**The shut-in drill pipe pressure.** Gives the formation pressure and therefore the kill mud weight.

**The shut-in casing pressure.** Gives, with the pit gain, the influx density.

**The pit gain.** Gives the influx volume and, with the annulus capacity, its height.

## The order to use them in

**First the SIDPP**, because it gives the number that ends the operation. Everything else is diagnosis; this one is the answer.

**Then the pit gain**, because it decides whether the standard procedure is safe. If the influx is larger than the kick tolerance, circulating it out normally will fracture the shoe.

**Then the SICP**, which tells you what you are dealing with and how much worse it will get on the way up.

## Why the SICP is third

Because it does not change what you do. A gas kick and a water kick of the same size are killed the same way, with the same kill mud weight and the same schedule.

What the SICP changes is the EXPECTATION: how much the casing pressure will rise as the influx comes up, and whether it will exceed the MAASP before it gets there.

That is planning for the operation rather than deciding it.

## What each of the three costs to get wrong

**SIDPP wrong:** the kill mud weight is wrong, and the well is either still underbalanced at the end or is overbalanced enough to fracture something.

**Pit gain wrong:** the influx height and density are both wrong, and the kick tolerance check is made against the wrong number.

**SICP wrong:** the influx density is wrong, which affects the expectation and not the procedure.

So the ranking of consequences matches the ranking of the reading order, which is not a coincidence.

## The numbers on the sheet

Of the twelve quantities the volume explorer computes, all twelve are known before the kick. Of the three here, all three are known only after it.

Fifteen numbers, twelve of them pre-filled. That is what makes a kill sheet completable in minutes under pressure, and it is why the standing part is kept up to date.
