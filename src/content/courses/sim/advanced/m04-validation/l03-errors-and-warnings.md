# Errors and warnings

The builder's validator has only errors. The simulator has both, and the distinction carries real information.

## The two categories

An **error** means the deck cannot be processed. Something is missing, contradictory or outside a required range, and there is no defensible way to continue.

A **warning** means the deck can be processed and something about it is unusual. The simulator will proceed, and it is telling you that it made a decision you might not have intended.

## Why a builder can refuse and a simulator cannot

A builder is upstream. It has a specification and it is producing a deck, so refusing costs the caller nothing but a corrected input.

A simulator is downstream of everybody. It receives decks written by hand, generated by six different tools and edited by people who have left. If it refused everything unusual, it would refuse most real decks. So it warns and continues, and the burden moves to the reader.

## What the warnings usually are

**Defaults applied.** A field left blank took its default value. This is the most common warning and the most ignored, and it is worth reading because a defaulted field is invisible in the deck text.

**Tables extrapolated.** A pressure or saturation outside a table's range, held at the end value. The Associate tier covered why that matters.

**Unusual but legal values.** A porosity of 0.45, a permeability of 20 darcies, a negative capillary pressure. Each is possible and each is more often a typo.

**Wells with no connections.** A well declared and never completed, which produces nothing and is usually an oversight.

## Why they get ignored

Because there are hundreds of them and most are harmless, so reading them all is expensive and reading none is cheap. Every simulation study has a log file full of warnings that nobody has read since the first run.

The practical response is triage rather than heroism. Read them once on the first run of a new deck, decide which classes are expected, and then watch for anything NEW. A warning that appears after a change is worth more than a warning that has always been there.

## The dangerous class

Warnings about things you did not know you were doing.

A defaulted three-phase relative permeability model. A table extrapolated over a range the run spends most of its time in. A well index computed with a defaulted direction flag on a horizontal well.

Each of those changes the answer materially and each is a warning rather than an error, because each is legal.

## What the builder's warnings look like

The composer emits warnings alongside the deck, not on the validation path. They describe modelling simplifications rather than input problems, in the same spirit as the waterflood engine's assumptions strings: statements that travel with the result.

A report that quotes a number from a run without its warnings has stripped the caveats off it.

## The misconception to avoid

"A run with no errors is a run that worked." Errors mean the deck could not be read. Everything else about whether the run means anything lives in the warnings and in the deck itself, and a clean error log is the weakest possible evidence of correctness.
