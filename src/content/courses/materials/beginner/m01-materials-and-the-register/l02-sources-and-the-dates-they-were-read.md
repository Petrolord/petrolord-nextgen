# Sources and the dates they were read

Every text this course leans on is named with its edition or date, its licence and the date it was read. That habit matters for a stock policy as much as for a lesson: a figure taken from a textbook is only as good as your ability to find the page again. Every text below was read on 2026-09-27.

## Why two texts are quoted and one is cited

Harris wrote the square-root lot size in a trade magazine in 1913, and those words are in the public domain. The course quotes them, always with the page of the 1990 reprint it read them in. The reprint's own typesetting belongs to its publisher, so only the 1913 words are used.

The MIT OpenCourseWare lectures are licensed CC BY-NC-SA 4.0. That licence is non-commercial, and this course is sold. So the course cites a lecture figure by its lecture and slide number, works the figure through its own calculator, and explains the idea in its own words. No slide, slide title or sentence of slide text is reproduced anywhere in the course. When you meet "lecture 8 slide 9" in a later lesson, that is a pointer to a page you can open yourself.

MIL-HDBK-338B is a US Department of Defense handbook in the public domain. The Expert tier reads it for spares.

## What was left out, and said plainly

Two well-known inventory textbooks are not publicly readable, so the course did not read them and cites neither. No licensed maintenance or reliability standard was read either. That is why the criticality criteria and weights in this course are the user's own stated policy, and why the course names VED (vital, essential, desirable) only as one scheme of that kind, described in its own words.

## The engine cites its sources too

Each function's result carries a basis block, and the source line in it names where the rule comes from. The EOQ basis, verbatim:

`Harris (1913), How Many Parts to Make at Once, Factory 10(2) pp. 135-136, 152; Caplice, MIT ESD.260J (2006) lecture 7 and lecture 8 slide 3`

The ABC basis, verbatim:

`Caplice, MIT ESD.260J (2006) lecture 11 slide 4 (standard ABC analysis; the classes are arbitrary, so the cut-offs are the caller's stated policy)`

Read that second line carefully. The source it cites supports the method; the cut-offs themselves are yours to state. Where a text leaves a choice open, the engine asks you for it.
