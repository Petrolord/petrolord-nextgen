# Entitlement and evidence

A claim succeeds on two things: an entitlement the contract gives, and evidence that the time or money was really lost because of the event. The contract manager tests both, in that order, and asks for what is missing before forming a view.

## Three things to check

The World Bank Contract Management Practice guidance (Second Edition, 2024), taught by concept as published practice, sets out three checks when assessing a claim (p.51):

1. the contract gives the supplier an entitlement to the time or cost claimed;
2. the extra time or cost was actually incurred and is reasonable in extent;
3. the buyer's default actually caused the loss claimed.

A claim that fails the first check fails, however good its evidence; one that lacks evidence for the second or third is incomplete, and the reply asks for what is missing.

## Knowing the allocation in advance

The same guidance expects the contract manager to know in advance which costs, possible extra costs included, sit with the contractor and which sit elsewhere (p.20). A contract manager who has mapped the risk allocation before any claim arrives can answer the first check quickly. On a reimbursable contract, for example, hours worked are paid at the stated rates; on a lump sum, most cost risk stays with the supplier unless a clause moves it.

## What evidence looks like

The World Bank guidance expects a claim to be backed by an analysis of its costs and by documents such as invoices, reports and records (p.51). On site that means timesheets, daily reports, permits, delivery notes and correspondence written at the time.

The buyer's own records matter as much. The guidance lists what to record: how the supplier performed and delivered, every communication and notice, the dates, and who was involved (Figure XIV, p.38).

## The buyer's own obligations

A World Bank case study (Figure XVI, p.40) describes an employer whose own late payments forced the contractor to suspend work; the employer's later attempt to rely on the contractor's default could not be defended. The lesson for claims is the same: before resisting a claim, check that Ekene met its own obligations.

## Exercise

EKC-06 is the synthetic instrument maintenance service with supplier IM-F (synthetic), reimbursed at stated hourly rates plus materials at cost, with a monthly service report. The scenario adds its own facts: IM-F claims extra hours for a month in which, it says, Ekene's work permits were issued late each morning and its technicians waited. The claim is one page with a total number of hours. Write the list of evidence you will ask IM-F for, and the Ekene records you will check yourself.

### A reading

First the entitlement: under a reimbursable contract, the question is whether the contract pays waiting time at the hourly rate and on what conditions. Then the evidence the World Bank guidance expects: timesheets showing the waiting hours per technician per day, the monthly service report, and an analysis linking each hour to a late permit. Ekene's own records to check: the permit log with issue times, the site attendance record, and any correspondence at the time. Causation is shown only if the late permits match the hours claimed.
