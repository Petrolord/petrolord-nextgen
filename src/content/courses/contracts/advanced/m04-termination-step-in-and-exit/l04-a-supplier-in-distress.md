# A supplier in distress

A supplier in financial distress can fail suddenly, and when it is critical the failure stops the operator's work. The Professional tier taught how to watch a supplier's financial standing. This lesson reads what a contract manager does when the warning signs appear, and how continuity is protected.

## Distress can outrun the accounts

The UK Corporate Financial Distress guidance note (2025 edition), openly licensed UK good practice, warns: "Financial decline can arise quickly, therefore an entity’s financial statements may not include signs of distress." (section 2.4, p.8). Its non-financial warning signs, which can show distress before published accounts do, include key supply chain partners refusing to keep trading and a supplier seeking to renegotiate contract terms (section 2.3.2); its indicator table places requests to be paid in advance and invoice discounting or factoring among the financial signs (Appendix 1, Table 1).

A contract manager often sees these signs first, in invoices, requests and late deliveries.

## Monitoring and reporting duties

The Sourcing Playbook (June 2023), a UK text, commits: "We shall therefore monitor the financial standing of our key suppliers on an ongoing basis." (Chapter 11, p.65). The UK Economic and Financial Standing guidance note (2026 edition) explains that under the UK Model Services Contract suppliers should promptly report a Financial Distress Event or anything that could cause one (paras 4.6.1 to 4.6.2).

## Continuity planning

The UK Resolution Planning guidance note (May 2021) applies to new critical service contracts, other outsourced service contracts worth more than £10m per year, and critical construction contracts, and has such contracts require the supplier to provide corporate resolution planning information and an insolvency continuity plan (paras 1.4.1 to 1.4.4). GovS 008 (version 2.2, issued 1 April 2026) states at 5.4.6: "For critical public services organisations shall have resolution plans." These are UK public sector rules; for Ekene they suggest a written plan for the day a critical supplier fails.

## Responding to the signs

When the signs appear, the contract manager records them, tells the contract owner, checks what the contract allows (reporting duties, step-in, assignment), reviews stock and alternative sources, and weighs any request to change terms against the contract and the evidence. A request for advance payment is itself one of the named warning signs.

## Exercise

EKC-04 is the synthetic casing and tubulars frame agreement with supplier TB-D (synthetic), the only supplier qualified for one premium connection the Ekene well designs use. The scenario adds its own facts: TB-D asks to be paid in advance for the next order, two deliveries were late because TB-D's mill held material pending payment, and TB-D proposes new unit prices. Write an early-warning note to the contract owner with your recommended actions.

### A reading

The note names three warning signs the UK Corporate Financial Distress guidance lists: a request for advance payment, a key supply chain partner refusing to keep trading, and a request to renegotiate terms. Because TB-D is a single source for a critical item, the risk is high. Actions: record the signs; ask TB-D for financial information under any reporting clause; check the frame agreement for assignment, step-in and security provisions; review Ekene's stock of the premium connection; start qualifying a second source; and treat any advance payment or price change as a decision for the contract owner, supported by evidence.
