# Single sources and continuity plans

Some supplies have only one source, and when it falters the operation waits. This lesson teaches how a contract team plans for a single source before it is tested.

Every text in this lesson was read on 27 September 2026.

## Why switching speed matters

The Sourcing Playbook (UK Cabinet Office, June 2023) names four factors in its contract tiering tool, among them "the speed and ease of switching suppliers". A single source scores worst on that factor by definition. The UK Contract Management Principles (publication page updated December 2025), principle 4, then direct the strongest resource to contracts where the risks are highest. Both are UK texts, read here as practice for Ekene.

## What a continuity plan answers

A continuity plan for a single-source contract answers four questions in writing:

1. **What stops** if the supplier cannot deliver, and how soon.
2. **What the contract provides**: the step-in rights, approval of key sub-contractors, and assignment and novation provisions the Sourcing Playbook, Chapter 5, lists as protections against supply chain risk, if the Ekene contract holds them.
3. **What the operator can do** inside its own operation to buy time.
4. **Who decides**, and at what trigger the plan is put into action.

GovS 008 (version 2.2, issued 1 April 2026), at 5.4.6, states for UK critical public services: "For critical public services organisations shall have resolution plans." The next lesson takes resolution planning further. For an Ekene single source, the principle is the same: the plan is written while it is not needed.

## Keeping the plan alive

The World Bank's Contract Management Practice guidance (Second Edition, 2024), taught by concept, expects the risk register to be reviewed regularly while the contract runs. A continuity plan is reviewed with it, and whenever the facts change: a new product qualifies with a second supplier, the design changes, or the single supplier's warning signs appear.

## What this lesson leaves to other courses

Two answers to a single source sit outside this course. Holding extra stock is a question of inventory and stock policy, taught in the materials course. Qualifying and competing a second supplier is a sourcing question, taught in the procurement course. The contract manager's part is to see the risk, record it, and ask for those decisions in time.

## Exercise

EKC-04 is the synthetic Ekene casing and tubulars frame agreement with TB-D, at stated unit prices per item, with delivery lead times stated per order. TB-D is the only supplier qualified for one premium connection the Ekene well designs use. The scenario adds its own fact: TB-D has told Ekene that a mill outage will delay orders for that connection by an unknown period.

Write the continuity note for the contract owner, answering the four questions.

### A reading

What stops: wells that need the premium connection cannot be cased, so their drilling dates are at risk. What the contract provides: the contract manager checks EKC-04 for sub-contractor approval or assignment terms and for anything on delivery failure. What Ekene can do: resequence wells so those needing the connection come later. Who decides: the contract owner, with drilling, at a trigger such as a confirmed delay date. The note asks the materials and procurement leads, in time, about stock and a second qualified source.
