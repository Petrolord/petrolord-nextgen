# Profit impact and supply risk

The oldest way to sort what a company buys asks two questions of each item. How much does it affect the result of the business? And how risky or complex is the market that supplies it? This lesson teaches that idea and a published cousin of it, then puts both to work on the synthetic Ekene contracts.

Every text in this lesson was read on 27 September 2026.

## The portfolio idea

Peter Kraljic set out the portfolio idea in the Harvard Business Review in 1983 (vol. 61, no. 5). The course teaches his article by concept only. The two questions give a grid of four groups:

* **strategic** items matter a great deal to the result and come from a difficult supply market;
* **bottleneck** items matter less to the result, yet their supply is hard to secure;
* **leverage** items matter to the result and many suppliers compete for them;
* **non-critical** items matter little and are easy to buy.

Each group gets its own buying approach and its own kind of relationship. A contract manager reads the grid across to the supplier who holds the contract.

## The World Bank's supply positioning model

The World Bank's guidance on Project Procurement Strategy for Development (Fourth Edition, August 2025) uses a close relative, the supply positioning model, which places each procurement by its relative supply risk and its value. The course teaches it by concept. Its four quadrants are:

* **Strategic Critical**: high cost and few suppliers;
* **Strategic Security**: low cost, and strategically important;
* **Tactical Advantage**: high cost and low risk, with many suppliers;
* **Tactical Acquisition**: routine and low value, with many suppliers.

The guidance usually counts a contract as high cost when its estimate reaches 1 percent of the project's total estimated procurement cost. That is World Bank practice for Bank-financed projects, and no threshold for an Ekene contract.

## Reading the grid with care

Neither model is a formula. Both ask for two judgements, written down. Two careful people can place the same supplier in different boxes, so the record carries the reason as well as the box.

Profit impact is read from what failure would cost the operation, which is often larger than the contract's price.

## Exercise

Using the portfolio idea, place two synthetic Ekene contracts and write one line of reason for each.

* EKC-04, casing and tubulars from TB-D (synthetic), the only supplier qualified for one premium connection the well designs use.
* EKC-03, camp catering and facility services from CF-C (synthetic), for a fixed monthly fee.

### A reading

EKC-04 sits in a difficult supply market because one supplier is qualified for the premium connection, and casing failure or delay stops a well, so its effect on the result is high. It reads as strategic; a team that judges the spend small may place it as bottleneck, and the reason line shows which judgement was made. EKC-03 is a service many suppliers could provide, with a modest effect on the result: non-critical, or leverage if its fee is large. In the supply positioning model the same reasoning puts EKC-04 among the strategic quadrants and EKC-03 among the tactical ones.
