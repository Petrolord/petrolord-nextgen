# Relationship models for each segment

This lesson matches relationship models to segments, and adds the supplier's view of the buyer.

Every text in this lesson was read on 27 September 2026.

## At one end, the contract alone

GovS 008 (version 2.2, issued 1 April 2026), at 6.2, accepts that some suppliers need no relationship management at all, typically for low cost, low risk purchases from a catalogue or a common framework. For those, checking delivery, paying correctly and keeping records is enough.

## At the other end, strategic supplier relationship management

The Sourcing Playbook (UK Cabinet Office, June 2023), Chapter 12, defines the practice for the few suppliers that matter most:

> This can be defined as the practices and behaviours adopted to engage more collaboratively with strategic suppliers to improve delivery of government objectives and increase mutual value beyond that originally contracted.

The test: it aims beyond what the contract already buys.

For complex projects the Playbook, also in Chapter 12, reports that a partnership model built on collaboration, openness, transparency and flexibility can help. Its critical success factors are a shared focus on delivering the service, clear roles, a shared understanding of how disputes are resolved, and a collaborative culture.

## A management system for collaboration

ISO 44001:2017 sets out a management system for collaborative business relationships between organisations. The course teaches it by concept only. Its stages run from awareness and choosing a partner, through working together and creating value, to planning how the relationship ends.

## How the supplier sees you

The World Bank's PPSD guidance (Fourth Edition, August 2025) adds supplier preferencing, taught here by concept. It places the buyer in one of four segments, Development, Core, Nuisance or Exploitable, by two measures: how much the buyer's business is worth to the supplier and how attractive that business is. A buyer of low value and low appeal is a nuisance; one of high value but low appeal is exploitable, open to short-term advantage. A buyer seen as core, or worth developing, tends to get more effort.

## Matching the model to the segment

Read together, the sources suggest a sketch, which each operator adapts:

* Non-critical contracts: the contract alone, as GovS 008 at 6.2 allows.
* Leverage contracts: firm contract management against the contract's terms.
* Bottleneck contracts: continuity first, the second factor of the UK tiering tool.
* Strategic contracts: the Playbook's collaborative practice, with a named senior owner.

## Exercise

EKC-01 is a well services call-off framework with WS-A (synthetic) for coiled tubing, nitrogen and pumping, with KPIs, a quarterly performance review and a Nigerian content plan. Ekene treats it as strategic. WS-A also serves three larger operators, and Ekene's call-offs are small and irregular.

Write a short note for the contract owner: where Ekene probably sits in WS-A's view, and one step that could improve it.

### A reading

Small, irregular call-offs from a buyer among larger customers are of low value to WS-A and costly to serve, so WS-A probably sees Ekene as a nuisance account, and a strategic label on Ekene's side does not guarantee WS-A's best crews. One step is to make Ekene's business more attractive within the framework: share a forward view of planned well work at the quarterly review, so WS-A can plan crews. The contract's terms stay as they are.
