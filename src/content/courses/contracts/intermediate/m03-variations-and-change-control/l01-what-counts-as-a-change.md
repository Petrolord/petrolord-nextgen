# What counts as a change

This module teaches how a change is recognised, authorised, assessed, limited and recorded. It starts with recognition, because much of the trouble begins when nobody noticed a change happening.

Every text in this lesson was read on 27 September 2026.

## The contract's own word first

Contracts use different words: variation, change order, change, amendment. The course follows the contract's own word first. "Modification" is the word of the UK Procurement Act 2023 (as enacted) for a change to a public contract after award. Whatever the word, a change alters what the contract requires, when, or for what price.

## The standard for change control

GovS 008 (version 2.2, issued 1 April 2026), at 5.4.5, sets the aim:

> Contract changes shall be justified and controlled to ensure the requirements continue to meet the organisation’s business needs and align with the organisation’s financial processes

This is a UK text, read here as practice: a change must be needed, and it must fit how money is controlled.

## Is it really extra?

The World Bank's Contract Management Practice guidance (Second Edition, 2024), taught by concept, puts one question before any price is discussed: was the so-called extra work already within the scope, and simply labelled as extra? Scope is read from the contract's documents in their order of precedence, as the Associate tier practised.

## Three things a change can touch

Under the UK guidance on Contract Modifications (Crown copyright 2024), a change is non-substantial if it does not lengthen or shorten the term by more than 10% of the maximum term provided at award, does not materially change the scope, and does not materially shift the economic balance in the supplier's favour. The figure is a UK rule for UK public contracts; the three things it watches are worth watching on any contract.

## Defective work is the contractor's cost

Nigeria's Public Procurement Act 2007 (Act No. 14, Official Gazette No. 65, Vol. 94, 19 June 2007) binds federal procuring entities; for an operator it is a published statement of public practice. Its s.53(3) speaks of a variation order requiring a contractor "at his own expense to repair, replace, or to do anything in his or her contract left undone or found to have been carried out with inferior or defective materials". Putting right the contractor's own defective work is the contractor's cost, whatever the order is called.

## Exercise

EKC-05 is the synthetic Ekene flowline replacement works by FW-E, for a lump sum paid against milestones, with a retention, a performance security and a defects period. The scenario adds two requests from FW-E in one week:

* extra payment to bury a section of flowline, which FW-E says was outside its scope, though the contract drawings show it buried;
* extra payment to cut out and remake a weld that failed its inspection.

For each, write one line: is it a change, and why?

### A reading

The burial is in scope if the drawings, read in the contract's order of precedence, call for it, and then it is paid within the lump sum. The failed weld is FW-E's own defective work, remade at its own cost and outside any paid change, as the logic of the Public Procurement Act 2007 s.53(3) shows for public contracts.
