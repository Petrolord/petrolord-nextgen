# Designing an indicator that drives the right behaviour

An indicator changes what a supplier pays attention to. A well-chosen one pulls effort toward the outcome the buyer wants. A poorly chosen one pulls effort toward the number. Most KPIs are set before award, but a contract manager reads them with this question in mind and brings the lessons to the next contract.

## Fewer, sharper indicators

The UK Sourcing Playbook (June 2023), Chapter 5, warns that "having too many KPIs (i.e. more than 10 to 15 per service) will lead to overcomplicated contracts and ambiguity with suppliers." It is UK practice. The point for any contract is that each indicator should earn its place: it measures something that matters to the outcome, and both sides can see how it is scored.

## Balance hard and soft measures

The UK Contract Management Principles (Crown Commercial Service, undated, page updated 12 December 2025) state principle 7:

> "Use a balanced scorecard to measure ‘hard’ data such as KPI performance alongside ‘soft’ measures e.g. customer satisfaction and relationship management, with a focus on achievement of outcomes."

Balance protects against gaming. If one indicator can be met by neglecting another part of the service, a second indicator should catch that neglect.

## What makes an indicator usable

An indicator a manager can use has four parts: a clear definition of what counts; a data source both sides accept; a period over which it is measured; and a target. If any part is missing, the score will be argued about.

## When an indicator does not fit

The UK Cabinet Office guidance on KPIs under the Procurement Act 2023 (April 2026 PDF) notes that the duty to set KPIs falls away where performance could not appropriately be assessed through them, and gives a one-off delivery of off-the-shelf goods as its example (para 10). The lesson carries over as practice: a simple supply may need an acceptance check and nothing more.

## Asking whether it worked

The Playbook, Chapter 13, puts the test plainly: "Did the KPIs drive the required behaviours and outcomes from the contract?" A manager who notices an indicator driving the wrong behaviour records it, so the next contract is written better.

## Exercise

EKC-06 is a synthetic instrument maintenance service. Supplier IM-F (synthetic) is paid at stated hourly rates plus materials at cost, and the contract states two KPIs: planned work completed, and repeat failures.

In this scenario, IM-F's planned work completed score has been high for three months, while field operators report the same instruments failing again after maintenance.

Write a short note to the contract file explaining what the two indicators together are telling you, and what you will record for the next contract.

### A reading

Each indicator alone can be met in a way that misses the outcome. A high planned work score can come from closing easy jobs quickly. The repeat failures indicator is the balancing measure that shows whether the work was done well. Read together, the pattern points to maintenance done fast and done poorly, which the manager raises with IM-F using the repeat failures evidence. For the file, the note records whether each indicator's definition, data source, period and target were clear enough, and asks, in the Playbook's words, whether the KPIs drove the required behaviours.
