# Why shale compacts

The frame you built in the last module has a floor and a ceiling and a wide gap between them. Nothing in it reads the well. To find out where inside the gap the pore pressure sits you need a measurement that responds to effective stress, and the sonic log is that measurement.

The chain is short. Effective stress squeezes porosity out of a rock. Porosity controls how fast sound travels through it. So transit time, which is what the sonic tool records, is a proxy for effective stress. Turn that around and a transit time that is too high for its depth is telling you that effective stress is too low for its depth, which by $\sigma = S - P$ means the pore pressure is high.

## Compaction is a one way process

Sediment arrives at the seabed as a loose mixture of grains and water. At the mudline in this well the modelled transit time is 656 us/m, which is close to a suspension, because most of the sample volume is water and sound in water is slow.

As more sediment piles on top, the grain framework carries more load. Grains rotate into tighter packing, water is expelled upward through the pore network, and porosity falls. Bulk density rises, which is the same story the density column of the last module told, running from 1900 kg/m3 at the mudline to 2505.265301734371 kg/m3 at TD. Velocity rises with it, so transit time falls, heading toward the matrix value of 220 us/m that the rock would have if all the porosity were gone.

Two features of this process make it useful for pressure work. It is driven by effective stress rather than by depth or by temperature, which is what lets you invert it. And it is essentially irreversible. Water squeezed out of a compacting clay does not come back and the grains do not loosen again, so the porosity of a shale records the highest effective stress it has ever experienced.

## Why shale and not sand

Sand is the rock you care about and shale is the rock you measure. There are three reasons for that.

Shale has very low permeability. When burial is fast, the water in a shale cannot escape as quickly as the load arrives, so the fluid takes up part of the load, effective stress stops rising, and compaction stalls. That stalling is exactly what produces overpressure, and it leaves its signature in the same rock that caused it. A sand at the same depth drains, so it carries no such record.

Shale compaction is repeatable. Across a basin, shales of similar mineralogy follow a similar porosity against effective stress path, which is what allows a single trend to be fitted and then used. Sands do not. A sand's velocity depends on cement, sorting, grain size and the fluid in its pores, and a gas charge alone can drop its velocity enough to imitate a large overpressure.

Shale is abundant. In a clastic section, most of the column is shale, so a shale based trend is defined over most of the depth range instead of at a handful of reservoir intervals.

## The trend and the departure

If a shale is normally pressured, its pore pressure equals the hydrostatic, so its effective stress is the overburden less the hydrostatic, and both of those rise smoothly with depth. Effective stress therefore rises smoothly too, porosity falls smoothly, and transit time follows a smooth curve down the well. That curve is the normal compaction trend, and the next lesson gives its equation.

The value of the trend is not the curve itself. It is the departure from it. Where the log sits on the trend, the shale has compacted normally and the pore pressure is hydrostatic. Where the log sits above the trend, at a higher transit time than the trend predicts, the shale is less compacted than its depth would require, which means it has been carrying less effective stress than its depth would require, which means the fluid is carrying the difference.

The golden well shows both cases. At 2500 m below the mudline the log reads 317.2847498247154 us/m and the well's own trend reads exactly the same 317.2847498247154 us/m. The log is on the trend, and the well is normally pressured there. At 3000 m the log reads 297.76677602422825 us/m while the trend reads 292.070315 us/m. The log is above the trend, and it stays above it to TD, where the log reads 270.92263512383806 us/m against a trend value of 259.553028 us/m.

That gap is the undercompaction signature. Converting it into a pressure in MPa is Eaton's method, and it is the work of the Intermediate tier. At this tier the job is to build the trend the departure is measured from, and to understand that a trend you got wrong turns into a departure you never had.

## Exercise

At 3000 m below the mudline the golden well's log reads 297.76677602422825 us/m and its normal compaction trend reads 292.070315 us/m. Say what that comparison tells you about porosity, about effective stress, and about pore pressure at that depth, in one sentence each.

Self check: the log transit time is higher than the trend, so the shale at 3000 m has more porosity than a normally compacted shale at that depth. More porosity means it has been carrying less vertical effective stress than a normally compacted shale would carry there. Since effective stress is the overburden less the pore pressure, and the overburden at that depth is fixed at 66.831143 MPa by the density column, a low effective stress means the pore pressure is above the hydrostatic value of 31.307730 MPa. The well is overpressured at 3000 m.
