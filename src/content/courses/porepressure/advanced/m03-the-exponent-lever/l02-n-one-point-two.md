# n equals 1.2

The graded number is one point; the calibration is a whole prognosis. This lesson runs the complete n 1.2 picture down the well, because the differences between the two calibrations are not uniform with depth, and the places they differ most are not where intuition puts them.

## The n 1.2 prognosis, down the ramp

Engine values for the low calibration, floor as EMW: 1029.8076923076924 kg/m3 at 2500 m, unchanged, since above and at the ramp top the ratio is 1 and every exponent returns hydrostatic. Then: 1056.6122938890458 at 3000 m, against n 3's 1095.6268524501886. At TD: 1091.881030400315 against 1179.1048116553065.

In pressure units the same story: overpressure 2.492970312778525 MPa at TD against 6, and at 3000 m 0.8139334257877301 MPa against 2, the anomaly quieted everywhere by the flatter power law.

The detected onset moves too: at n 1.2 the first sample past the 0.05 MPa rule is 2540 m, two samples deeper than n 3's 2520, because the quieter anomaly needs more ramp to clear the same threshold. Neither onset is the mechanism's 2500 m; both are detector properties, the Professional tier's lesson holding at a second calibration.

## Where the calibrations disagree most

In MPa the disagreement grows monotonically with depth: 0 at the ramp top, 3.507 MPa at TD. In mud units, the tier's language, it is worth tabulating the floor gap: 0 at 2500 m, 39.01 kg/m3 at 3000, 87.22 at 4000.

So the calibrations agree exactly where the well is normal, and disagree in proportion to how much anomaly there is to read. This has an underrated consequence for well planning: the exponent argument is invisible in the shallow section and maximal at the deepest, most expensive part of the hole. Two teams can agree about the whole upper well, sign the same casing plan for it, and then find their mud programs diverging just as the section costs peak. The time to surface the disagreement is at the prognosis, not at the weight-up decision.

## The window under the low calibration

Module 2's preview found the shape flip and it deserves its numbers here. Windows at n 1.2: 750.2832396352455 kg/m3 at 2500 m, 761.1573865123303 at 3000, 782.968235764595 at TD, widening below the onset, where n 3's narrowed from 750.28 to 724.82.

Mechanism: the window changes by the ceiling's climb minus the floor's. The ceiling's climb barely differs between calibrations, since it takes only a third of the floor's difference through the mixture. The floor's climb differs a lot, 62.07 against 149.30 kg/m3 over the ramp. At n 1.2 the floor climbs slower than the ceiling; at n 3, faster. Same well, same geometry, opposite trend in the one curve a section plan reads first.

The general point outranks this well: the exponent controls not just the level of the floor but the SLOPE of the floor through anomalous sections, and window shape inherits that slope. Any conclusion of the form the window narrows ahead is calibration-conditional and should be stated with its n attached.

## Reading a two-calibration plot honestly

The professional convention this module builds toward: plot both floors, label each with its exponent, and let the band between them BE the statement of calibration uncertainty. On this panel, flipping the control back and forth is that plot, animated. Three readings to practise. The pessimistic floor for mud purposes is the higher one, n 3 here, everywhere. The pessimistic floor for casing-seat spacing may be either, since seat logic uses slopes as well as levels. And the width between floors, 87 kg/m3 at TD here, is itself a deliverable: it prices what a measured pressure at depth would be worth before it is bought.

## Worked example

Compute the floor gap at 3000 m from first principles, checking the table. n 3 overpressure at 3000 m: 2.0 MPa exactly. n 1.2: budget 35.523412418439044 times $(1 - 0.9808693876607879^{1.2})$; the ratio's 1.2 power via logarithms is $e^{1.2 \times (-0.019315970318894725)} = e^{-0.02317916438267367} = 0.9770874088277273$; fraction 0.0229125911722727; overpressure 0.8139334257877301 MPa. Gap in pressure: $2.0 - 0.8139334257877301 = 1.1860665742122699$ MPa. Convert at 3000 m, divisor $9.80665 \times 3100 = 30400.615$: $1186066.5742122699 / 30400.615 = 39.0145585611433$ kg/m3, and the table's floors differ by $1095.6268524501886 - 1056.6122938890458 = 39.0145585611428$: agreement to ten decimals.

## Exercise

A colleague argues: since n 1.2 gives lower pressures, using it is conservative for the fracture side, because a lower floor means a lower ceiling and we will avoid breaking rock. Untangle this in three sentences.

Self check: the ceiling under n 1.2 is indeed lower, 1874.85 against 1903.92 at TD, but using a LOWER ceiling estimate is the conservative direction for avoiding losses, so that half of the claim is right for the wrong reason: it is conservative because it under-promises rock strength, not because low pressures are safe. On the floor side, n 1.2 is the ANTI-conservative choice: it authorises lighter mud, and if the true calibration is 3, that mud is 87 kg/m3 under the real floor at TD and the well kicks. Conservatism is directional per wall, and a single calibration cannot be conservative for both walls at once; that is why the honest object is the two-floor band, not a chosen line.
