# Which n is right

Two calibrations, 87 kg/m3 apart at the floor, and a well must be drilled at one mud weight. This lesson is the decision discipline: what evidence discriminates between exponents, how this well answers its own version of the question, and how to behave before the discriminating evidence exists.

## The hierarchy of evidence

From strongest to weakest, the sources that settle an exponent argument.

Direct pressure measurements in this well: wireline formation pressures in permeable beds, or a well test. One good point in the anomalous section does more than any argument, as module 3's worked pricing showed, 3.05 MPa of separation between the calibrations' predictions at 3800 m against instrument accuracy far better than that.

Drilling events, read quantitatively: a connection-gas trend that starts at a depth, a kick with a measured shut-in pressure, mud-weight history against hole behaviour. A shut-in drillpipe pressure is a pore pressure measurement paid for the hard way.

Offset-well calibration: exponents fitted where neighbouring wells had measured pressures, imported with the usual caveat that the import assumes shared mechanism and shared trend quality.

Mechanism reasoning: undercompaction responds well to the classic sonic exponent; velocity-derived prognoses in gentler-response basins motivated the low calibrations. Weakest of the four, but not empty, and it is the one this tier's module 5 arms properly, because Bowers' two curves are precisely a mechanism instrument.

## How this well answers

This well affords two of the four, and they agree.

The loop: at n 3 the recovered overpressure matches the encoded ramp to a hundred-millionth of a pascal at all 401 samples; at n 1.2 the recovery misses by up to 3.5 MPa. As calibration evidence, the loop is this well's equivalent of a measured pressure at every sample, and it selects 3 without residue.

The second method: Bowers' loading curve, fed the TD velocity, returns a pore pressure 0.038 MPa from the n 3 Eaton value, module 5's centrepiece. Two methods with different mathematics converging on 47.4 is independent endorsement; the n 1.2 value of 43.9 sits 3.5 MPa from the Bowers answer.

So on the golden well, n 3 is not a preference, it is the finding. The capstone still grades the n 1.2 number because computing the alternative correctly IS the skill: a calibration argument conducted between people who cannot each run both candidates cleanly is noise.

## Before the evidence: how to carry two exponents

The working protocol for the undiscriminated case, assembled from the modules behind you. Publish the band, both floors labelled, not a compromise line: an average of 1179 and 1092 describes no calibration and drills no well. Design to the conservative wall per decision: mud floor from the high calibration, ceiling commitments from the low one, so that either truth leaves the plan safe. Price the band in mud units at each section boundary, since the throw is depth-dependent. Name the discriminator in advance: which measurement, at which depth, moves the program to which calibration, module 3's worked example formalised as a plan. And re-fit rather than switch: when the point at 3800 m reads 42.0, the honest response is an exponent fitted to it, near 1.46 on this well's machinery rather than exactly 1.2, and a re-run of everything downstream.

That protocol is this course's answer to the deepest habit the tier teaches: uncertainty is carried explicitly, spent deliberately, and retired by measurement, never by assertion.

## Worked example

Fit the exponent to the hypothetical 42.0 MPa point at 3800 m, closing module 3's loop. Engine inputs at that sample: overburden 86.21902323993118 MPa, hydrostatic 39.388409725, so the budget is 46.83061351493119 MPa; trend 264.5959141279743 us/m over a log of 275.1833451254513, ratio 0.9615259019667401. Observed overpressure: $42.0 - 39.388409725 = 2.611590275$ MPa; observed handover fraction $2.611590275 / 46.83061351493119 = 0.0557667320366696$. Solve $1 - r^n$ equal to that fraction: $n = \ln(1 - 0.0557667320366696) / \ln(0.9615259019667401) = 1.4625673207936873$. The point endorses not 1.2 but a refit near 1.46, between the presets, which is the normal outcome: presets bracket, data interpolates.

## Exercise

State, in two sentences each, what each of the following observations would do to the exponent argument on a real well shaped like this one: a kick at 3900 m with shut-in pressures implying 46.8 MPa; connection gas rising steadily from 2550 m; and a clean RFT at 2400 m reading exactly hydrostatic.

Self check: the kick is a direct measurement endorsing the high calibration, 46.8 sits near the n 3 curve's 45.998 at 3900 m and far above the n 1.2 curve's, and after the well is secured the refit pins n slightly above 3, since 46.8 exceeds even the n 3 prediction there. The connection-gas onset near 2550 m supports the ramp top's reality and the detection lesson's geometry, but constrains the exponent only weakly, since both calibrations put the onset within a few samples of there. The hydrostatic RFT at 2400 m confirms the normal section and the trend's quietness check and says NOTHING about the exponent, because above the onset every exponent predicts the same hydrostatic; evidence discriminates only where the calibrations disagree, which is the module's closing thought.
