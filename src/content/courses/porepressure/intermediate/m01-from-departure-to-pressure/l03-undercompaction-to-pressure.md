# Undercompaction to pressure

The Associate tier explained why a slow shale is suspicious: if the water in a compacting shale cannot escape as fast as the load arrives, the fluid carries part of the load, effective stress stops rising, compaction stalls, and the rock stays more porous and slower than its depth would suggest. This lesson turns that story into the equation the tier runs, because Eaton's relation is that story written in symbols.

## Effective stress is the hinge

Terzaghi's principle says the total vertical stress at a depth is shared between the grain framework and the pore fluid:

$$S = \sigma_e + PP$$

The overburden $S$ you integrated at the Associate tier is the total. The effective stress $\sigma_e$ is the part the grains carry, and it is the part that compacts them. The pore pressure $PP$ is the rest.

Rearranged, $PP = S - \sigma_e$. The overburden is known from the density column. So a pore pressure estimate is really an effective stress estimate in disguise: find how much stress the grains are carrying, subtract from the total, and the remainder is the fluid's share.

## Velocity measures effective stress

How do you measure what the grains carry, three kilometres down, from a wireline log? Through compaction itself. Grains carrying more stress are packed tighter; tighter packing means less porosity; less porosity means faster rock. The sonic log is, indirectly, an effective stress gauge.

A normally pressured shale at depth $z$ carries a known effective stress: the overburden less the hydrostatic, $S - P_h$. Its transit time is whatever the normal trend says, $\Delta t_n(z)$. An overpressured shale at the same depth carries less effective stress, so it is undercompacted, so its measured transit time $\Delta t$ is higher than $\Delta t_n$.

The comparison of the two transit times is therefore a comparison of two effective stresses: the one the rock should carry and the one it does.

## Eaton's step

Eaton's method makes that comparison quantitative with a single empirical assumption: the ratio of actual to normal effective stress is the transit-time ratio raised to a power,

$$\frac{\sigma_e}{\sigma_e^{normal}} = \left( \frac{\Delta t_n}{\Delta t} \right)^n$$

Substitute $\sigma_e^{normal} = S - P_h$ and $PP = S - \sigma_e$ and you get the working form:

$$PP = S - (S - P_h)\left( \frac{\Delta t_n}{\Delta t} \right)^n$$

Read it as a machine with three moving parts. The ratio $\Delta t_n / \Delta t$ is the evidence: 1 where the log sits on trend, below 1 where the rock is slow. The gap $S - P_h$ is the budget from the last lesson: the most pressure the well could possibly be hiding at that depth. The exponent $n$ is the amplifier that says how strongly a given slowness should be read as pressure. On trend, the ratio is 1, the whole budget stays with the grains, and $PP = P_h$ exactly: the method returns the hydrostatic without being told to.

## What kind of equation this is

It is worth being clear about the epistemic status of each piece, because they are not alike.

Terzaghi's partition is physics. The overburden and hydrostatic are arithmetic on measured densities, as solid as anything in the well. The claim that velocity responds to effective stress is well-established rock physics.

The specific power-law form, and the exponent $n = 3$, are empirical. Eaton calibrated them to Gulf of Mexico wells, and they have travelled well, but nothing derives them. On this synthetic well $n = 3$ is exactly right because the well was built with it, and the tier exploits that to show the machinery working perfectly. On a real well the exponent is a calibration parameter, and the Expert tier grades what happens when it is chosen differently.

One more boundary: the method reads undercompaction. Overpressure generated by mechanisms that do not leave a compaction signature, or velocity anomalies that are not pressure at all, are outside the equation's sight. Lesson 5 of the next module maps that blind zone.

## Worked example

Take the equation to the two ends of its range at total depth, where $S = 91.12306695073282$ MPa and $P_h = 41.408579625$ MPa.

On trend, ratio 1: $PP = 91.123067 - 49.714487 \times 1 = 41.408580$ MPa. Hydrostatic, exactly.

Ratio 0.9580337483265022, which is this well's actual ratio at TD, with $n = 3$: the ratio cubed is 0.8793108342707514, so $PP = 91.123067 - 49.714487 \times 0.879311 = 47.408580$ MPa. That is the graded pore pressure at total depth, and the 6 MPa above hydrostatic is the encoded ramp.

## Exercise

A colleague says: Eaton's method computes pore pressure from the sonic log. Improve that sentence in two or three sentences of your own, naming what the sonic actually measures and where the pressure number really comes from.

Self check: the sonic measures transit time, which responds to porosity, which responds to the effective stress the grain framework has been carrying. Eaton's method uses the transit-time ratio to estimate how far effective stress falls short of its normal value, and then gets pressure by subtraction from the overburden, which comes from the density log, not the sonic. The pressure number is therefore built from two logs and a trend: the sonic supplies the evidence of undercompaction, the density supplies the total stress being shared, and the trend defines what normal means.
