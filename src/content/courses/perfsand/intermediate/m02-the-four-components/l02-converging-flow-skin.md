# Converging-flow skin

The vertical part of the problem, and the one term that shot density really attacks.

{{panel:ps-skin-explorer}}

## What it is

Fluid arriving from the reservoir has to reach a perforation, and the perforations are discrete points along the well rather than a continuous slot. So the flow has to converge vertically as well as radially, and that convergence costs pressure.

The term is always positive. There is no arrangement of discrete holes that converges more easily than a continuous open cylinder.

## What it depends on

Two dimensionless groups, and the correlation is a fitted power law in them.

The dimensionless spacing is the perforation spacing divided by the tunnel length, times the square root of the permeability anisotropy. It says how far the fluid has to travel vertically relative to how far the tunnel reaches.

The dimensionless perforation radius is the tunnel radius divided by twice the spacing, times one plus the square root of the inverse anisotropy. It says how much of the vertical interval the tunnel actually intercepts.

## Why the anisotropy appears twice

Because it does two different things. A low vertical permeability makes the vertical journey harder, which raises the dimensionless spacing. And it changes how effectively a tunnel of a given radius drains vertically, which enters the dimensionless radius.

They pull the same way. More anisotropy is more converging-flow skin, and on the published gun going from isotropic to ten to one roughly triples it.

## What moves it

Shot density, hard. Halving the spacing lowers the dimensionless spacing and raises the dimensionless radius, and both changes reduce the term.

Tunnel length, also hard, and in the same direction as everything else: a longer tunnel lowers the dimensionless spacing directly.

Entrance hole, gently, through the dimensionless radius.

## Where it dominates

In a laminated or strongly anisotropic reservoir with a low shot density. The published through-tubing gun at four shots per foot in a three to one anisotropy carries a converging term nearly three times the high-shot-density gun's, and the difference is almost entirely the density.
