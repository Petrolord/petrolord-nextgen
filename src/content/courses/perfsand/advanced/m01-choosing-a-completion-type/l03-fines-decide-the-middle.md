# Fines decide the middle

A result from running five sands through the ladder, and what it says about where to spend measurement effort.

{{panel:ps-sand-explorer}}

## The experiment

Five sieve curves, chosen to land on the four rungs, run through the ladder. Their uniformities rise steadily from about one and a half to about six, and their fines from a few tenths of a percent to twenty two.

## The result to look at

The middle two. One has a uniformity of about three point four and four percent fines and gets a premium screen. The other has a uniformity of about three point six and seven percent fines and gets a gravel pack.

Their uniformities differ by about a fifth of a point, which is well inside the scatter of a sieve analysis. Their fines differ by three points, and that is what moved the answer.

## Why the middle behaves that way

Because the uniformity thresholds are three and five, and both sands sit comfortably between them. Neither is near a uniformity boundary, so the uniformity has nothing left to decide.

The fines thresholds are two, five and ten, and both sands sit near the five. So the fines are the number the rule is actually reading.

## Where the uniformity takes over

At the ends. The cleanest sand in the set has a uniformity of about one and a half, which clears every threshold, and the poorest has about six, which fails the last one on uniformity alone whatever its fines are.

So the ladder is uniformity-driven at the extremes and fines-driven in the middle, and most real sands are in the middle.

## What that means for the laboratory

The fines number is the one to get right. It is also the one that is hardest to measure well: it depends on how the sample was handled, whether the fines were washed out during coring, and where the sieve stack stopped.

A programme that spends its money on more sieves at the coarse end and stops at a hundred microns has optimised the wrong end of the curve.
