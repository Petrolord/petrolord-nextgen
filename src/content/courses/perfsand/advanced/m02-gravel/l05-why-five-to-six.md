# Why five to six

What sets each end of the band, and what would move them.

{{panel:ps-sand-explorer}}

## The lower end

Below about five times, the pack is too fine. Its own pore throats are small, its permeability is low, and the pressure drop across the pack becomes a meaningful part of the drawdown.

A gravel pack is supposed to be transparent to the flow. A pack contributing its own skin has defeated the purpose, and the extra drawdown needed to overcome it feeds straight back into the sanding calculation as a worse case.

## The upper end

Above about six times, the pack lets the formation in. Formation grains enter the pack pore throats and either travel through it, in which case the well produces sand through a sand control completion, or lodge in it, in which case the pack plugs from the inside.

Plugging is the worse of the two, because it is not recoverable and it raises the drawdown.

## What would move the band

A very uniform formation could tolerate a coarser pack, because there is little fine material to migrate. A poorly sorted one needs a finer pack or a different approach entirely, which is what the ladder already said.

So the five to six band is implicitly for a formation of moderate uniformity, and the two rules of this course, the ladder and Saucier, are less independent than they look.

## What is not in the band

Any statement about the screen. Saucier sizes gravel against formation and stops. The screen is sized against the gravel in the next module, and that is a separate rule with a separate basis.

Any statement about placement. A perfectly sized gravel that has not been placed, because the pack bridged prematurely or the annulus was not filled, is not a gravel pack. Placement is most of the operational risk and none of this calculation.

## What to take away

The band is an empirical compromise between two failure modes that pull in opposite directions, and knowing which failure each end guards against is what lets you choose a direction when no commercial cut fits.
