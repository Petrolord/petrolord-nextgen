# The margin you actually get

Computing the difference between the bound and the chosen gauge, and why it is worth having.

{{panel:ps-sand-explorer}}

## The quantity

The smallest gravel grain, less the chosen gauge. In microns, it is the amount by which the screen is finer than it strictly had to be.

## Why compute it

Because it varies enormously between gravels and it is not a decision anybody made. It is the leftover from fitting a continuous requirement onto a discrete series.

A gravel whose smallest grain falls just above a standard gauge gets a small margin. One whose smallest grain falls just below the next gauge up gets a large one.

## What a large margin costs

Open area. A finer slot has less flow area per unit length of screen, which means a higher velocity through the slots at the same rate, which means more erosion and more pressure drop.

So a large margin is not free conservatism; it is a real cost paid for by the discreteness of the series.

## What a small margin costs

Risk. The gravel catalog's smallest grain is a nominal sieve opening, and a real vendor certificate has a tail below it. A screen sized with almost no margin against a nominal bound will pass some of the real tail.

So neither end is comfortable, and knowing which end you are on is the point of computing the number.

## Why it is a good graded quantity

Because it cannot be looked up. The gauge is a catalog value and the bound is a catalog value, but the difference is neither, and getting it right requires having done both selection steps correctly: the gravel from the sand, and the gauge from the gravel.

A learner who reports the right margin has demonstrated the whole chain.

## What to do with it in practice

Ask the vendor for the actual sieve certificate of the actual pack, and recompute the bound from the real smallest grain rather than the nominal one. The margin then means something.
