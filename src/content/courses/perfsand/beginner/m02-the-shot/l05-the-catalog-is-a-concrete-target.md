# The catalog is a concrete target

Where the nine rows come from, what they are good for, and what they are not.

{{panel:ps-shot-explorer}}

## What the rows are

Nine gun classes, from a one and eleven sixteenths inch through-tubing gun to a seven inch big-hole casing gun. Each carries a carrier outside diameter, a shot density, a phasing, an entrance hole and a penetration.

Every row is published-typical for its class, in the style of an API RP 19B Section One report, and every row is flagged approximate.

## What an API Section One test is

A standardised test in which a gun is fired into a concrete target of specified strength, and the entrance hole and total penetration are measured. It is a comparison standard: it lets two vendors' charges be compared on equal terms.

It is not a downhole measurement. The target is unconfined concrete of a standard strength, at surface, at ambient temperature. A reservoir is confined rock at depth and temperature, and penetration there is generally less.

## Why the course uses the catalog anyway

Because the structure it teaches is real. Through-tubing guns are small, low density and in-line. Casing guns are larger, denser and phased. Big-hole charges trade penetration for hole size. Those relationships hold for every vendor's catalog because they follow from the physics of the charge and the size of the carrier.

The nine rows carry that structure and a set of plausible numbers. Substituting a real data sheet changes the numbers and leaves the structure.

## What the flag on every row means

That the row may be used to learn the method, to compare classes, and to see which lever matters. It may not be used to order a gun, to promise a client a penetration, or to argue with a vendor.

A real design uses the manufacturer's charge data sheet for the actual gun, the actual charge and the actual casing weight, because the casing the jet has to cross first is part of the system.

## The correction nobody in this course applies

There are published methods for converting an API concrete penetration into an expected formation penetration, using the rock strength and the effective stress. This engine applies none of them, and takes the penetration it is given.

That is a scope decision and it is stated so the reader can apply the correction outside the tool rather than assume it has been applied inside.
