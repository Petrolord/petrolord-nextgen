# Sorting

A second spread number over a wider span, and why the course computes it but does not decide on it.

{{panel:ps-shot-explorer}}

## The definition

The sorting coefficient here is D10 divided by D95.

Same idea as the uniformity, over a much wider span: the coarse decile against the fine twentieth rather than the fortieth against the ninetieth.

## What it adds

The uniformity looks at the middle of the distribution and is fairly insensitive to the tails. The sorting looks at the tails and is dominated by them.

Two sands can share a uniformity and differ enormously in sorting, if one of them has a long fine tail and the other does not. The published sand has a uniformity of a little over three and a sorting of over eleven, which says its middle is moderately graded and its tails are wide apart.

## Why the ladder does not use it

Because the tails are exactly where a sieve analysis is least reliable.

The coarse end of a stack often has very little material on it, so D10 is computed from a percentage that is a few grams. The fine end suffers the opposite problem: the finest sieve catches everything below it, so D95 depends on where the stack happened to stop.

A decision rule built on a ratio of two badly determined numbers would be a decision rule with a lot of scatter in it.

## Why compute it at all

Because it is free, because parts of the literature quote it, and because a large gap between the uniformity and the sorting is a signal worth seeing. It says the distribution has tails that the uniformity is not describing, and it tells you to look at the fines number carefully.

On the published sand the gap is nearly four to one, which is exactly the situation where the fines figure earns its place.

## The general point

A model can compute a quantity, report it, and decline to make a decision from it. That is not indecision; it is a statement that the quantity is worth seeing and not worth betting on.

The alternative, which is to feed every computed number into the decision rule because it is available, produces rules that are sensitive to the worst-measured inputs.
