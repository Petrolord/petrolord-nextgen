# The hole in the casing

What a perforation physically is, and the four dimensions the rest of the course reduces it to.

{{panel:ps-shot-explorer}}

## What happens

A shaped charge is a cone of metal backed by explosive. When it fires, the liner collapses into a jet travelling at several kilometres a second. The jet punches through the gun carrier, the casing, the cement and into the formation.

It does not burn or melt its way through. It is a hydrodynamic penetration: at those velocities steel and rock behave more like very stiff fluids than like solids.

## What is left behind

A roughly conical tunnel, wide at the casing and tapering to a point in the rock. A hole through the casing wall, which is what the entrance hole diameter measures. And a ring of damaged rock around the tunnel, compacted and shattered by the jet, whose permeability is a fraction of the rock it came from.

That last one is the crushed zone, and it is one of the four skin components the next tier computes.

## The four dimensions

Everything after this lesson reduces the tunnel to four lengths.

The tunnel length, which is how far the jet reached beyond the cement. The tunnel radius, which is taken as half the entrance hole. The spacing between successive shots along the well, which is the reciprocal of the shot density. And the wellbore radius, which the well already had.

Nothing else about the geometry enters the skin calculation. Not the taper of the tunnel, not the shape of its tip, not the roughness of its wall.

## Why that reduction is defensible

Because the flow problem those four lengths describe is the one that matters. Fluid converges from the reservoir into a set of small cylinders spaced along a large one, and the resistance of that geometry depends on how long the cylinders are, how fat they are, how far apart they are and how big the pipe is.

The taper and the tip contribute at the margins. The four lengths contribute the answer.

## Exercise
