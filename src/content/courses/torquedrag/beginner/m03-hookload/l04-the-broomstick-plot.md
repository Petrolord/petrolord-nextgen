# The broomstick plot

The picture the three hookloads make, and how to read it.

{{panel:td-string-explorer}}

## What it is

Tension plotted against measured depth, three curves on one chart: pick up, rotate off bottom, slack off.

It is called a broomstick because the three curves start together at the bit end and fan apart going up, like the bristles of a broom.

## Where they separate

They separate exactly where there is side force, and nowhere else.

Open the panel on the vertical well: the three curves lie on top of one another for their whole length, because there is no side force anywhere. Switch to the slant well and they start together at the bit, stay together through the vertical section above it, then fan apart from the kick-off upward.

That is the plot's real value. The GRADIENT of the gap between the curves is the local friction contribution, so the plot shows you where in the well the drag is being generated rather than just how much of it there is.

## Reading the slope

In a section with no side force the three curves are parallel: all three are gaining tension at the same rate, w cos(theta) per metre, and none of them is gaining friction.

In a section with side force the pick-up curve climbs faster than the rotating one and the slack-off curve climbs slower. The wider the fan opens over an interval, the more that interval is contributing.

## The zero line

The chart draws a line at zero tension. Any curve crossing it has gone into compression below that point.

On the build-and-hold well tripping in, none of them does. On the horizontal well tripping in, the slack-off curve crosses zero and keeps going, reaching -281944.752574833 N. At that point the plot has stopped describing a real string, and the next lesson is about why.

## What a real broomstick looks like

On a real well the three curves are the MEASURED hookloads at each connection, plotted against the depth at which they were taken, with the modelled curves overlaid.

The comparison is the whole point. Matching curves means the friction factors are right and the hole is behaving. A measured pick-up curve that starts to climb away from its model while the slack-off curve does not is a specific diagnosis, and it is different from both curves moving together.

## The trap

The plot is not a picture of the hole. It is a picture of the tension in a string that is currently in the hole, computed on assumptions.

Two wells with very different geometries can produce similar broomsticks. The plot tells you what the string is experiencing, not what the hole looks like, and the survey listing is where the geometry lives.
