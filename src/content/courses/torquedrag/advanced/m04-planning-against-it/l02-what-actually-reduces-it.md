# What actually reduces it

Five levers, in order of how much they move the answer.

## The model again

    wear volume = wear factor x side force x sliding distance

Three terms, so three families of lever, plus the geometry that turns volume into depth.

## The wear factor: the biggest lever

Plausible values span a factor of twenty, and the volume is proportional to it.

**Casing-friendly hardbanding.** A tungsten carbide band that protects the tool joint can be aggressive against casing. Materials specifically formulated for low casing wear exist and are chosen for this reason.

**Mud.** Solids content and lubricity both move it. Cleaner mud and an oil-based system give a lower factor.

**Non-rotating protectors.** Sleeves that sit on the drill pipe, contact the casing and do not turn with the string. They remove the sliding entirely at their location, which takes the factor to nearly zero there.

## The side force: the design lever

Set by the trajectory, and mostly fixed by the time drilling starts.

**Move the dogleg out of the casing.** A kick-off below the shoe rather than above it removes the curvature term from the cased section entirely.

**Reduce the dogleg severity.** Side force is proportional to it.

**Set the shoe away from the build.** The wear here concentrates just above a shoe that sits at the top of a build.

## The sliding distance: the operational lever, and a weak one

Proportional to revolutions, which is footage divided by rate of penetration times rpm.

**Fewer revolutions per foot** means a higher rate of penetration or a lower rpm, and the two are usually coupled: dropping the rpm drops the rate of penetration and the total revolutions barely move.

**Less back reaming**, which is rotating hours with a high side force and no footage at all.

That second one is real and controllable, and it is the reason back reaming has a cost beyond rig time.

## The geometry: not a lever

The crescent is what it is. It does mean that reducing the volume by half reduces the depth by rather less than half, so the payoff from the levers above is smaller in depth terms than in volume terms.

## The ranking

Wear factor first, by a wide margin. Trajectory second, and only if you are still designing. Back reaming discipline third. Rpm last and nearly useless on its own.

That ranking is worth stating because the instinctive response to a wear prediction is to reduce rpm, which is the lever that moves the answer least.
