# The surface of one tube

One tube is a cylinder, so its surface is pi times its outside diameter times its length. The diameter is given in inches and the length in feet, so the diameter is divided by twelve before the multiplication. That is the whole calculation, and there is no judgement anywhere in it.

## Two sizes in this course

Across the seven cases this course carries for tube counts, only two per-tube surfaces appear. The studio case and four of the published cases are 3.141593 ft2 a tube. ORON and one published case are 5.235988 ft2 a tube.

Two values across seven cases is a property of the cases rather than of the engine. Each one was set up on one of two tube sizes, and a per-tube surface is fixed the moment a diameter and a length are chosen. Nothing about the duty, the streams or the coefficient reaches it.

## It is the outside surface

The diameter in that expression is the outside diameter, and this matters more than it sounds. The area the division produced is an outside tube surface, because the coefficient it was divided by is referred to the outside tube surface. So the per-tube surface has to be an outside surface too, or the count would be a count of the wrong thing.

Use an inside diameter by mistake and the per-tube surface comes out smaller, so the count comes out larger. The error is quiet, because a larger tube count looks like a conservative answer rather than a wrong one. This is the same discipline as naming the reference area on a coefficient, applied one step further down the chain.

## Nothing here is fitted

It is worth noticing what this step does not contain. There is no correlation, no exponent and no constant that somebody measured. Pi is pi. The twelve turns inches into feet. If the diameter and the length are right then the per-tube surface is right, and there is nothing in it to be uncertain about.

That is unusual in this module and it will not last. The bundle diameter a tube count also returns is built from constants whose source this module records that it cannot establish, and it says so on every answer. Knowing which steps are arithmetic and which steps are fitted is most of what this course is teaching.

## Length is the lever

One more thing follows straight from the expression. Surface goes up in proportion to length, so a longer tube carries more surface and a given area needs fewer of them. Diameter works the same way. Neither is free: a longer tube needs a longer shell and a plot to put it on. But the direction is worth having in your head when a tube count comes back larger than you wanted.

## Exercise

Work out the per-tube surface for a tube whose outside diameter and length you choose, and check that your arithmetic reproduces one of the two figures above when you pick the right pair. Then say what would happen to a tube count if the inside diameter were used in that expression by mistake, and in which direction the error would go.
