# The limit as P tends to zero

There is one place on the correction surface where the answer is known before any code runs. As P goes to zero the factor goes to one, at every R. A vanishing cold rise is a vanishing departure from counter-current flow, so nothing is left for a correction to correct. That needs no publication behind it, and a table that fails to show it is a table with an error in it.

## The limit, read downward

| P | F at R 0.400000 | F at R 0.900000 | F at R 1.000000 | F at R 1.700000 | F at R 3.000000 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0.100000 | 0.999228 | 0.998166 | 0.997939 | 0.996196 | 0.992029 |
| 0.010000 | 0.999993 | 0.999985 | 0.999983 | 0.999971 | 0.999948 |
| 0.001000 | 1.000000 | 1.000000 | 1.000000 | 1.000000 | 0.999999 |
| 0.000100 | 1.000000 | 1.000000 | 1.000000 | 1.000000 | 1.000000 |

Read each column downward rather than each row across. Every one of the five climbs toward one, and by the bottom row all five have reached it to the six decimals this course prints at. The R values spread across the useful range, and the limit holds in every one of them. Read the top row across as well: the column at the lowest R sits closer to one there than the column at the highest R does, at 0.999228 against 0.992029.

## Why a limit outranks a point

An analytic limit is a known truth. Nobody has to have measured an exchanger for it to be right, and nobody can have measured one badly and had that error propagate into it. So a limit of this kind does two things a transcribed published case cannot. It can be checked without a citation, and it discriminates: a wrong constant anywhere in the closed form shows up as a column that fails to climb, or climbs to something other than one.

That is worth stating plainly, because it is the argument that runs through this whole tier. Where a module has published data, a published case is the evidence. Where it has none, an analytic limit is the evidence that remains, and it is not a weaker kind.

There is a second use for it, closer to the desk. A low P is the common case on a real exchanger, because most units are asked for a modest cold rise across a wide span between the inlets. So the flat part of this surface is where a good deal of ordinary work sits, and a factor of one is very nearly right across that whole class of duty. Where it stops being nearly right is the sweep in the lesson before this one.

## The column with its own branch

The R equal to one column deserves a second look. A ratio of exactly one makes the general closed form indeterminate, so the engine takes a separate branch for it, written for that case alone. The middle column above is that branch answering, and it climbs to one exactly as the four general columns do.

Two branches of arithmetic agreeing at a limit they both have to satisfy is a real check on both. If the special branch carried the wrong sign or the wrong grouping, its column would part company with its neighbours on the way down, and the table above is where that would be visible.
