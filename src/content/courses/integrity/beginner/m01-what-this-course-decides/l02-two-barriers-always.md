# Two barriers, always

One barrier holding is not the same as a well that is safe, and this lesson is about the difference.

{{panel:wi-envelope-explorer}}

## Why one is never enough

A single barrier is a single point of failure. When it goes, the fluid is out. There is no interval in which anyone notices, decides and intervenes, because the failure and the release are the same event.

Two barriers change the shape of the problem. The first one failing becomes an event you detect and respond to rather than an event you report afterwards. The well is still contained while you fix it. That is the entire argument, and it is why the requirement is written as two independent envelopes rather than as one very good one.

The engine takes this seriously enough to make it a hard check rather than a warning. A well with flow potential and only one envelope fails the two-envelopes check outright.

## What the rule table does with the second envelope

Run every combination of primary and secondary status through the categorisation and the pattern is stark. Across the sixteen combinations for a well with flow potential, the engine returns green exactly once, yellow three times, orange seven times and red five times.

Green requires both envelopes intact. Nothing less earns it.

Read two of the orange cases carefully, because they are the cases people argue about.

| Primary | Secondary | Category |
|---|---|---|
| intact | failed | orange |
| intact | empty | orange |

In the first row a barrier has failed and the engine does not call it red, because the other envelope is whole. In the second row nothing has failed at all, and the engine still refuses to call it better than orange, because a well that requires two envelopes has one. A missing barrier and a broken barrier land in the same place. That is the rule doing what it was written to do.

## What independence is supposed to mean

Two envelopes are independent when nothing that defeats one can defeat the other. Different steel, different seals, different cement, different test histories, different failure mechanisms.

Independence is an engineering claim about the design, not a property the engine can measure. What the engine can see is the one violation that shows up in the data: an element listed as serving both envelopes at once. It flags every such element by name. That case is important enough to have its own lesson at the end of module 2.
