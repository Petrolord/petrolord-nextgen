# Reading a barrier table

A practical order of attack, so that the answer falls out instead of being hunted for.

{{panel:wi-envelope-explorer}}

## The order

**One. Count seats against elements.** Add the two envelope counts and compare to the roster length. The gap is the shared count. Do this first, because it tells you whether you are looking at two envelopes or one wearing a costume.

**Two. Scan for failed.** One failed element in an envelope makes that envelope failed and nothing else in that envelope can change it. If you find one, you already know that envelope's verdict.

**Three. Scan for degraded and not-verified.** Treat them as the same finding at this stage, because they produce the same verdict. Note which is which for the follow up work, not for the verdict.

**Four. Roll each envelope up.** Worst status present. Failed beats degraded, degraded beats intact, and an envelope with nothing in it is empty.

**Five. Put the two envelope statuses into the category.** Two words in, one traffic light out. Nothing about individual elements enters at this step, which is why steps two to four have to be finished first.

## Worked on the published roster

Seats: 5 plus 5 is 10. Roster: 10 elements. Gap zero, nothing shared.

Failed: none.

Degraded or not-verified: one, the DHSV, in the primary envelope.

Rollup: primary **degraded**, secondary **intact**.

Category: **yellow**, `Barrier degradation (or unverified elements); no failure.`

Five steps, one pass down the table, no backtracking.

## The checks are not the verdict

Alongside the category the engine returns 4 named checks: two envelopes present, no common element, every element verified, no failed element. On this roster all 4 pass and the well is still yellow.

Read them as the work list, not the score. They tell you what to go and do. The category tells you what the well is.

## The habit worth building

Write the verdict down before you look at the engine's answer. Five steps is little enough to hold in your head, and committing to an answer is what turns the rule from something read into something known.

## Exercise
