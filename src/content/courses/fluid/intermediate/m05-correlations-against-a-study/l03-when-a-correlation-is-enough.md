# When a correlation is enough

Most of the time, and it is worth being clear about why, because the alternative is expensive.

## The cases where it is enough

**Screening and ranking.** Comparing two prospects, or two development options on the same fluid. Systematic errors cancel in a comparison and the ranking survives.

**Early appraisal.** Before a sample exists there is nothing else. A correlated description with its provenance stated is the correct thing to use, and it should be replaced when data arrives.

**Interpolating a measured fluid.** A study measured Bo at twelve pressures and you need it at a thirteenth. A correlation anchored to the measurements is a perfectly good interpolator.

**Properties the answer does not turn on.** Water viscosity in an oil study where water is 5 percent of the flow. Get it roughly right and move on.

**Sanity checking a measurement.** A laboratory number far from every correlation is worth a second look before it is used.

## The case where it is enough and people assume otherwise

A fluid that sits squarely inside a correlation's population, on a property that correlates well.

Volume properties are like this. Bo from Standing on an ordinary black oil inside its range is usually good to a few percent, which is comparable to the uncertainty in most of the other inputs to a volumetric estimate.

Running an untuned equation of state instead would not obviously improve it, as this tier has now measured: on Good Oil the untuned model is six percent out on saturation pressure and nine API out on gravity. A correlation would have done better on the gravity, because a stock tank gravity from a separator test is a measurement rather than a model output.

That is a genuinely important result and it inverts a common assumption.

## The economics

A full PVT study costs real money and takes weeks. A representative sample needs a well flowing above its bubble point, which constrains when it can be taken.

So the question is never "correlation or measurement" in the abstract. It is whether this particular answer is worth that particular cost, and the answer is often no.

What is never acceptable is spending nothing and reporting as though you had spent something.

## The rule

Use a correlation where its error is smaller than the other uncertainties in the calculation, and say you used it.

On a volumetric estimate whose porosity is known to ten percent, a three percent Bo from a correlation is not the problem. On a mobility ratio that decides a flood design, a viscosity known to a factor of two is the problem.

## The misconception to avoid

"Correlations are for when you cannot afford to do it properly." They are for when the answer they give is good enough for the decision, which is most decisions. The failure is not using a correlation; it is using one where the decision turns on the difference and not saying which one you used.
