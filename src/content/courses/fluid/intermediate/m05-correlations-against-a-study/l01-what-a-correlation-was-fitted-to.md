# What a correlation was fitted to

The Associate tier said a correlation is a fit to somebody else's samples. Here is what that means when a real study is available to check against.

## The populations

**Standing (1947).** 105 experimentally determined bubble points from 22 crude oil and natural gas mixtures. All Californian.

**Vasquez and Beggs (1980).** Over 6000 measurements from a worldwide data set, split at 30 API into two coefficient sets.

**Glaso (1980).** North Sea oils.

**Beal (1946), dead oil viscosity.** 655 measurements on 492 oils.

**Beggs and Robinson (1975), live oil viscosity.** A few hundred measurements.

**Hall-Yarborough and Dranchuk-Abou-Kassem.** Both fits to the Standing-Katz z chart, which is itself a fit to measurements on natural gases from 1942.

## What the sizes tell you

Standing's 105 points from one region is a small, coherent sample. It will do well on Californian oils and it has no way of knowing about anything else.

Vasquez-Beggs's 6000 points from everywhere is broad and heterogeneous. It will do reasonably on most things and it may do less well than a regional correlation on a fluid from that region.

Neither of those is better in general. They are different trades between breadth and specificity, and knowing which trade you are buying is the point.

## Where Good Oil sits

A 40.7 API Texas crude with 768 scf/stb at 220 F.

Standing's Californian oils covered roughly 16 to 64 API and 20 to 1425 scf/stb, so this fluid is inside the range. Being inside the range is necessary and not sufficient: a point can be inside the convex hull of a data set and still be somewhere the fit is poorly constrained.

## What having a measurement changes

Everything, because it converts a spread into an error.

Without a measurement, three correlations disagreeing tells you they disagree. With one, you can say which is closer, by how much, and in which direction.

Do that on enough fluids and you know something about your basin that no published correlation does. That is what operators with long histories in one area actually have, and it is why a company correlation fitted to local data often beats a published one.

## The honest use of a correlation

**As a first estimate**, before any laboratory work exists.

**As a cross-check** on a measurement that looks odd, since a bubble point 40 percent off every correlation is worth re-examining.

**As an interpolator** between measured points on a fluid you have characterized.

**As a substitute for measurement on a fluid that matters**, which is where it stops being honest.

## The thing to write down

Which correlation, and what it was fitted to. One line.

> Bubble point from Standing (1947), fitted to 105 measurements on Californian oils; this fluid at 40.7 API and 768 scf/stb is inside its stated range.

A reader can then decide for themselves whether they believe it, which is more than a bare number allows.

## The misconception to avoid

"A correlation fitted to thousands of measurements is more reliable than one fitted to a hundred." Only if your fluid resembles the thousands. A large heterogeneous data set produces a fit that is mediocre everywhere rather than good somewhere, and for a fluid that sits squarely in a small correlation's population, the small correlation can win comfortably.
