# Constant composition expansion

The simplest experiment in the study, and the one that gives the bubble point.

## What is done

A sample of reservoir fluid goes into a cell at reservoir temperature and at a pressure above the bubble point. Nothing is ever removed.

The pressure is lowered in steps and the total volume of the cell contents is recorded at each step. That is the whole experiment.

Because nothing leaves, the overall composition is constant throughout, which is what the name says.

## What it gives

**The bubble point.** Plot pressure against total volume. Above the bubble point the sample is a nearly incompressible liquid, so the line is steep. Below it a compressible gas phase is growing, so the line bends sharply. The break is the bubble point.

That is the primary purpose of the experiment and it is the single most important number in the report.

**Relative volume.** Total volume at each pressure divided by the volume at the bubble point. It is reported as a dimensionless column and it is how the compressibility of the undersaturated oil is obtained: above the bubble point, the slope of relative volume against pressure gives co directly.

**Liquid dropout**, for a gas condensate. The fraction of the cell volume that is liquid, which for a condensate rises to a maximum below the dew point and then falls. That curve is the whole reason a condensate study exists.

## Why it is the least ambiguous experiment

Because there is no procedure to argue about. Nothing is removed, so there is no question of what was in contact with what, and no reference volume to choose.

The bubble point from a constant composition expansion is about as close to a raw measurement as a PVT report gets. Everything downstream involves a procedural choice; this one does not.

## What it does not give

**Solution gas ratio.** Nothing was removed, so no gas was collected and no gas volume was measured. Rs does not come from this experiment.

**Formation volume factor.** Relative volume is referenced to the bubble point volume, not to a stock tank barrel. Turning it into Bo requires knowing what a stock tank barrel of this fluid is, which is the separator test.

Those two absences catch people. The experiment that gives the cleanest measurement gives neither of the two properties a reservoir engineer uses most.

## Good Oil Well No. 4

Its constant composition expansion gives a bubble point of 2634.65 psia at 220 F, and that number is the anchor for everything else in the study and for everything the Expert tier does with it.

Note the pressure base. The report quotes pressures in psig against a base of 14.65 psia, so a reported 2620 psig becomes 2634.65 psia. Getting that conversion wrong shifts every pressure in the study by about 15 psi, which is small enough to look like noise and large enough to matter when you are comparing a model against it.

## The misconception to avoid

"Relative volume is the formation volume factor." It is volume relative to the volume at the bubble point, so it equals one AT the bubble point by construction. Bo at the bubble point is around 1.4 for this fluid. They are different quantities with different references and the only thing they share is being dimensionless ratios of volumes.
