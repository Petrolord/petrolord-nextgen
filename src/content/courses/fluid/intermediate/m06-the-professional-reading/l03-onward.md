# Onward

What this tier gave you, and what the Expert tier does with it.

## What you can do now

Read a reservoir fluid study. Say which experiment produced which number, why the differential liberation's columns are not the Bo and Rs a reservoir model wants, what the separator test anchors, and which stage the report does not list.

Run an untuned compositional model against a real study, and read the gaps as the documented biases they are rather than as failures.

And say when a correlation is enough, which is more often than the machinery available might suggest.

## What this tier deliberately did not do

**It did not tune anything.** Every model number came from composition and published characterization correlations, with nothing adjusted to fit.

**It did not open the equation of state.** The saturation pressure, the flash and the separator train were used as they come. What is inside them is the next tier.

**It did not touch the quantities the engine labels screening.** Those get a module of their own, ungraded, at the Expert tier.

## What the Expert tier does

**Opens the equation of state.** Why a cubic, what Peng-Robinson 1978 does, how mixing rules and binary interaction parameters work, what fugacity equality means and why volume translation is free.

**Runs the machinery deliberately.** Stability testing, the Rachford-Rice solve, saturation pressure as a search rather than a lookup, the phase envelope.

**Tunes.** Four bounded knobs on the C7+ pseudo-component, regressed jointly against the four measurements this tier compared against. The saturation pressure closes to under a tenth of a percent and the gravity bias closes from nine API to under two.

**Reports what it cost.** Three targets improve and one gets worse. The formation volume factor, which was the best-matched target untuned, is the one the joint fit gives up.

**Grades nothing in one module.** The quantities the engine labels screening are taught, with their limits stated, and no capstone field touches them.

## The thread through the series

The decline curve course asked which points a fit was made to. The material balance course asked which implementation of an aquifer model a number came from. The SCAL course asked what an averaged curve had averaged. The simulation course asked which section a keyword lived in and which convention a volume was clipped under.

This tier asked which experiment produced a number and what it was referenced to.

Same question, five domains. It is the question that separates reading a report from quoting one.

## The one habit to carry

When a model and a measurement disagree, find out the direction and the mechanism before deciding which is wrong.

This tier's biases were both understood before they were measured: heavy oils saturate high because a lumped pseudo-component smears the light-heavy balance, and single pseudo-components come out dense because a generalized volume shift is being extrapolated. Knowing the mechanism turned two alarming numbers into two documented ones.

A disagreement with no mechanism is where the work is.

## The misconception to avoid

"The Expert tier fixes the model, so this tier's numbers are provisional." The Expert tier fits the model to four measurements, which makes it reproduce those four and says nothing about anything else. This tier's untuned comparison is the only unbiased look the model ever gets, and it stays in the report after tuning as the record of how much the tuning had to do.
