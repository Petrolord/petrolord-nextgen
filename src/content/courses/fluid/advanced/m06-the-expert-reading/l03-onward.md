# Onward

Three tiers, two fluids, and no measurement of our own.

## The three things this course taught

**Every number has a provenance, and the provenance has a tier.** Measured, armed, oracle gated, lab tuned, published method, screening. The engine states its own, which is unusual, and reading it is the skill.

**A correlation is a fit to somebody else's samples.** Standing had 105 bubble points from 22 Californian mixtures. Three correlations disagree on the same oil because they were fitted to three different populations, and the spread is a measurement of what a correlation can know.

**A model tuned to data reproduces that data by construction.** It is a calibration and not a validation, it trades its targets against each other, and the trade belongs in the report.

## The three findings on these fluids

Ekene's designed fluid sits a few percent from every correlation on its volume properties and a factor of 2.4 away on its viscosity. That asymmetry is normal: volume behaviour correlates well from gravity and temperature and viscosity does not.

An untuned equation of state on Good Oil Well No. 4 saturates 5.9 percent high, reports a stock tank oil 8.9 API light, and declines to report a formation volume factor at all, because its saturation pressure sits above the pressure the laboratory calls reservoir pressure. All three are documented and pinned rather than surprising.

Tuning four bounded knobs closes the saturation pressure to under a tenth of a percent and the gravity to under two API, and makes the formation volume factor worse, because three of the four targets divide by the same stock-tank volume.

## What comes next and is not here

**Compositional simulation.** Running a tuned equation of state inside a reservoir simulator, which is where a compositional description earns its cost.

**Splitting the plus fraction.** Multiple pseudo-components, the distributions used to generate them, and the extra freedom that brings.

**Viscosity tuning.** The engine's four knobs are aimed at phase behaviour and density; tuning critical volumes to measured viscosities is on its out-of-scope register.

**Non-hydrocarbon-rich fluids.** High CO2 or H2S content needs interaction parameters and sometimes a different equation.

**Asphaltenes and waxes.** Solid phases the cubic does not describe at all.

## The order to work in on a new fluid

**Read the report before running anything.** Which experiments, which separator conditions, what the differential column is referenced to.

**Run the correlations.** Three of them, and record the spread.

**Run the untuned model, if you have a composition.** Compare against every measurement the study reports, and write down each gap.

**Only then tune**, with the targets and weights chosen for what the model is for.

**Report the ledger**, including the target you gave up.

That order matters. Tuning first destroys the only unbiased comparison the model will ever get.

## The habit that generalises

Ask what kind of number you are holding.

The decline curve course asked which points a fit was made to. The material balance course asked which implementation of an aquifer a number came from. The SCAL course asked what an average had averaged. The simulation course asked which section a keyword lived in and which convention a volume was clipped under.

This course asked which tier a quantity sits on and what would move it up one.

A fluid description is where all of those answers end up, because everything downstream divides by Bo or multiplies by a viscosity. It is the smallest artifact in a study with the widest reach, which is the best argument for reading one carefully.

## The misconception to avoid

"Now that the model is tuned, the correlations are obsolete." The tuned model reproduces four numbers on one fluid at one temperature. Ask it about a different fluid, or a different temperature, or a viscosity, and it is back to resting on correlated pseudo-component properties. The tools do not supersede each other; the evidence behind a particular number does.
