# Gas viscosity

Two orders of magnitude below oil viscosity, which is why gas goes first.

## The size of it

Ekene's gas at 3200 psia and 180 F:

$$\mu_g = 0.022022626655780805 \text{ cp}$$

Against an oil viscosity around 0.8 cp on the correlated chain, or the designed 1.8 cp. Gas is thirty to eighty times less viscous than the oil it shares the pore space with.

That single fact drives most of what makes gas awkward in a reservoir. Mobility is permeability over viscosity, so once free gas can flow at all it flows preferentially, and a reservoir below its bubble point spends its energy producing gas.

## Lee, Gonzalez and Eakin (1966)

The engine's correlation. It needs the gas density, which needs z, which needs the reduced state, which needs gas gravity.

The form is exponential in density:

$$\mu_g = K \exp\left(X \rho^{Y}\right)$$

with K, X and Y functions of molecular weight and temperature. Gas viscosity rises with density, so it rises with pressure and falls with temperature, which is the opposite of a liquid on the temperature axis.

That reversal is worth holding onto. Heating a liquid thins it; heating a gas at fixed density thickens it, because viscosity in a gas comes from molecules crossing between layers rather than from cohesion.

## Another chain

Gas gravity gives pseudo-criticals, which give the reduced state, which gives z, which gives density, which gives viscosity. Five steps.

So gas viscosity inherits everything upstream of it. The third of a percent that separates the two z correlations flows through, and so does any error in the gas gravity that started it.

## Where the gas gravity comes from

Measured at surface, on separator gas. Which means it depends on the separator conditions, because different separator pressures release different gas.

That is the Professional tier's subject and it is worth flagging now: gas gravity is not a fixed property of the reservoir fluid. Quote a gas gravity and you should be able to say which separator it came off.

## The order of magnitude check

A natural gas at reservoir conditions is somewhere between 0.01 and 0.05 cp. At atmospheric conditions it is nearer 0.012.

Anything outside that range is a unit error or an input error. Gas viscosity is one of the few quantities in this course where the plausible range is narrow enough that the check is decisive.

## The misconception to avoid

"Gas viscosity is so small it can be neglected." It cannot, because it appears in a RATIO. Mobility ratio, fractional flow and the productivity of a gas well all divide by it, and a number in a denominator is never negligible however small it is. What can often be neglected is its VARIATION, because it changes far less across a reservoir's pressure range than oil viscosity does.
