# What the gap would cost

Three or four percent sounds like nothing. This lesson traces what it would actually do.

## The three gaps

| quantity | designed | correlated | gap |
|---|---|---|---|
| solution gas at the bubble point | 400 scf/stb | 421.94 scf/stb | +5.5 percent |
| formation volume factor | 1.2 rb/stb | 1.2407824121407645 rb/stb | +3.4 percent |
| bubble point at 400 scf/stb | 2000 psia | 1912.1923059028293 psia | -4.4 percent |

## Oil in place

Oil in place divides by Bo. Substituting the correlated 1.2408 for the designed 1.2 reduces Ekene's stock tank oil in place by about 3.3 percent.

On a booking of 12.139 million barrels that is roughly 400 thousand barrels, which is a real number: a well, or a platform slot, or the difference between two development options.

And it is worth setting against what the simulation course spent effort on. That course calibrated the structure until the deck reproduced the booked volume to within 0.056 percent. This substitution is sixty times larger. The structural work was worth doing and it was not where the uncertainty lived.

## Voidage

The waterflood ledger multiplies produced oil by Bo to get reservoir voidage. A 3.4 percent larger Bo means 3.4 percent more produced voidage at the same production.

The voidage replacement ratio is injected voidage over produced voidage, so raising the denominator lowers the ratio by about the same 3.4 percent. A field managed at a VRR of 1.03 would be reported at about 1.00, which is the difference between "we are replacing voidage" and "we are not".

The whole surveillance conclusion of that course would shift, on a fluid property nobody measured.

## The mobility ratio

This one is the largest by far, and it comes from viscosity rather than volume.

The designed oil viscosity is 1.8 cp. The correlated chain, Beal dead oil followed by Beggs-Robinson for the dissolved gas, gives 0.7559673199800581 cp at the designed 400 scf/stb.

That is not a few percent. It is less than half.

Mobility ratio scales with oil viscosity, so Ekene's designed mobility ratio of 1.2 would become roughly 0.5. A mildly unfavourable flood becomes a favourable one, the areal sweep correlation returns a different answer, the layered sweep analysis moves, and the fractional flow curves shift. An entire course's worth of conclusions changes on a PVT substitution that nobody would call major.

## The lesson in the pattern

The volumetric properties disagree by a few percent and the transport property disagrees by more than a factor of two.

That is typical. Volume behaviour is well constrained by simple measurements and correlates well across oils. Viscosity depends on molecular detail that gravity and temperature do not capture, so viscosity correlations are much weaker than volume correlations.

If you can measure one thing about a fluid, and volumes are already reasonable, measure the viscosity.

## What follows for practice

Not that correlations are useless. That a study should say which fluid description it used, and that substituting one for another is a change to the study rather than an update to an input.

## The misconception to avoid

"Small percentage errors in PVT wash out." They compound in the same direction through every calculation that uses them, because they are systematic rather than random. A Bo that is three percent high is three percent high in the oil in place, the voidage, the recovery factor's denominator and the forecast. Nothing averages it away.
