# What a bubble point is

The pressure at which the first bubble of gas appears as an oil is expanded at constant temperature. Everything about how a reservoir behaves changes when it is crossed.

## The experiment behind the definition

Put a sample of reservoir oil in a cell at reservoir temperature and at a pressure well above anything the reservoir has seen. It is a single liquid.

Now expand the cell slowly. The oil expands, slightly, because liquids are nearly incompressible. At some pressure a bubble of gas appears. That pressure is the bubble point, and it is a property of this fluid at this temperature and nothing else.

Above it the oil is **undersaturated**: it holds all the gas it contains, dissolved, and it could hold more. Below it the oil is **saturated**: it is holding all it can and any further drop in pressure releases gas.

## Why the crossing matters so much

Above the bubble point the reservoir has one mobile phase. Oil expands as pressure falls, and that expansion is the whole drive mechanism. It is a weak drive, because oil compressibility is small, so pressure falls fast for the oil produced.

Below the bubble point gas comes out of solution everywhere in the reservoir at once. Three things follow:

**The oil shrinks.** It has lost the gas that was swelling it, so Bo falls.

**The oil thickens.** Dissolved gas was thinning it, so viscosity rises, sometimes sharply.

**A second phase appears and it is far more mobile.** Once free gas exceeds its critical saturation it flows, and it flows preferentially because gas viscosity is two orders of magnitude below oil viscosity. Producing gas-oil ratios climb and the reservoir spends its energy pushing gas out.

That is why development plans are built around keeping the reservoir above its bubble point where the economics allow, and it is why the material balance course treated the crossing as a distinct regime.

## Which way it moves

Bubble point rises with more dissolved gas, with lighter oil, and with higher temperature. It falls with heavier oil and with less gas.

The direction is worth holding, because it is the sanity check on any correlation output. A bubble point that goes down when you add gas has been computed wrongly.

## What it is not

It is not a property of the reservoir. It is a property of the fluid at a temperature. The same oil at a different temperature has a different bubble point, which is why every bubble point must be quoted with its temperature.

It is also not the reservoir's initial pressure. A reservoir whose initial pressure equals its bubble point is called saturated at discovery, and it is a special case rather than the norm. Ekene starts at 3200 psia with a bubble point of 2000, so it has 1200 psia of undersaturation to spend before anything interesting happens to the fluid.

## The measurement

In a laboratory the bubble point comes out of the constant composition expansion, which the Professional tier covers. The volume of the cell is recorded as pressure falls, and the plot of pressure against volume has a distinct break at the bubble point: above it the sample is a nearly incompressible liquid and the line is steep, below it a compressible gas phase is growing and the line bends.

Reading that break is how the number in a report was produced.

## The misconception to avoid

"The bubble point is where gas starts to be produced." It is where gas starts to come OUT OF SOLUTION in the reservoir. Free gas does not flow until it exceeds its critical saturation, so a well can produce at a stable gas-oil ratio for some time after the reservoir has crossed its bubble point, and then the ratio climbs. The lag is real and it has fooled people into thinking the crossing had not happened.
